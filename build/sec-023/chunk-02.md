### Price a distillation for me. Frontier teacher into a 3–8B student — show me the whole break-even, not just the token price.

I will use a concrete case because the arithmetic is the answer. Say a document-extraction feature: 1,800 input tokens, 300 output tokens, currently served by a frontier model behind a prompt.

**💰 Math — teacher side.** At $3/Mtok input and $15/Mtok output (**📅 Volatile:** re-price the week of your loop), each call is `1800/1e6 × 3 = $0.0054` in, `300/1e6 × 15 = $0.0045` out, total `$0.0099`. Call it a cent a request. At 10M requests/month that is **$99,000/month**.

**💰 Math — student side, and this is where people wave their hands.** An 8B model in bf16 is 16 GB of weights. Decode reads all of them per forward pass, so on an H100 with ~3.35 TB/s of HBM bandwidth the ceiling is `3350/16 ≈ 209` tokens/s at batch 1, and batching amortizes that weight read almost linearly until you become KV-bandwidth- or compute-bound. Assume a realistic sustained 4,000 output tok/s aggregate at server-side batching. Decode for the month: `10e6 × 300 / 4000 = 750,000 s = 208 GPU-hours`. Prefill is compute-bound: `2 × 8e9 × 1800 = 2.88e13` FLOPs per request; at 40% MFU on ~990 TFLOP/s dense bf16 you get ~396 TFLOP/s, so `2.88e13 / 3.96e14 = 0.073 s`, and `10e6 × 0.073 = 730,000 s = 202 GPU-hours`. Total **~410 GPU-hours/month of actual compute.**

But you cannot buy 410 hours of an H100 spread evenly. You provision for peak and redundancy — say 2 H100s running continuously, which is 1,460 GPU-hours of capacity at ~28% utilization. At $2.50/H100-hour that is **$3,650/month**. So the ratio is `99,000 / 3,650 ≈ 27×`. The "20× cheaper" claim is the right order of magnitude, and now you know exactly which assumptions produce it.

**💰 Math — build cost, which the token comparison always omits.** Generating 60k distillation examples with rejection sampling at n=4: `60,000 × 4 × $0.0099 ≈ $2,400`. Human verification of a 2,000-example sample at $2/label: `$4,000`. Building a real 500-example eval set with expert labels at $8: `$4,000`. Training compute is genuinely noise — LoRA on 8B over 60k examples × 800 tokens × 3 epochs ≈ 144M tokens, `6 × 8e9 × 1.44e8 ≈ 6.9e18` FLOPs, about 5 GPU-hours or $12 per run; ten experiments is $120. The real cost is a senior engineer for six weeks building the pipeline, the eval, the training loop, the serving path and the rollout — at a fully-loaded ~$6,700/week that is **~$40,000**. Total build ≈ **$50,000**, plus roughly a week per quarter of maintenance (~$6,700/quarter) forever.

**The decision rule that falls out.** Monthly saving is `99,000 − 3,650 = $95,350`, so payback is `50,000 / 95,350 = 0.52 months`. Obvious yes. Now run it at 1M requests/month: teacher spend is $9,900, but you still need at least one GPU provisioned for latency reasons, so student cost is ~$1,825 and the saving is $8,075/month — payback `50,000 / 8,075 = 6.2 months`, before you count the treadmill. That is marginal at best.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "My threshold is roughly $10k/month of teacher spend on a *single* task before distillation pays for itself, because the build is about $50k of engineering and you want payback inside two quarters. Below that I'd do prompt compression, prefix caching and a cheaper tier of the same family, and revisit when volume grows."

### You quoted 27×. What has to be true for that number to survive contact with production?

Utilization, and it is the single most common way a distillation project fails its own business case.

The teacher's cost is purely marginal — you pay per token, and if traffic drops to zero at 3am you pay zero. The student's cost is a *reservation*. In my arithmetic above the GPUs sat at 28% utilization and the comparison still won because the per-token gap was so large. But run the same model on a workload with a 20:1 peak-to-trough ratio and no autoscaling, and you are paying for peak capacity 24 hours a day against a teacher bill that follows the diurnal curve. A distillation that shows 27× on paper can show 4× on the actual invoice.

So the things I check before I sign off on the number. **Is the traffic shape amenable?** Batch or near-batch workloads (nightly document processing, backfills) are the ideal case because you can run the GPU flat out; interactive traffic with a sharp diurnal curve is the hard case. **Can I autoscale on the right signal?** Scaling GPU replicas on CPU utilization is meaningless; you scale on queue depth or on KV-cache utilization, and cold-start for a 16 GB model plus engine warmup is 60–180 seconds, which means you must scale on a *leading* indicator with that much lead time. **Can I co-tenant?** If I have five distilled students I should be serving them as LoRA adapters over one base model rather than five deployments — that turns five 28%-utilized fleets into one 90%-utilized fleet and is usually a bigger win than the distillation itself. **What is the p99 under batching?** Continuous batching gives you throughput by making individual requests wait; if your SLO is a 2s p95 you cannot batch as aggressively as the throughput math assumed, and the effective tok/s drops.

**⚠ Trap:** comparing your student's cost at 100% utilization against the teacher's per-token price. That is not a comparison, it is a fantasy. The honest form is *cost per resolved request at your actual traffic shape*, including idle GPU time, and I would rather quote 8× and be right than 27× and get audited.

**🔍 Failure taxonomy — when the savings evaporate.** Escalation rate higher than modelled (student defers to teacher on 25% of traffic instead of 5% → you now pay both bills). Retry amplification (student fails schema validation and you retry 3×, tripling its cost and often falling back to the teacher anyway). Fleet fragmentation (one deployment per tenant). Eval drift forcing monthly retrains instead of quarterly (the $6,700/quarter maintenance becomes $6,700/month). Quantization regressions discovered post-launch forcing you back to bf16 and doubling your GPU count.

### Design the whole distillation pipeline for me. Assume a Notion-style "summarize and tag this document" feature.

I would build it in six stages, and I would build the eval before anything else.

**Stage 0 — the eval, first.** 300–500 real documents sampled stratified from production (by length, by workspace type, by language), each with a human-written reference summary and a tag set, plus a rubric. I need three metrics: a task metric (tag F1 against the human set — cheap, deterministic, no judge), a quality metric (pairwise judge comparing student vs teacher output, reported as win/tie/loss with confidence intervals), and a guardrail metric (does it ever emit content not grounded in the document — I score that with a separate entailment check). If I cannot build this, I stop; a distillation without an eval is an unfalsifiable claim.

**Stage 1 — prompt sampling.** Pull 100k documents from logs, stratified as above, oversampling the rare intents to ~3× their natural rate. Embed, cluster to ~1,000 clusters, cap per cluster, land on ~40k prompts. This is where the generalization comes from.

**Stage 2 — teacher generation with rejection.** Run the current production prompt against the teacher at `n=2` with temperature 0.7. Filter: JSON schema valid, tag vocabulary in-vocabulary, summary length in range, and an entailment check that every claim in the summary appears in the source. Expect to keep 70–85%. Keep both samples when both pass — a small amount of one-to-many supervision helps the student not collapse onto a single phrasing.

**Stage 3 — human audit.** Sample 500 of the kept pairs and have a human grade them. This is non-optional and it is cheap ($1,000). Its purpose is not to improve the data; it is to measure the *teacher's* error rate, because that number is your student's floor and you need it in the design doc. If the teacher is at 91% and your product bar is 95%, the project is dead and you have found out for $1,000 instead of $50,000.

**Stage 4 — train.** LoRA rank 16–32 on all linear layers of an 8B base, 2–3 epochs, LR 1e-4 with cosine decay, loss masked to completion tokens only. Mix in 10% general instruction data as replay so the student does not forget how to follow a rephrased instruction. Run the data-scaling curve (5k/10k/20k/40k) in parallel because it costs four cheap runs and it tells you whether to buy more data.

**Stage 5 — serve behind a router with an escalation predicate.** This is the part juniors omit. The student handles the modal traffic; a predicate escalates to the teacher. My predicate is a disjunction: schema validation failed, document length above the 95th percentile of the training distribution, language not in the training mix, workspace flagged enterprise-tier, or the student's own mean token logprob below a threshold calibrated on the eval set. I log the escalation rate as a first-class SLO with an alert, because escalation rate rising is the earliest signal of distribution drift — earlier than any quality metric.

**Stage 6 — shadow, then canary.** Run the student in shadow against 100% of traffic for a week, comparing against the live teacher output with the judge, and only then canary at 5% with an automatic rollback on the guardrail metric. Rolling back a model is a config flip if you built it as a router and a two-day incident if you did not.

**⚠ Trap:** freezing the teacher prompt and forgetting it. Your production prompt evolves — someone adds a rule about acronyms in week three. Now your distilled student encodes the week-one prompt and drifts silently away from stated product behaviour, with no diff anywhere in your repo showing it. I pin the exact prompt string, its hash, and the teacher model version into the model card, and I fail CI if the live prompt hash differs from the one the deployed student was distilled from.

### Explain context distillation. What am I actually trading when I bake a system prompt into weights?

Context distillation is the special case where the thing you are compressing is not a bigger model but a *longer prompt*. The teacher is the same model with a huge context — a 10k-token system prompt full of policies, few-shot examples, style rules, tool descriptions. The student is the same model with an empty or tiny system prompt. You generate `(user_input, output)` pairs by running the long-context teacher, then fine-tune the model on `(user_input → output)` with the context removed. The behaviour induced by the prompt migrates into the weights.

The mental model is closure conversion. A prompt is a set of free variables the model has to re-read on every call; context distillation binds them into the function. You stop paying to re-transmit and re-attend over a constant.

**📄 Paper:** Snell et al. (2022), *Learning by Distilling Context* — established the recipe of training a model on its own outputs produced under a scaffold (instructions, chain of thought, retrieved context) with the scaffold removed at training time, so the behaviour internalizes.

What you trade, precisely, and this is the part interviewers want:

**Editability.** A prompt is a config change; a distilled behaviour is a training run. When Legal asks you to change one refusal rule on Thursday, the prompted version ships Thursday and the distilled version ships in two weeks after a retrain, a re-eval and a canary. For any policy surface that changes more than quarterly, this alone disqualifies context distillation.

**Auditability.** With a prompt you can point at the exact sentence that produced a behaviour. With weights you can only point at a dataset. In regulated settings (Harvey-style legal, anything touching finance) that traceability is a compliance requirement, not a nicety.

**Compositionality.** Prompts compose — per-tenant instructions layered on a global system prompt. Distilled behaviour does not compose; you get one baked policy, and per-tenant variation means per-tenant adapters, which is a serving problem.

**Coverage.** The distilled behaviour only holds on the input distribution you sampled. The prompt's rules apply to *all* inputs, including ones you never imagined; the distilled version applies them where it saw them and generalizes unreliably elsewhere. This is the failure that shows up in month three: a novel input class, and the rule that was explicit in the prompt is simply absent from the model's behaviour.

What you gain is real, though: latency and cost, and one more thing people forget — you free up context window. If your system prompt is 10k tokens and your real constraint is fitting retrieved documents in, distilling the prompt buys you 10k tokens of retrieval headroom, which can be worth more than the money.

### So when do I use context distillation versus just turning on prefix caching?

Prefix caching first, essentially always. It is a config flag, it is reversible, it keeps all the properties I just listed, and it captures most of the cost.

**💰 Math.** An 8k-token system prompt at $3/Mtok is `8000/1e6 × 3 = $0.024` per call uncached. With a 90% cached-input discount it is `$0.0024`. At 200k calls/day that is `$4,800/day → $480/day`, a saving of `$4,320/day = ~$130,000/month`, for the cost of adding a cache breakpoint and making sure your prompt is byte-stable at the prefix. Context distillation, by comparison, requires a training run, an eval, and permanently gives up editability — to capture the *remaining* $480/day. That is a terrible trade at that volume, and stating it with the arithmetic is how you demonstrate you would not over-engineer.

**📅 Volatile:** cached-input discounts, cache TTLs and minimum cacheable prefix lengths differ by provider and change often. Verify the discount, the TTL and whether writes to the cache carry a surcharge before you build the model.

Prefix caching's limits are where context distillation earns its place. Caching does nothing for *decode* cost, so if your system prompt is long but your outputs are long too, the cache only touches part of the bill. Caching does nothing for the attention cost during decode over that prefix — every generated token still attends over the cached KV, so a 100k-token prefix still slows decode even at a 100% cache hit rate. Caching evicts, so a low-traffic tenant may never hit a warm cache. And if you are self-hosting, the cache is a memory budget you are spending that could hold more concurrent sequences.

**The rule I enforce in review:** context distillation is justified when (a) prefix caching is already on and the residual cost is still material, (b) the prompt is stable — it has not changed in a quarter and no one has a plan to change it, (c) you are serving your own weights so you can actually deploy the result, and (d) you need the context-window headroom or the decode-side attention savings, not just the prefill dollars. Fewer than one in five teams that propose context distillation to me actually satisfy all four.

### Draft-model distillation for speculative decoding — why does distillation specifically help, and how much?

Speculative decoding's entire economics live in one number: the acceptance rate. The draft model proposes `γ` tokens cheaply; the target model verifies all of them in a single forward pass; you keep the longest accepted prefix. The target's output distribution is provably preserved — this is not an approximation, it is exact sampling from the target — so the only thing you can tune is how often the draft guesses right.

And that is a *distribution-matching* problem, which is exactly what distillation solves. An off-the-shelf small model from the same family has never been trained to imitate the target; it was trained on web text. Distilling it on the target's own outputs — ideally on-policy, over your production prompt distribution — aligns the draft's next-token distribution with the target's, and acceptance rate is precisely the probability that those distributions agree at each step.

**📐 Numbers you must know:** expected accepted tokens per target forward pass, for acceptance rate `α` and draft length `γ`, is `(1 − α^(γ+1)) / (1 − α)`. At `α = 0.70, γ = 4`: `(1 − 0.7⁵)/0.3 = (1 − 0.16807)/0.3 = 2.77` tokens. At `α = 0.85, γ = 4`: `(1 − 0.85⁵)/0.15 = (1 − 0.4437)/0.15 = 3.71` tokens. Now net out the draft's own cost: a draft at ~1/10 the target's size run 4 times costs about 0.4 target-forwards, so the wall-clock speedup is `2.77/1.4 = 1.98×` versus `3.71/1.4 = 2.65×`. **Moving acceptance from 0.70 to 0.85 is a 34% throughput gain, and that is entirely what draft distillation buys you.**

**💰 Math:** on the 10M-request/month workload from earlier, decode was 208 GPU-hours/month. A 2.65× versus 1.98× speedup is `208/2.65 = 78.5` versus `208/1.98 = 105` GPU-hours — 27 hours saved, ~$67/month, which is nothing. But on a latency-sensitive interactive product the same delta is the difference between 45 ms and 33 ms per token at the tail, and inter-token latency is what makes a coding assistant feel alive or dead. This is a case where I argue on latency, not cost, and I would say so explicitly rather than inventing a cost story.

**⚠ Trap:** distilling the draft on generic instruction data and expecting it to transfer. Acceptance rate is *distribution-specific*. A draft distilled on chat data will have a high acceptance rate on chat and a poor one on your code-completion traffic, because the target's distribution over code tokens is a different object. I measure acceptance rate per traffic segment in production and treat a segment-level drop as a distillation-data bug, not a serving bug. The second trap: raising `γ` indefinitely. Each additional draft token costs you unconditionally but pays off only if all previous ones were accepted, so the optimal `γ` is a function of `α` — at `α=0.6`, `γ=6` is worse than `γ=3`. Sweep it.

### What are the legal constraints on distilling a closed model? Be specific about what would stop you shipping.

This is the constraint that kills more distillation projects than any technical issue, and I want an AI Engineer to raise it in the design doc rather than in the post-mortem.

The general shape, which has been stable across major providers even as the wording changes: commercial terms for hosted frontier models restrict using the outputs to develop or train models that *compete* with the provider's own services. Some providers word it as competing models, some as any model training, some carve out fine-tuning your own non-competing application. **📅 Volatile:** the exact clause text, and whether it covers "improve" versus "develop," differs by provider and has been revised repeatedly. Never assert a specific clause from memory in an interview; assert the *shape* and say you would have counsel read the current terms.

What I actually do as an engineer, which is the answer that shows seniority:

**Pick a teacher whose license permits it, and make that a first-class selection criterion.** Several strong open-weight models are released under permissive licenses precisely so their outputs can be used freely — DeepSeek-R1 shipped under MIT with distillation explicitly encouraged, and Apache-2.0 releases in the Qwen and Mistral families carry no output restriction. If my student is going to be a product asset, I would rather take a two-point quality hit on the teacher and own the output cleanly than build on a teacher whose terms could be reinterpreted.

**Know the family-specific traps.** Llama's community license has carried both a monthly-active-user threshold (700M MAU) above which you need a separate license, and naming/attribution requirements on derivative models — the derivative's name must begin with "Llama" and you must include the attribution notice. Gemma ships under its own terms with a prohibited-use policy that follows the outputs. **📅 Volatile:** these clauses have been revised across versions; read the license file in the repo you actually downloaded, not a blog summary.

**Build provenance into the pipeline as data, not as a memo.** Every row in my distillation dataset carries `teacher_model_id`, `teacher_version`, `license_id`, `generated_at`, and `prompt_hash`. That makes "which rows came from a teacher we can no longer use" a SQL query rather than an archaeology project. I have watched a team have to throw away nine months of synthetic data because nobody recorded which model produced which row.

**Distinguish the three cases legally, because they are not the same.** (1) Using a closed model's outputs to *evaluate* your system — generally fine. (2) Using them to train a model for internal, non-competing use — usually the grey zone, needs counsel. (3) Using them to train a model you sell as a general-purpose LLM — that is the case the clauses are aimed at.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Before I scope a distillation I ask two questions: what is the teacher's license, and is our student a competing product or an application feature? If the teacher is a hosted frontier model and the student is a general-purpose model we'd sell, I don't scope it at all — I pick a permissively-licensed open-weight teacher and accept the quality delta, because a legal blocker discovered at launch is a total loss and a two-point eval delta is a tuning problem."

### My student matches the teacher at 96% on our eval set but users say it's noticeably worse. Debug this.

I have seen this exact gap four or five times and it is almost always one of five things. I would work them in this order because that is roughly their prior probability.

**First: the eval is drawn from the training distribution.** If your eval prompts and your distillation prompts came from the same log dump, the student is being scored on what it memorized. The tell is the shape of the error — check per-cluster eval scores using the same clustering you built for the training data. If the student is at 99% on clusters that are dense in training data and 78% on sparse ones, you have a coverage problem, not a training problem, and the aggregate number was hiding it. Fix: hold out entire *clusters*, and hold out by time — train on last quarter, evaluate on this week.

**Second: your eval measures the wrong axis.** Tag-F1 and schema validity are easy to move and easy to match. Users complain about tone, verbosity, hedging, and the model's willingness to say "the document doesn't say." Distillation on greedy or low-temperature teacher outputs systematically flattens those. Run a blind pairwise judge on 200 real production inputs, student vs teacher, and look at the *loss* cases specifically — not the aggregate win rate. Nine times out of ten the losses cluster into two or three named behaviours you can then go fix with targeted data.

**Third: the long tail was never in the data.** The student's aggregate is fine because the tail is 3% of traffic, but the tail is 100% of the complaints because those are the users who notice. Segment production traffic by input length, by language, by tenant, and compute the metric per segment. The fix is not more data overall; it is oversampling that segment plus an escalation predicate that routes it to the teacher.

**Fourth: multi-turn or stateful drift.** If the feature has any conversational or agentic component, your eval is probably single-turn and your users are on turn six. Offline sequence-level distillation trains only on teacher-generated prefixes, so the student has never seen its own turn-three output as context. Reproduce by evaluating on full logged conversations replayed with the *student's* own prior turns substituted in, not the teacher's. This one is diagnostic: if single-turn is 96% and replayed multi-turn is 71%, you need on-policy data.

**Fifth: the serving path differs from the eval path.** Different sampling temperature, different chat template, a stop sequence that truncates, a quantization applied at deploy but not at eval, a system prompt the eval harness omits. I check this first in practice because it is free to check — dump the exact tokenized input the server sends and diff it against what the eval harness sends. A single missing BOS token or a `\n` difference in the template can cost several points and is invisible in every dashboard.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "The first thing I do is not look at the model — I diff the eval harness's tokenized input against the production server's tokenized input, byte for byte. Template and sampling mismatches are the cheapest cause to rule out and in my experience they're the cause about a third of the time."

### Distilling a reasoning model — what changes when the teacher's outputs are 4,000-token chains of thought?

Almost everything about the economics, and one thing about the method.

The method change first, because it is the interesting one: with a reasoning teacher you are no longer distilling an *answer*, you are distilling a *procedure*. The training example is `(problem → full reasoning trace → answer)`, and the student learns to generate the trace at inference. That works remarkably well — the widely-replicated result is that small dense models fine-tuned on long chain-of-thought traces from a strong reasoning teacher pick up a large fraction of the teacher's reasoning gains, far more cheaply than running RL on the small model directly. DeepSeek shipped exactly this as a set of distilled checkpoints alongside R1, under MIT, and reported that distillation into small models outperformed running RLVR on those same small models — which is a genuinely important operational result: if you have a strong reasoning teacher, *distill, do not RL*, unless you are the one training the teacher.

**📄 Paper:** DeepSeek-AI (2025), *DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning* — pure-RL reasoning emergence plus the distillation result that traces from a large reasoning model transfer to small dense students better than direct RL on those students, released under a license that explicitly permits distillation.

Now the economics, which is what an interviewer will push on.

**💰 Math — generation cost explodes.** A non-reasoning teacher answering a math problem emits maybe 400 tokens. A reasoning teacher emits 400 tokens of answer plus 4,000 tokens of trace. If reasoning output is priced at the same $15/Mtok, one example goes from `400/1e6 × 15 = $0.006` to `4,400/1e6 × 15 = $0.066` — **11× per example**. Add rejection sampling at n=4 with a 60% pass rate and each *kept* example is `4 × 0.066 / 1.0 ≈ $0.26`. Fifty thousand kept examples is `$13,200` versus `$1,200` for the non-reasoning equivalent. That is a real budget line and it is the number to have ready.

**💰 Math — serving cost inherits the trace.** This is the part people miss. If the student reproduces a 4,000-token trace per request, your student is emitting 11× the tokens of a non-reasoning student, and decode is the memory-bandwidth-bound part. On the earlier arithmetic — 4,000 output tok/s aggregate on one H100 — a 300-token response costs 0.075 GPU-seconds and a 4,400-token response costs 1.1 GPU-seconds. **You just made your "cheap" student 15× more expensive to serve than the number in your business case.** A distilled reasoning student can easily cost more per request than the frontier non-reasoning model it replaced. I have seen this exact mistake in a design review.

So the questions I ask before distilling reasoning traces: does the task actually need reasoning, measured — run the teacher with and without extended thinking on your eval and look at the delta, because on extraction, classification and rewriting it is frequently under a point. If it does, can I compress the trace — train on a *summarized* or pruned trace rather than the raw one, which loses some accuracy and cuts decode cost several-fold. Can I make trace length a controllable parameter, so cheap requests get short reasoning and hard ones get long. And is the latency acceptable at all, since 4,000 reasoning tokens at 40 ms/token is 160 seconds of thinking before the first useful word appears in a product where users expect two.

**⚠ Trap:** filtering reasoning-trace training data on final-answer correctness alone. A trace containing an arithmetic error that cancels out, or an unjustified leap, still lands on the right answer maybe 10–20% of the time on hard problems — and you are now training the student on invalid reasoning with a correct label, which is precisely the recipe for confident wrong answers on the problems that differ slightly. Where I can afford it I run a second model over the trace to check step validity, and I always oversample problems where multiple independent traces agree, because trace-level consensus is a much stronger correctness signal than a single correct answer.

### How do you pick the student — family, size, and what's the tokenizer gotcha?

Three decisions, in this order.

**Family: same family as the teacher if you can, permissive license always.** Same-family distillation is strictly easier because the tokenizers match, which keeps logit-level distillation available and keeps the "same tokens mean the same things" property that makes sequence-level distillation efficient. If the teacher is closed, family-matching is impossible anyway and you choose the student purely on license, serving-stack support, and base quality on your task.

**Size: run the ladder, do not guess.** I fine-tune the same data at three sizes — roughly 1–2B, 3–4B, 7–8B — and plot eval score against serving cost. The curve is almost always concave and the knee is informative: on narrow extraction and classification tasks the 1–2B often lands within a point of the 8B, which is a 4× serving win nobody would have predicted; on anything requiring multi-step reasoning or long-context synthesis, the drop below ~7B is a cliff, not a slope. Three LoRA runs is a day and it replaces an argument with a chart.

**Tokenizer: this is the gotcha and it bites in two distinct ways.** The first is the one I mentioned — cross-tokenizer logit distillation is undefined, so a Qwen teacher and a Llama student rules out KL-matching entirely. The second is subtler and costs money: *tokenizer efficiency differs across families on your actual data.* A tokenizer trained with heavy code and multilingual representation will encode your JSON or your Hindi text in meaningfully fewer tokens than one that was not. If student A needs 1.25× as many tokens as student B for the same content, then student A is 25% more expensive to serve *and* 25% slower per response *and* effectively has a 20% smaller context window, none of which shows up in a parameter count. Measure it: take 10,000 real production documents, tokenize with each candidate, and report the mean token count. I have seen a 15–30% spread across same-size models on domain-specific text, which completely reorders the cost ranking.

**⚠ Trap:** picking a base model instead of an instruct model, or vice versa, without thinking. If you are distilling a narrow behaviour with plenty of data, a *base* model is often the better student — you are not fighting an existing instruction-following prior, and you avoid inheriting the instruct model's refusal quirks and verbosity. If you have little data, start from the instruct model because you are leaning on its priors. The failure I have seen: distilling onto an instruct model with 3k examples and then being confused that the student still emits chirpy preambles the teacher never produced. Those came from the base checkpoint's own post-training, not from your data.

### Do I distill then quantize, or quantize then distill? And do they compound?

Distill first, quantize second, and evaluate after each — but the ordering question hides the more important one, which is whether the two error sources compound and by how much.

Mechanically: distillation changes what the weights encode; quantization changes how precisely they are stored. If you quantize the base model first and then fine-tune on top of quantized weights (the QLoRA pattern), your training is happening in a landscape that already has quantization error in it, and the adapter partly *learns around* that error — which is good, and is exactly why quantization-aware fine-tuning outperforms post-hoc quantization of a fine-tuned model. But it constrains you: the adapter is now coupled to that specific quantized base, and merging it back into full-precision weights loses part of what it learned.

The practical recipe I use: train the LoRA in bf16 against a bf16 base if the base fits (an 8B does, comfortably), evaluate the merged bf16 student to get a clean quality number, then quantize the merged model with a modern weight-only method using calibration data *drawn from your task distribution*, and evaluate again. Two numbers, two attributable deltas. If instead you do everything at once you get one number and no idea which step cost you the point.

The compounding question: they are not independent. Distillation to a small student already removes capacity headroom; quantizing after that removes more. Empirically the quantization delta on a *narrowly distilled* student is often smaller than on a general model — because the student only needs to represent one task's function and has slack — but the delta on the *tail* of that task is larger. So the aggregate number looks fine and the hard cases degrade. The measurement that catches it is per-segment eval after quantization, not aggregate.

**⚠ Trap:** calibrating your quantization on a generic corpus like WikiText and deploying on a JSON-extraction workload. Weight-only methods that use calibration activations are sensitive to the calibration distribution; calibrating off-distribution reliably costs you more than the quantization method's headline number suggests. Use 256–1,024 real production prompts.

**💰 Math:** on the 8B student, bf16 weights are 16 GB, 4-bit weight-only is ~4.5 GB with overheads. Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, so on the same H100 the ceiling goes from `3350/16 = 209` tok/s to `3350/4.5 = 744` tok/s at batch 1 — about 3.5×. At batch, the win is smaller because KV traffic and compute start to dominate, typically 1.5–2×. Either way it is the single largest serving lever after distillation itself, which is why I always sequence them together and budget eval time for both.

**🏋 Drill:** 60 minutes, unaided. Given an 8B student and a 500-example task eval, produce a two-row table: bf16 and 4-bit, each with aggregate score, score on the hardest 20% segment, tokens/s at batch 1 and batch 32, and peak GPU memory. Pass criterion: you can state the quality-per-dollar winner and defend it against the objection "but the aggregate score only dropped 0.4 points."
