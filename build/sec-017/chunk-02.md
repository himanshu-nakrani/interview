### What is an attribution graph, and what does it give you that patching doesn't?

An attribution graph is the attempt to produce, for a single prompt, a **directed computation graph over interpretable features** rather than over raw components — nodes are "the feature for capital cities," "the feature for Texas," "the say-Austin output feature," and edges are weighted causal contributions. Patching answers "does this component matter?" one component at a time; an attribution graph tries to answer "what was the whole program, end to end?"

The construction, at the level I would explain it: replace the model's MLPs with cross-layer transcoders so that every MLP's contribution is expressed as a sparse sum of interpretable feature activations writing into downstream layers. Freeze attention patterns and normalization denominators so the remaining computation is linear in the features. Now the influence of any feature on any downstream feature or on the logits is a linear quantity you can compute directly, giving a dense graph. Prune it to the paths that carry most of the effect, group nodes into supernodes for legibility, and then — critically — **validate the resulting story with interventions**, suppressing a node and checking the predicted downstream features and outputs actually move.

**📄 Paper:** Anthropic's 2025 circuit-tracing work (the "Circuit Tracing" methods paper and the companion "On the Biology of a Large Language Model") introduced this at production-model scale, showing multi-step reasoning chains — e.g. a "Dallas" feature activating a "Texas" feature which activates a "capital of Texas → Austin" pathway — and demonstrating that you could intervene mid-chain to swap Texas for California and get Sacramento.

What it buys you over patching: *composition*. Patching gives you a list of important sites; a graph gives you a mechanism with intermediate variables you can name and manipulate. The famous applied findings from that work — that a model plans a rhyme's target word before writing the line, and that when asked to show its work on an arithmetic problem it sometimes computes the answer by one route and generates a *different*, plausible-sounding justification — are only visible with composition.

**⚠ Trap:** an attribution graph explains the **replacement model**, not the original. You substituted transcoders for MLPs and froze attention; the gap between the two shows up as "error nodes," and the honest reporting requirement is what fraction of the behavior the graph accounts for. A graph that explains 60% of the effect is a hypothesis about the other 40%, and a written-up circuit that omits the error-node share is over-claiming. Also: it is *per-prompt*. A graph for one prompt is an anecdote until you show it generalizes across a prompt family.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Attribution graphs turn a list of important components into an actual causal graph over features, which is the first thing in interpretability that surfaces multi-step internal reasoning. The caveat I'd state up front is that it explains a linearized replacement model on a single prompt, so the error-node fraction and cross-prompt generalization are part of the result, not a footnote."

### I want a truthfulness probe on my model's activations. Build it, then tell me why you don't trust it.

Building it is twenty minutes of work, which is precisely why it is dangerous.

Collect a dataset of statements labelled true/false. Run each through the model, capture the residual stream at some layer `l` at the final token position, and fit a logistic regression on those `d_model`-dimensional vectors. You will get 90%+ accuracy at mid-to-late layers with almost no effort, and you will be tempted to ship it as a hallucination detector.

```python
X, y = [], []
for text, label in dataset:
    h = model(**tok(text, return_tensors="pt").to(dev),
              output_hidden_states=True).hidden_states[LAYER][0, -1]
    X.append(h.float().cpu().numpy()); y.append(label)
probe = LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000, C=0.1).fit(np.stack(X), y)
direction = probe.coef_[0] / np.linalg.norm(probe.coef_[0])   # a "truth direction"
```

Now the confounds, and I would list them unprompted because listing them is the actual signal.

**The probe learns your dataset, not truth.** If your false statements were generated by negating true ones, the probe learns "contains a negation." If your true statements come from Wikipedia and false ones from an LLM, it learns style. The only control that works is **cross-dataset transfer**: train on one topic/generator and evaluate AUROC on a structurally different one. A probe that scores 0.96 in-distribution and 0.61 cross-dataset has learned the dataset.

**"True" and "the model believes it's true" are different targets, and you probably want the second.** For hallucination detection you want a probe on the model's own confidence in its own generated claim, which requires labelling the model's outputs, not curated propositions.

**Unsupervised methods do not escape this.** **Burns et al. (2022), "Discovering Latent Knowledge in Language Models Without Supervision"** (CCS) finds a direction satisfying logical consistency constraints without labels, which is elegant. Subsequent work showed the objective is satisfied by *many* salient binary features in the activations — anything that happens to be consistent and prominent, including features introduced by the prompt itself — so it does not reliably isolate truth. Say that; it is the kind of critical read interviewers weight heavily.

**The probe is a shallow readout, and shallow readouts get gamed.** If you close the loop and train against the probe, you optimize the model to fool it.

**💰 Math:** the probe itself is nearly free — a `4096`-dim dot product per token, ~8k FLOPs against roughly `2 × 8e9 = 16 GFLOP/token` for an 8B forward pass, i.e. `8e3 / 1.6e10 = 0.00005%` overhead. Compare with a second-model LLM judge on every response: at a 700-token response and 300-token judge prompt, `1000 tokens × $0.30/Mtok ≈ $0.0003` per check, which at 2M responses/day is `2e6 × 0.0003 = $600/day ≈ $18k/month`. That cost gap is why probes keep getting proposed for guardrails despite their weakness — so the honest framing is that a probe is a cheap *triage* signal that routes 3% of traffic to the expensive judge, not a replacement for it.

**⚠ Trap:** deploying a probe trained on last quarter's checkpoint against this quarter's fine-tune. Probe directions are checkpoint-specific; a LoRA merge can rotate the relevant subspace enough to silently halve AUROC while the probe keeps returning confident scores. Probes need the same retrain-and-revalidate discipline as any other model artifact, pinned to a checkpoint hash.

### Tell me about refusal directions. Why does that result change how I think about shipping open weights?

This is the interpretability finding with the most direct security consequence, and it is the one I would lead with if an interviewer asks whether interpretability matters commercially.

The finding: in a wide range of open-weight chat models, refusal behavior is mediated by a **single direction** in the residual stream. You compute it the boring way — mean activations over harmful instructions minus mean activations over harmless ones, at a mid-depth layer — and then two symmetric interventions work. **Ablate** it (project it out of every write to the residual stream, at every layer and every position) and the model stops refusing harmful requests while otherwise remaining coherent and capable. **Add** it to a harmless prompt and the model refuses to explain photosynthesis.

**📄 Paper:** **Arditi et al. (2024), "Refusal in Language Models Is Mediated by a Single Direction."** The deployment-relevant part is that the ablation can be **baked into the weights** — since projecting out a direction from every residual write is itself a linear operation, you can compose it into the output projection matrices `W_O` and `W_down` once, producing an ordinary checkpoint with no runtime hooks and no inference cost. That is a rank-one weight edit, computable in minutes on a laptop for a 7B model.

Three consequences I actually act on.

**One: safety fine-tuning on released weights is a veneer, and your threat model must say so.** If your company's plan for a weights release rests on "the model refuses harmful requests," the plan is that an attacker spends an afternoon and a few hundred paired prompts. The defensible arguments for open release are about marginal uplift over existing resources, not about the refusal behavior surviving.

**Two: for a product built on open weights you serve yourself, guardrails belong outside the model.** Input classification, output classification, tool-call authorization, and rate limits are separate systems with separate failure modes. A model's own refusal is a UX layer and a first line of defense, never the control.

**Three: the flip side is a legitimate product control.** The same machinery lets you *raise* refusal strictness for a high-risk tenant without maintaining a second checkpoint — one vector, one coefficient, per-request.

**⚠ Trap:** concluding "safety is one-dimensional." What the result shows is that the *final gating* of refusal is well-approximated by one direction in these models — the upstream harm classification is not one-dimensional, and ablating the direction does not make the model unable to recognize harm; it makes it unwilling to act on that recognition. Overstating it as "alignment is a single vector" is the kind of glib line that gets marked down in an alignment-adjacent round.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Refusal in open-weight chat models is largely mediated by a single residual-stream direction that you can compute with difference-of-means and remove with a rank-one weight edit, at zero inference cost. So I treat model refusal as UX, not as a security boundary — the enforcement layer has to be an external classifier and an authorization check on tool calls."

### Our production model gives the same wrong answer to a whole family of queries, and prompt tweaks haven't fixed it. Walk me through how you localize it.

I want to be honest about the ordering here, because the interpretability-first version of this answer is a trap: nine times out of ten the cause is in your stack, not in the model's weights, and the expensive tooling should come last. But the reason to know the expensive tooling is that it gives you a **stopping condition** — a way to conclude "this is in the weights, stop editing prompts" instead of iterating forever.

**Step 1 — reproduce and minimize.** Get a deterministic repro at `temperature=0`, then bisect the prompt down to the minimal input that still produces the wrong answer. Half of these die here, when removing a paragraph of the system prompt fixes it and you discover two instructions were contradictory.

**Step 2 — look at the actual bytes sent to the model.** Not your template inputs: the fully rendered string after chat-template application, with special tokens visible. Duplicated BOS, a role tag that does not match training, a retrieved chunk containing an unescaped delimiter that terminates your context section early. This is the single highest-yield step and it requires zero ML knowledge.

**Step 3 — go to logprobs before you go to activations.** Ask for top-20 logprobs at the divergence token. Three distinct pictures: the wrong answer at p=0.98 (the model is confident and wrong — a knowledge or circuit problem, keep going); the wrong answer at p=0.31 with the right answer at p=0.29 (a *sampling* problem — your temperature or a penalty is doing this, and no amount of prompt engineering will fix a coin flip); or the right answer entirely absent from the top-20 (usually a formatting or constraint problem — a logit-bias or grammar mask is banning it).

**Step 4 — build the contrastive pair.** Find the minimally-different prompt that produces the *right* answer. This is the most intellectually demanding step and the whole rest depends on it: if you cannot construct a pair that differs in one variable, you do not yet have a hypothesis.

**Step 5 — logit lens the pair, layer by layer, at the final position.** You are looking for the crossover depth: at which layer does the wrong token become the argmax in the broken run, and what is the corresponding trajectory in the working run? A typical finding is "in the working prompt the correct entity is top-1 from layer 19; in the broken one the wrong entity takes over at layer 14 and never yields." Now you have a depth.

**Step 6 — patch.** Denoise: copy residual activations from the working run into the broken run at each `(layer, position)` and measure normalized logit difference restoration. The heatmap usually shows one or two hot cells — often at the position of a specific token in the retrieved context or the system prompt. Then refine: attention output vs MLP output at that layer, then per-head, then check what that head is attending to.

**Step 7 — read the attention.** If a name-mover-style head at the final position is attending to the wrong entity token, you have a concrete story: some earlier token is winning the retrieval competition. Frequently the culprit is a distractor in the retrieved context — which converts a "model problem" back into a *retrieval* problem, and that is a great outcome because it is cheap to fix.

**Step 8 — choose the intervention at the right layer of the stack**, cheapest first: fix retrieval or chunking; restructure the prompt so the correct entity is positionally advantaged; add a few-shot exemplar that recruits the copying circuit; ablate or steer if you serve open weights; fine-tune only if the pattern is broad and stable enough to justify a checkpoint.

**💰 Math on when this is worth doing:** steps 1–3 cost an engineer maybe two hours. Steps 4–7 on an 8B open-weight model cost a day of engineer time plus a single A100 (roughly `24 × $2/hr ≈ $48` of compute — negligible against the salary). So the real question is whether the bug is worth a day. My rule: if the failure family is reproducible, affects >0.5% of a revenue-relevant query class, and has survived two rounds of prompt iteration, it is worth the day — because a third round of prompt iteration also costs a day and has a much worse track record.

**🔍 Failure taxonomy — how internals-based fixes break in production, as a decision procedure.** Ask these in order after any localization-driven change ships. *Did the metric move on the fitting prompts only?* → you found a dataset artifact; refit on a differently-generated set and re-gate. *Did the target behavior improve while a neutral-corpus perplexity rose more than a few percent?* → the intervention is broad, not surgical; reduce the coefficient or narrow the layer. *Did it work at launch and decay over weeks?* → your input distribution drifted away from the contrastive pairs you fit on; feature and direction artifacts need scheduled revalidation, not fire-and-forget. *Did it break after a model or quantization change with no code change?* → directions and dictionaries are pinned to a weights hash; this should have been a deploy-time assertion, not an incident. *Did the behavior return under adversarial input?* → you removed one path, and self-repair or an alternate circuit covered it; that is expected, and it is why an internals fix is never a security control.

**⚠ Trap:** doing this on a hosted API model. You have no activations, so steps 5–7 are impossible; the deepest you can go is logprobs, and many providers cap or omit them. If deep localization matters for your product, that is an argument for keeping an open-weight model in the fleet for *diagnosis* even if you serve a hosted one — but be honest that circuits do not transfer between models, so it only helps for reasoning about failure classes, not for this specific bug.

### We fine-tuned on 40k support tickets and instruction-following got noticeably worse. How would you diagnose that?

First, resist the interpretability answer for ten minutes, because the base rates are brutal and the boring causes account for most of these.

**The boring causes, in order of frequency.** A **chat-template mismatch**: you trained on a template that differs from what you serve — a different role tag, a missing generation prompt, an extra BOS — and the model is now operating slightly off-distribution at inference. **Loss masking**: if you computed loss over prompt tokens as well as completion tokens, the model has been trained to *produce* system prompts and user turns, which corrupts turn-taking. **Learning rate too high** for the adapter rank; instruction-following is one of the first capabilities to degrade under aggressive LR. **No replay**: training on 40k in-domain tickets with 0% general instruction data is the textbook recipe for catastrophic forgetting — 5–30% replay of a general instruct mix is the standard mitigation. **Format homogeneity**: if every ticket response is three short paragraphs, the model has learned that as an unconditional prior and will now ignore "answer in one word."

Only after those are ruled out do the internals tools earn their keep, and here is what each actually buys.

**Per-module weight-delta norms.** Cheapest possible diagnostic: for a LoRA, compute `‖BA‖_F` per target module per layer, normalized by `‖W‖_F`. If the updates are concentrated in late-layer MLPs you have shifted output style; if they are concentrated in mid-layer attention you have plausibly disturbed routing/instruction circuitry. This takes ten minutes and needs no interpretability machinery.

**Per-token KL divergence between base and tuned** on a held-out *instruction-following* set. For each position, compute `KL(p_base ‖ p_tuned)`. The positions where KL is largest tell you *what kind* of token the fine-tune changed. If the mass is on content tokens, fine — that is what you trained for. If it is concentrated on the first few tokens after the assistant turn begins, and on structural tokens like list markers and closing braces, your fine-tune has overwritten the response-planning prior, which is exactly the instruction-following symptom.

**Directional check.** Take the base model's difference-of-means direction for the behavior you lost (e.g. "follows an explicit length constraint" vs "ignores it", built from paired prompts on the base model). Project both models' activations at that layer onto it. If the tuned model's activations have collapsed toward the "ignores" side across all prompts, the fine-tune has shifted the model's operating point along that axis, and you can quantify by how much rather than saying "it feels worse."

**Model diffing with a crosscoder** is the heavyweight option: train a joint dictionary on base and tuned activations and inspect features that are tuned-only or base-only. This is the most informative and by far the most expensive, and I would only reach for it if the fine-tune is a repeated, strategic pipeline rather than a one-off.

**⚠ Trap:** concluding "catastrophic forgetting" from an eval-suite regression alone. Regression on a general benchmark is consistent with forgetting, with template mismatch, and with the model having correctly learned a domain style that conflicts with the benchmark's format expectations. Those three have completely different fixes. The disambiguator is to run the same benchmark *with the fine-tune's own template and format conventions* — if the regression largely disappears, it was format, not forgetting.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I'd rule out template mismatch, prompt-token loss and zero replay first, because those explain most of these. Then I'd do a per-token KL between base and tuned on held-out instruction data — if the divergence concentrates on structural and turn-opening tokens rather than content tokens, the fine-tune ate the response-planning prior, and the fix is replay plus a lower LR, not more domain data."

### You want a "conciseness" dial in the product. Would you ship a steering vector? Talk me through the whole decision.

I would ship it only on open weights I serve myself, only after a capability-regression gate, and — this is the part people miss — only after I have worked out what it does to my prefix cache.

**Why it is attractive.** The compute cost is genuinely zero-ish: one `d_model` vector at bf16 is `4096 × 2 = 8,192 bytes`, and adding it to the residual stream is `4096` adds per position, against roughly `2 × 8e9 = 16 GFLOP` per token for an 8B forward. That is `4096 / 1.6e10 ≈ 2.6e-7`, i.e. 0.000026% — unmeasurable. Compare against the alternative of maintaining three fine-tuned checkpoints for terse/normal/verbose: three sets of weights to train, evaluate, version, and hold in GPU memory, or LoRA adapters with swap overhead and a multi-adapter serving path. A continuous coefficient also gives you something no checkpoint can — a *dial*, not three buttons, which you can move per-request based on a UI slider or on device class.

**The prefix-cache interaction, which is the real engineering catch.** If you apply the steering vector at *all* positions including the prompt, then the residual stream at every prompt position changes, which changes the K and V tensors written to the KV cache. Two requests with the same system prompt but different steering coefficients therefore have **different KV entries and cannot share a cache prefix**. Your prefix-cache key must include the steering configuration, and your hit rate fragments across the number of distinct coefficients in flight. With a continuous slider you have effectively destroyed prefix caching.

**💰 Math:** suppose a 6,000-token shared system prompt, 200k requests/day, and prefix caching that currently gives you a 90% hit rate. Prefill of 6,000 tokens on an 8B model costs about `2 × 8e9 × 6000 = 9.6e13 FLOP`; at a realistic 300 TFLOP/s effective that is `9.6e13 / 3e14 ≈ 0.32 s` of GPU time per uncached prefill. At 90% hit rate you pay it 20,000 times/day = `6,400 s ≈ 1.8` GPU-hours; at 0% hit rate you pay it 200,000 times = `64,000 s ≈ 17.8` GPU-hours. On an H100 at roughly `$3/hr` that is the difference between `$5.3/day` and `$53/day` — call it `$1.6k/year` versus `$19k/year` on that one prompt, before counting the TTFT regression your users see. The fix is architectural, not financial: **quantize the coefficient to 3–5 discrete levels** so the cache fragments 5 ways instead of infinitely, or **apply steering only at generated positions**, which leaves prompt KV untouched and preserves the cache entirely. I would default to the second.

**The gate before shipping.** A capability suite at the exact deployed coefficient — instruction-following, one task-specific accuracy eval, and neutral-corpus perplexity — plus a check for non-monotonicity by sweeping the coefficient and plotting both the target behavior and the capability metric. You are looking for the widest coefficient band where behavior moves and capability does not, and you deploy in the middle of that band, not at its edge.

**⚠ Trap:** assuming the vector transfers across checkpoints. It does not. A steering vector is a function of a specific weights hash; after any fine-tune, merge, or even a quantization change, it must be recomputed and re-gated. Pin the vector to the checkpoint in your model registry and fail the deploy if they disagree — treat it exactly like a schema migration paired to an application version.

### Honestly: what can interpretability *not* tell me today?

I would rather be the candidate who volunteers this list than the one who has to be pushed into it, because over-claiming on interpretability reads as a lack of research literacy.

**It cannot give a complete account of any interesting behavior in a frontier model.** We have partial circuits for narrow tasks in small models, and per-prompt attribution graphs at production scale that explain a substantial-but-not-total fraction of the computation. Nobody can hand you "here is everything the model does when it writes a SQL query."

**It cannot certify absence.** No current method lets you say "this model will never do X." You can find and remove a mechanism you located; you cannot enumerate mechanisms you did not find. This is why interpretability complements red-teaming and evals rather than replacing them.

**SAE feature sets are not canonical.** Feature splitting means the dictionary is a function of width and sparsity; feature absorption means the taxonomy is not clean; and no SAE reconstructs its site perfectly, so some fraction of the computation is outside your basis entirely. Treat a dictionary as a *useful coordinate system for this analysis*, not as the model's ontology.

**Localization does not imply the only cause.** Self-repair means components compensate for each other, so "we ablated the head and behavior dropped 40%" is a lower bound on that head's role and says nothing about whether an equivalent path exists.

**Interventions have side effects that are hard to bound.** Steering a feature changes many things; measuring "did anything else break" is a general evals problem that interpretability does not solve.

**Human review does not scale.** Millions of features need automated interpretation, and automated interpretation is itself a model-based measurement with its own error modes.

Where it *has* delivered concrete value, and I would name these specifically: the refusal-direction result (a real security consequence for open weights), feature steering as a demonstrated control surface, model diffing for fine-tunes, and blind-auditing exercises where teams given only model access and interpretability tools successfully identified a deliberately-implanted hidden objective — that last one is the strongest existing evidence that these tools can find something a behavioral eval missed.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Today interpretability is a debugging and auditing tool, not a verification tool. It can tell me where a behavior is implemented well enough to intervene, and it can surface something evals missed. It cannot certify that a behavior is absent, and any complete-circuit claim about a frontier model should be read as a claim about a linearized replacement model on a narrow prompt distribution."

### 🎯 You're in an applied AI round, not a research round. How do you bring interpretability up without sounding like you're name-dropping papers?

By talking about *interventions and measurements*, never about vocabulary. The failure mode is real and interviewers are allergic to it: a candidate says "we could use SAEs to find the feature and steer it" for a problem where the answer was that the retriever was returning stale chunks. That reads as someone who has read blog posts, and it will cost you the round.

The reframe that works: interpretability is the branch of debugging that applies when the bug is inside the weights. So bring it up **only after you have exhausted the layers you can see**, and bring it up as the escalation with an explicit trigger condition.

Here is the bad version and the good version, verbatim.

**Bad:** "I'd use mechanistic interpretability — sparse autoencoders, attribution graphs, activation patching — to understand what the model is doing internally."

**🗣 Say this in the room (the good version):** "My ladder for a persistent wrong answer is: rendered prompt bytes, then top-k logprobs at the divergence token, then a contrastive prompt pair. If the wrong token is at p=0.98 across a whole family of inputs and a minimally-different prompt fixes it, that's a signal the cause is in the weights rather than in my stack — and on an open-weight model I can localize it by comparing per-layer predictions between the two prompts and then patching activations between the runs. In practice I stop at the logprobs step most of the time, because most of these turn out to be a retrieval or template bug."

Notice what that does. It shows the ladder, it names a stopping condition, it is honest about base rates, it demonstrates you know the technique without asserting you would reach for it first, and it puts the cheap steps first — which is what an applied interviewer is actually screening for. The word "mechanistic" never appears.

Two more moves that land well in applied rounds. **Logprobs are interpretability.** Treating the output distribution as an observable rather than just taking the top token is the highest-leverage habit in this whole section and it works on hosted APIs. **Say what you'd measure.** "I'd check whether the correct answer is even in the top-20" is a concrete, executable next step; "I'd investigate the model's internal representations" is not.

**🎯 In an alignment-adjacent loop** (Anthropic, DeepMind safety, an OpenAI alignment-flavoured team) the calculus inverts: there, depth on activation patching methodology, the necessary-vs-sufficient distinction, self-repair, and the limits of SAE evidence is exactly what is being probed, and hedging will read as shallowness. Route your emphasis by the loop, and if you cannot tell which loop you are in, ask the recruiter what fraction of the round is research discussion.

### What would it cost to run SAE-based feature monitoring in production? Give me the numbers.

Let me do the arithmetic, because the answer is "cheaper than people fear if you sample, and prohibitive if you do not."

**Parameter and memory cost.** For Llama-3-8B, `d_model = 4096`. A 16× expansion dictionary is `d_sae = 65,536`. Encoder plus decoder is `2 × 4096 × 65,536 ≈ 5.37e8` parameters — 537M, which at bf16 is `5.37e8 × 2 = 1.07 GB` **per hooked site**. If you naively hook all 32 layers you are carrying `32 × 1.07 = 34.4 GB` of dictionaries alongside a 16 GB model. That is a bigger memory footprint than the model, on a card whose HBM you are already fighting for KV cache. This alone kills "SAEs everywhere."

**Compute cost.** Encoder plus decoder is `2 × 2 × 4096 × 65,536 ≈ 2.15 GFLOP` per token per site. The base model is `2 × 8e9 = 16 GFLOP` per token. So one hooked site adds `2.15 / 16 ≈ 13.4%` compute. Hooking four sites is +54%; hooking all 32 is +430% — you would be spending four times more compute on the microscope than on the model.

**💰 The design that actually works:** hook **one** site (a mid-depth residual layer, where behavioral features are richest), and run it on a **sampled** fraction of traffic, offline. At 1% sampling, the marginal cost is `0.01 × 13.4% ≈ 0.134%` of serving compute — genuinely free. Concretely, at 2M requests/day averaging 800 total tokens, 1% sampling is `20,000 × 800 = 1.6e7` tokens/day through one SAE, `1.6e7 × 2.15e9 = 3.4e16` FLOP; at a conservative 200 TFLOP/s effective that is `3.4e16 / 2e14 = 172 s` — under three minutes of GPU time per day, well under `$1/day` at H100 rates. And because it is offline and sampled, you can run it on a spot instance from stored activations rather than in the request path at all.

**What you get for that.** A daily distribution over feature-activation frequencies. The monitoring value is *drift*: feature 41,203 fired on 0.3% of tokens for six weeks and today fires on 4%. That is a change-detection signal on your input distribution or your prompt template that no output-level metric will catch, and it is interpretable enough to act on — often it turns out a new tenant onboarded with a different document format.

**⚠ Trap:** putting the SAE in the request path to gate responses. Beyond the 13.4% latency tax, you have now made a research artifact a hard dependency of your availability, and SAE features are checkpoint-specific — the next model update invalidates every threshold you tuned. Feature monitoring belongs in the analytics plane, asynchronous, with alerting thresholds you expect to re-baseline on every model version.

**📅 Volatile:** dictionary widths, sparsity conventions and the availability of pretrained open SAE suites (e.g. the Gemma Scope release of SAEs across layers and sites of Gemma 2 models) move quickly. Verify what pretrained dictionaries exist for your model family before assuming you must train your own — training is the expensive part.

### Prompt, few-shot, steering vector, LoRA, or SAE feature clamp — give me your decision rule.

The organizing principle is **specificity versus cost versus reversibility**, and I would answer it as a ladder you climb only when the rung below fails a measured eval rather than a vibe check.

**Prompt instruction.** Zero marginal cost beyond tokens, instant reversibility, works on any hosted API, and it is the only option that is legible to a non-engineer. Fails when the behavior needs to hold across long contexts (instructions get crowded out), when it conflicts with a strong prior from post-training, or when you need it to be non-bypassable by user input. `**💰**` cost is literally the tokens: a 200-token behavioral instruction at `$3/Mtok` is `$0.0006` per call, `$120/day` at 200k calls — and if it is a stable prefix, prefix caching makes it near-free.

**Few-shot exemplars.** Substantially stronger than instructions for *format* and *style*, because they recruit the copying/induction machinery directly rather than asking the model to interpret a description. Costs context and money proportional to example length, and eats the same context budget as your retrieved documents. Use when the behavior is easier to demonstrate than to describe.

**Steering vector.** Continuous, per-request, ~zero compute, requires open weights you serve. Right for a *dial* over a global trait — tone, verbosity, hedging, refusal strictness. Wrong for anything involving knowledge or multi-step procedure. Carries the prefix-cache interaction discussed above and requires a capability gate per coefficient.

**SAE feature clamp.** More semantically precise than a difference-of-means vector — you are manipulating one concept rather than a direction that entangles several — but requires a trained dictionary for your exact checkpoint plus the memory and compute above, and features are unstable across model versions. I would reach for this in a research or auditing context, and in a product only if a difference-of-means vector proved too coarse and the behavior was worth the operational weight.

**LoRA / full fine-tune.** The only option that adds *capability* rather than reweighting existing behavior: new domain knowledge patterns, a genuinely new output format, latency wins from a smaller model matching a larger one. Costs a training run, an eval suite, a checkpoint to version and serve, and it is the least reversible — reversibility here means a rollback deploy, not deleting a line.

**My rule in review:** you may not skip a rung. If someone proposes a fine-tune, the question is "show me the eval where the prompt-plus-few-shot version failed, with the number." Most proposed fine-tunes die on that question, and the ones that survive it are the ones worth doing. And the inverse rule: if you have been prompt-engineering the same behavior for more than two sprints, the ladder has already told you to climb.

### Everyone reads attention maps as explanations. Convince me that's wrong — and tell me what attention *does* tell me.

Attention weights are a **routing decision, not an importance score**, and the gap between those is where the misreading lives.

The mechanism: a head's contribution to the residual stream is `Σ_j α_ij · (v_j W_O)` — the attention weight `α_ij` times what the OV circuit *writes*. If the OV circuit maps a token's value to something near zero, or writes into a direction irrelevant to the output, then `α_ij = 0.9` contributes nothing. Attention tells you where a head *looked*; the OV circuit determines whether looking mattered. Any claim of the form "the model focused on the word 'not', so it understood the negation" ignores the second factor entirely.

**📄 Paper:** **Jain & Wallace (2019), "Attention is not Explanation"** showed you can often find alternative attention distributions producing the same prediction; **Wiegreffe & Pinter (2019), "Attention is not not Explanation"** pushed back that this depends on what claim you are making. The honest summary is that attention is weak evidence that requires causal follow-up, which is exactly what path patching provides.

**The thing that makes naive attention-reading actively misleading in modern models: attention sinks.** Softmax forces attention weights to sum to 1, so a head that has nothing useful to attend to at a given position still has to put its mass *somewhere*. Models learn to dump it on the first token(s) — usually BOS — which act as a no-op destination. So a large fraction of the attention mass in your heatmap is a null routing decision that means "this head is inactive here," and reading it as "the model is focused on the beginning of the prompt" is backwards.

**📄 Paper:** **Xiao et al. (2023), "Efficient Streaming Language Models with Attention Sinks" (StreamingLLM)** identified this and turned it into an engineering result: a naive sliding-window KV cache that evicts the first tokens causes catastrophic quality collapse, because you deleted the sink that every head depends on. Keeping the first ~4 tokens permanently pinned alongside the sliding window restores quality. That is one of the cleanest cases of an interpretability observation becoming a serving-infrastructure requirement.

**⚠ Trap:** implementing a "keep the most recent N tokens" context-window compaction in your own agent loop and being surprised that quality collapses. It is the same bug at the application layer: pin the system prompt and the opening tokens, evict from the middle. And if you use an engine's sliding-window attention, verify it implements sink retention rather than assuming it.

What attention *does* tell you: it is an excellent **hypothesis generator** for where information flows. A head at the final position attending strongly to a specific retrieved chunk is a lead worth patching. Value-weighted attention (scaling `α_ij` by `‖v_j W_O‖`) is a strictly better plot than raw attention and takes one extra line to compute. But the claim only becomes real when you delete the edge and the output moves.

### 🎯 Alignment-adjacent loop: design an experiment that establishes whether behavior X is *caused* by mechanism Y. Then tell me what you'd do with a negative result.

This is the question the round is actually about — not whether you know the tool names, but whether you can design a falsifiable experiment. I would structure the answer as a protocol and commit to it before seeing data.

**Hypothesis, stated so it can fail.** "Behavior X (the model asserts a specific false fact when the prompt contains a particular framing) is mediated by direction/component Y at layer `l`." Vague hypotheses — "attention is involved" — cannot be falsified and should be rejected at the design stage.

**Predictions before the experiment.** Write down, in advance: if Y mediates X, then (a) ablating Y reduces the X-metric by at least some pre-registered amount; (b) adding Y to prompts that do not exhibit X *induces* X; (c) the effect is dose-dependent in the coefficient; (d) the effect appears on held-out prompts drawn from a different generator than the ones used to fit Y. Pre-registering the effect size is what stops you from declaring victory on a 4% shift.

**The metric.** Normalized logit difference between the X-answer and its counterfactual, averaged over a prompt set, reported with a confidence interval over prompts — not over random seeds, because prompt variance dominates.

**Necessity and sufficiency, run separately.** Noising (patch corrupted Y into the clean run) tests necessity; denoising (patch clean Y into the corrupted run) tests sufficiency. Report both; a component can be sufficient and unnecessary because of backups, and reporting only the flattering direction is the most common way these results get overstated.

**Controls, and this is where most candidate answers fall apart.** A **random-direction control**: apply the same intervention with a random unit vector of the same norm at the same layer, and show the effect is far smaller. A **matched-component control**: ablate a different head with a similar activation norm. A **capability control**: measure perplexity on neutral text under the intervention, because an intervention that degrades everything trivially reduces the X-metric. And an **intermediate-variable check** to guard against the subspace illusion: verify the intervention changed the downstream quantity your mechanism story predicts, not merely the final logit.

**Generalization.** Fit Y on prompt set A, evaluate on prompt set B built by a different author or generator, and report both. If the effect only exists on the fitting set, you found a dataset artifact.

**What I do with a negative result** — and I would answer this part with some emphasis, because eagerness to publish a null is a genuine signal in an alignment round. First, check the experiment can detect an effect at all: run a positive control by intervening on something known to work (an embedding-level ablation of the key token, say). If the positive control also shows nothing, the pipeline is broken, not the hypothesis. If the positive control fires and Y does not, I write up the null with the effect size and confidence interval, because "direction Y does not mediate X, with the effect bounded below 5%" is a genuinely useful result that stops three other people from repeating the work. Then I revise: the mechanism may be distributed across several components (test joint ablation), it may be at a different layer (sweep), or the behavior may not be linearly mediated at all (which is itself worth knowing, and is where you would look at multi-dimensional or nonlinear structure).

**⚠ Trap:** running the experiment, seeing a 30% effect, and reporting "Y causes X." A 30% restoration means 70% of the behavior comes from somewhere else. The honest sentence is "Y accounts for approximately 30% of the effect on this prompt distribution, with the remainder unlocalized," and interviewers in this loop are specifically listening for whether you say the second half.

**🏋 Drill:** pick any open-weight chat model under 8B. In three hours, unaided: build 64 paired harmful/harmless instructions, compute a difference-of-means direction at three candidate layers, ablate it from all residual writes, and report refusal rate before and after — *plus* a random-direction control at matched norm and MMLU-style accuracy on 200 held-out questions to show capability survived. Pass criterion: your write-up states an effect size with a confidence interval, reports the control, and names one thing your experiment cannot rule out.
