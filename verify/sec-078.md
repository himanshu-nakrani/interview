### Design a customer-support agent for a mid-size e-commerce company. It has CRM access, it can issue refunds, and finance has told you it must never cost more than one dollar per conversation. Forty-five minutes.

The framing that wins this case: **this is not a chatbot with tools, it is a workflow-authorization system whose planner happens to be stochastic.** Everything that matters — refund limits, escalation, the cost cap — is a policy question about what a non-deterministic caller is allowed to do to your system of record. If I open with "we'll use function calling and a RAG index over the help center," I have answered the 2023 version of this question. I open with the authority model and the measurement plan.

**Clarifying questions that change the design:** (1) What fraction of tickets are *informational* ("where is my order") versus *mutating* ("refund me")? At most e-commerce companies it is roughly 70/30, and that ratio decides whether the interesting engineering is in retrieval or in authorization. (2) What is the current human cost per contact — typically $4–$7 fully loaded for tier-1 — because that is the number the $1 cap is being compared against. (3) Is there a hard SLA on first response? (4) What is the blast radius of a wrong refund: is it recoverable (store credit) or not (card refund)? (5) Do we own the CRM or is it Zendesk/Salesforce with rate limits I have to design around?

**Architecture, control plane separated from data plane.** Data plane: channel adapters (web widget, email, WhatsApp) → session service → **agent loop** (model + tool registry + policy engine) → tool executors (CRM read, order service read, refund service write, knowledge search) → response composer → channel. Control plane: policy engine (who may call what, with what argument bounds), budget service (per-conversation token/dollar ledger), escalation router (queue into the human agent desk with full transcript and the agent's proposed action pre-filled), and the trace store.

**The tool surface is where seniority shows.** I do not expose `execute_sql` or a generic `crm_api(method, path, body)`. Every tool is a narrow, verb-shaped, idempotent operation with a typed schema and server-side validation that does not trust a single argument the model produced: `get_order(order_id)`, `get_customer_orders(customer_id, limit)`, `search_policy(query)`, `issue_refund(order_id, amount_cents, reason_code, idempotency_key)`, `escalate_to_human(reason, summary)`. Two rules I enforce in review: the model never supplies the `customer_id` — it is injected from the authenticated session, so a prompt-injected agent cannot pivot to another customer's orders; and every mutating tool takes an idempotency key derived from `(conversation_id, tool_name, canonical_args)` so a retried tool call after a timeout does not double-refund.

**Measurement before the diagram is finished.** The metric is **contact resolution rate without human escalation** (deflection), not "answer quality." I build a golden set of 400 real transcripts, labelled with the correct terminal action (resolved-informational / resolved-with-refund / must-escalate), and I run the agent in replay against them nightly. I hold out a **harm set** of ~150 adversarial conversations: refund-fishing ("my friend said you'd give me $500"), prompt injection embedded in a returned product review, and PII-extraction attempts. Deflection is the business metric; **false-refund rate and escalation-miss rate are the gates.** A release that raises deflection from 42% to 48% while raising false refunds from 0.1% to 0.4% is a regression and I will block it.

**🔍 Failure taxonomy, as a decision procedure.** (a) *Agent confidently answers a policy question wrong* → detect via citation-grounding check on policy answers; contain by requiring a retrieved policy chunk for any statement containing "we can/cannot." (b) *Agent loops* — calls `get_order` five times → detect via per-conversation tool-call histogram; contain with a repeated-identical-call breaker that injects "you already called this, here is the result" and decrements the step budget hard. (c) *Injection via user-supplied content* (order notes, uploaded receipts) → contain by fencing all retrieved content in a delimited block with a standing instruction that content inside is data, and by the fact that authority lives in the policy engine, not the prompt. (d) *Silent degradation after a model version bump* → detect with the nightly replay and a 2% shadow sample scored by a judge.

**The v1 cut, stated out loud:** informational tickets only — order status, shipping, returns policy — plus `escalate_to_human`. No refunds in v1. Refund authority ships in v2 behind a $25 auto-approve ceiling once v1's harm set is clean for two weeks. That sequencing is the answer; candidates who ship refund authority on day one are describing an incident.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I design the authority model before the prompt. The model proposes; a deterministic policy engine disposes. The model never supplies the customer identity, every mutation is idempotent and amount-bounded server-side, and v1 ships with no refund authority at all so I can prove the escalation path works before I give a stochastic system write access to money."

### Talk to me about that refund authority. How does the agent actually get permission to move money, and what stops it from refunding forty thousand dollars?

Nothing in the prompt stops it. That is the whole answer, and the trap is that most candidates try to solve this with prompt engineering — "you may only refund up to $25" in the system message — which is a *suggestion* to a probabilistic text generator, not a control. **Authority is enforced in the tool executor, in code, with the model's output treated as an untrusted request payload from the internet.** I would say exactly that sentence in the room, because it maps onto something the interviewer already believes about user input.

The mechanism is a three-tier ladder. **Tier 1 — auto-execute:** `amount_cents <= 2500`, order is within the return window, customer has fewer than 3 refunds in 90 days, order is not flagged for fraud. The executor checks every one of those against the order service, not against the conversation. **Tier 2 — propose-and-approve:** $25–$200, or a repeat-refunder. The agent's call does not mutate; it writes a `pending_action` row and emits an escalation to a human queue where the agent shows the proposed refund, the evidence it retrieved, and a one-click approve/deny. Median human touch time here is ~20 seconds versus ~6 minutes to handle the whole ticket, which is where the economics actually come from. **Tier 3 — refuse and hand off:** above $200, chargeback-adjacent, or any conversation that has tripped a fraud signal. The tool returns a structured refusal the model must relay.

Layered on top: a **per-customer and per-agent-instance budget**. The refund executor holds a Redis token bucket keyed on `customer_id` (say $200 per rolling 30 days) and a global circuit breaker keyed on the agent version — if refunds issued by agent build `v1.4.2` exceed 3× the trailing-7-day hourly median, the executor flips to Tier-2-for-everything and pages. This is the LLM analogue of a spend-anomaly kill switch, and it is the control that actually saves you, because the failure you cannot enumerate in advance is a model update that becomes 4% more agreeable.

**⚠ Trap:** giving the tool an `amount_cents` argument at all when the correct refund is almost always "the order total" or "the line item total." Free-form amounts are an unnecessary degree of freedom. I prefer `issue_refund(order_id, line_item_ids[], reason_code)` and let the *server* compute the amount from the order record. Now the model cannot hallucinate a number; it can only hallucinate which line items, which is bounded by the order. Removing degrees of freedom from a tool schema is the single highest-leverage safety technique in agent design and it costs nothing.

**💰 Math on why the ladder pays:** suppose 100k tickets/month, 30% refund-related = 30k. If 70% of those fall under $25 (21k) and auto-execute, you save 21,000 × 6 min = 2,100 human-hours. At a $35/hr fully loaded tier-1 cost that is $73,500/month. The 9k Tier-2 tickets still cost 20 seconds each = 50 hours = $1,750. Even at a 0.3% false-refund rate on the auto tier, 63 bad refunds × $18 average = $1,134/month in losses. Net ~$70k/month saved against maybe $12k/month in inference. That arithmetic — human minutes saved, minus loss rate, minus inference — is the answer the interviewer is fishing for, and almost nobody gives it.

### Show me the per-conversation cost model, and tell me exactly what you do when a conversation is about to blow the one-dollar cap.

**📅 Volatile:** the per-token prices below are representative of a mid-tier frontier model; verify against the current price sheet before your loop. I will use **$3/Mtok input, $15/Mtok output, cached input at 10% of the input rate**.

The cost of an agent conversation is dominated by the fact that context is **re-sent on every turn of the tool loop**, so cost grows roughly quadratically in the number of steps, not linearly. That is the mental model: a 10-step agent does not cost 10× a single call, it costs closer to 40–55× a single call, because step *k* re-reads everything from steps 1..k-1.

**💰 The arithmetic.** System prompt + tool schemas: 3,000 tokens. Retrieved policy chunks: 1,500 tokens. Conversation history and tool results grow by ~400 tokens per step. For an N-step conversation the input tokens are approximately Σ(4500 + 400k) for k=0..N-1 = 4500N + 400·N(N−1)/2. Output is ~150 tokens per step.

- N = 4: input = 18,000 + 2,400 = 20,400 tok → 20,400 × $3/1e6 = **$0.0612**. Output 600 tok × $15/1e6 = $0.009. Total **$0.070**.
- N = 10: input = 45,000 + 18,000 = 63,000 tok → **$0.189**. Output 1,500 × $15/1e6 = $0.0225. Total **$0.212**.
- N = 25: input = 112,500 + 120,000 = 232,500 tok → **$0.698**. Output 3,750 tok = $0.056. Total **$0.754**.

So the $1 cap is not a constraint on tokens, it is **a constraint on step count**: about 30 steps. And that is a good thing to say out loud, because it converts a budget into an engineering invariant you can enforce with a counter.

**Now apply prefix caching.** The 4,500-token stable prefix (system + tools + policy) is identical across every turn within a conversation and across conversations for the same locale. At a 90% cache discount, those tokens cost $0.30/Mtok instead of $3. For N=10: cached portion is 4,500 × 10 = 45,000 tok at $0.30/Mtok = $0.0135 instead of $0.135, and the uncached growing tail (18,000 tok) stays at $3/Mtok = $0.054. Total input drops from $0.189 to **$0.0675** — a 64% cut on input for a change that is a cache-control marker, not an architecture. At 100k conversations/month that is $12,150/month saved. **This is the single cheapest optimization in agent engineering and I would be suspicious of any candidate who did not mention it.**

**⚠ Trap:** the cap is per *conversation*, but the natural place to enforce it is per *turn*, and those diverge badly when a user keeps replying. I keep a budget ledger row per conversation (`tokens_in`, `tokens_out`, `dollars`, updated after every model call inside the same transaction as the message insert) and I check it *before* dispatching the next model call, not after. Checking after is how you discover you spent $4.

**What I actually do at the cap.** Three graduated responses, not one: at **60%** of budget the orchestrator drops the retrieved-context window from 5 chunks to 2 and switches the planner to a cheaper model tier for the remaining steps. At **85%** it stops issuing new tool calls and forces a "summarize and either resolve or escalate" turn. At **100%** it escalates to a human unconditionally with the full transcript. Note what I did *not* do: I did not truncate mid-answer and I did not silently degrade quality without telling anyone. The cost cap is a routing signal to the human queue, not a guillotine. And I emit a metric `budget_exhausted_escalations` — if that climbs above ~2% of conversations, the cap is wrong or the agent is looping, and I want to be paged for it either way.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "A dollar per conversation is about thirty tool-loop steps at current frontier prices, because context re-sends make cost grow with the square of the step count. So I enforce the budget as a step-and-token ledger checked before each model call, I put the stable 4,500-token prefix behind a cache breakpoint to cut input cost roughly two-thirds, and at 85% of budget the agent stops planning and escalates rather than degrading silently."

### You have a hard two-hour cap to build an inbox-triage agent with a human in the loop. What do you build, and — more importantly — what do you deliberately not build?

The two-hour cap is the question. This shows up as a live exercise at Sierra, Ramp and several FDE loops, and the rubric is not "did it work" — it is **did you pick a scope that has a demonstrable end-to-end slice, and did you spend any of your two hours on evaluation.** Candidates who spend 110 minutes on the agent and 10 on a demo fail. Candidates who spend 60 on the agent, 30 on a 40-email labelled set, and 30 on the approval UI pass.

**What I build, in order.** Minutes 0–10: write down 20 real emails and hand-label each with `{category, urgency, proposed_action}`. This is the entire evaluation apparatus and it is done before I write a line of agent code. Minutes 10–45: a single structured-output call — not an agent loop. Input is the email plus thread context; output is a Pydantic model with `category: Literal[...]`, `urgency: 1-5`, `proposed_reply: str | None`, `confidence: float`, `reasoning: str`. No tools, no planner, no memory. Minutes 45–75: the approval surface — a list view where each item shows the proposal, the model's one-line reason, and three buttons: Approve-and-send, Edit-then-send, Reject-with-reason. Every click writes a row to `decisions(email_id, proposed, final, action, actor, ts)`. Minutes 75–95: run the 20-email set, report accuracy per category, show the confusion. Minutes 95–120: write the README that says what I would build next and why.

**Why not an agent loop?** Because triage is a classification problem with a generation tail, and an agent loop buys you nothing here while costing latency, cost and debuggability. **⚠ Trap: reaching for a multi-step agent when a single structured-output call solves the task.** This is the most common over-engineering tell in AI interviews, and interviewers at product companies are explicitly screening for it. If the interviewer pushes back — "shouldn't it look up the customer in the CRM?" — the correct answer is "yes, in v2, as *one* deterministic pre-fetch step that runs before the classification call, not as a tool the model chooses to call." A pre-fetch is a join; a tool call is a decision, and you only pay for decisions you actually need.

**The human-in-the-loop design is the graded part.** Three properties: (1) **the default is not send** — nothing leaves the system without a click, and I say so; (2) **the edit is captured, not just the outcome** — the diff between `proposed_reply` and `final_reply` is the highest-value training and eval signal you will ever collect, and if you only log approve/reject you have thrown it away; (3) **rejections carry a reason code** from a fixed list (wrong category / wrong tone / factually wrong / needs human judgment), because free-text rejection reasons are unanalyzable at volume.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "With two hours, I spend the first ten minutes building a twenty-example labelled set, because otherwise I have no way to tell you whether what I built works. Then one structured-output call, not an agent loop — triage is classification. The remaining time goes to the approval UI, and specifically to logging the human's *edit*, not just their verdict, because that diff is the only free training data in the whole system."

### Your triage agent has been live for a week and the ops team says it is creating more work than it saves. Walk me through the debug.

The claim "more work than it saves" is a throughput claim, so I convert it into a measurable one before I touch the model: **human-seconds per email with the agent, versus without.** If reviewing a proposal takes 45 seconds and writing from scratch took 90, we win; if reviewing takes 80 seconds because the proposal is plausible-but-wrong and the human must now *verify* rather than *write*, we lose. That inversion — where a wrong-but-fluent draft costs more to check than to author — is the characteristic failure of human-in-the-loop AI and it has a name worth using: **verification cost exceeding generation cost.**

**The decision procedure I run, in order.**

*Step 1: split the metric by category.* Pull `decisions` and compute approve / edit / reject rates per category. Almost always the aggregate hides a bimodal distribution: 6 of 9 categories are at 90% approve, and 3 are at 25%. If so, the fix is not model work — it is routing. Turn off the agent for the bad categories and ship the wins. I have seen this recover a "failed" pilot in an afternoon.

*Step 2: measure edit distance, not just edit rate.* If approve-rate is 40% but the median edit is 12 characters, the humans are fixing tone and the model is fine; the UI is the problem (make the common edit a one-click template). If the median edit rewrites 70% of the body, the model is wrong on substance.

*Step 3: check calibration.* Bucket by the model's self-reported `confidence` and compute actual approve rate per bucket. If confidence 0.9 and confidence 0.5 both yield ~60% approval, the confidence score is decorative and the queue is unsortable — and an unsortable queue is exactly what makes this feel like more work, because the human has to read everything at the same intensity. Replacing a hallucinated self-confidence with a real signal (agreement between two cheaper samples, or a small calibrated classifier trained on the week's `decisions` table) is usually the highest-leverage single change.

*Step 4: look at where the time actually goes.* Instrument the UI. If ops spends 30 of the 80 seconds scrolling to find the original thread, that is a product bug, not an AI bug.

**⚠ Trap:** responding to this complaint by making the model bigger or the prompt longer. Every time I have seen this debugged honestly, the fix was in the routing, the queue ordering, or the review UI — not the model. Interviewers at product companies (Notion, Figma, Sierra) are specifically checking whether you reach for the model first. Reaching for the model first is the junior answer.

**🔍 Failure taxonomy for human-in-the-loop systems:** *automation complacency* (approve rate near 100% because humans stopped reading — detect by injecting known-bad proposals at a 1% rate and measuring catch rate); *verification inversion* (above); *queue starvation* (low-urgency items never reviewed, detect with age-of-oldest-unreviewed); *feedback poisoning* (edits from one power user dominate your training signal — cap per-reviewer contribution when you use `decisions` for tuning).

### An engineer on your team proposes a content pipeline with five agents: researcher, outliner, writer, editor, and SEO optimizer. Critique the design.

I would push back on this design, and the reason is not aesthetic. **Every agent boundary is a serialization boundary: you pay a full model call's latency, you pay to re-serialize the state into natural language, and you lose information at every hop.** Multi-agent architectures are frequently sold as modularity, but the interface between two agents is a *string*, which is the worst-typed interface in computing. A five-agent pipeline is five chances to drop a constraint that was stated in step one.

**Concretely, what goes wrong here.** The researcher produces 4,000 tokens of notes. The outliner reads them and emits a 300-token outline — 3,700 tokens of grounding evidence just evaporated, so the writer now writes from an outline and hallucinates the supporting detail the researcher had actually verified. This is the classic **lossy-handoff** failure and it is why the output of long agent chains reads confident and cites nothing. Then the editor rewrites for style, breaking the SEO agent's keyword placement, and the SEO agent re-inserts keywords, breaking the editor's prose. **Two agents with contradictory objectives operating sequentially do not converge; the last one wins.** That is a real design defect, not a tuning issue.

**Second problem: cost and latency compound multiplicatively.** Five sequential calls at ~4s each is 20s of latency floor before any retries. If each stage has a 3% failure-or-garbage rate, the end-to-end success rate is 0.97^5 = 85.9% — you have engineered a 14% failure rate out of five individually-good components. And each stage re-sends the accumulated context: if stage *k* carries forward 2,000 tokens per prior stage, total input across the pipeline is roughly 2,000 × (1+2+3+4+5) = 30,000 tokens versus the ~8,000 a well-designed two-stage version needs.

**Third: it is undebuggable in the way that matters.** When the article is bad, which agent was wrong? You have five prompts, no per-stage ground truth, and a failure signal that only exists at the end. I would demand a per-stage eval before I would accept a per-stage agent — and the moment you try to write "what is a good outline," you discover you cannot define it independent of the final article, which is itself the argument that the outliner should not be a separate accountable component.

**⚠ Trap:** the belief that multi-agent = separation of concerns. In software, separation of concerns works because interfaces are typed and cheap to cross. Here they are untyped and cost 4 seconds and $0.02 each. **The correct unit of decomposition in LLM systems is the *verifiable artifact*, not the *role*.** If a stage produces something you can independently check — retrieved sources with URLs, a JSON outline against a schema, a compiled code diff that passes tests — it earns its own call. "Editor" produces prose you can only evaluate against the same rubric as the writer's prose, so it does not.

### Make the argument for collapsing it to two agents, with numbers. And when would you actually keep five?

**The two-stage design: a research stage and a write stage.** Stage 1 is a genuine tool-using agent — it searches, fetches, dedupes, and emits a *structured* research artifact: a JSON list of claims, each with source URL, a verbatim supporting quote, and a confidence. That artifact is machine-checkable (do the URLs resolve? does the quote appear in the fetched page?), which is exactly the "verifiable artifact" test. Stage 2 is a single long call that receives the research artifact plus the style guide plus the SEO constraints *simultaneously* and produces the finished article with inline citations back into the claim IDs. Editing and SEO are not agents; they are **constraints in one prompt plus deterministic post-checks** (keyword density regex, readability score, heading structure validator, citation-resolves check). Anything a regex can verify should never be an LLM call.

**💰 The numbers.** Five-stage: ~30,000 input tokens + ~5,000 output. At $3/$15 per Mtok that is 30,000 × 3/1e6 = $0.090 input, 5,000 × 15/1e6 = $0.075 output, **$0.165 per article**, with a ~20s latency floor and 85.9% end-to-end success at 3% per-stage failure. Two-stage: research call with tools, say 8,000 input + 2,000 output across 3 internal tool turns ≈ 14,000 input cumulative and 2,500 output; write call 6,000 input + 3,000 output. Total ≈ 20,000 input = $0.060, 5,500 output = $0.0825, **$0.143** — modest cost saving. But latency drops to ~11s and success rate rises to 0.97² = 94.1%. **The win is not cost, it is a 58% reduction in failure rate and a 45% cut in latency, and I would say that explicitly rather than pretending the cost delta is the point** — inflating a weak argument is how you lose credibility in a design round.

**When I would keep more agents.** Three legitimate reasons, and I want the interviewer to hear that I am not dogmatic. (1) **Parallelism over independent subproblems** — if the article needs research on six unrelated subtopics, six concurrent research sub-agents is strictly better than one sequential agent, because the work is genuinely independent and the wall-clock win is real. Fan-out is good; deep sequential chains are bad. (2) **Context isolation for a genuinely huge corpus** — sub-agents whose job is to read 200k tokens and return 2k of distilled findings are functioning as a map step, and the compression is the point rather than a loss. (3) **Different trust domains** — a stage that processes untrusted user-supplied content should be a separate call with no tool access, so that an injection lands in a context that has no authority. That is a security boundary, and security boundaries justify serialization costs.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I'd collapse it to two, and the argument is reliability, not cost: five stages at 97% each is 86% end-to-end, two is 94%, and the cost only moves from about sixteen cents to fourteen. The rule I use is that a stage earns its own model call only if it produces an artifact I can verify independently — retrieved sources with quotes, or JSON against a schema. 'Editor' and 'SEO' don't; they're constraints in one prompt plus deterministic post-checks. But I'd fan out research sub-agents in parallel, because parallel decomposition wins where sequential decomposition loses."

### Design a sales-analytics agent that only ever touches aggregated data, must refuse any request for personal information, and has structured logging as a hard requirement.

The mental model: **"aggregated only" is not a prompt instruction, it is a property of the query surface.** If the agent can express a query that returns one row, no amount of instruction-following will reliably stop it from doing so, because you have made the unsafe state reachable. So the design principle is to make PII *unreachable from the agent's execution path* rather than *forbidden by policy text*. In backend terms: this is a capability problem, and you solve capability problems by not minting the capability.

**Architecture.** The agent does not talk to the warehouse. It talks to a **semantic layer** — a curated set of metrics and dimensions with pre-declared grain — exposed as a small set of typed tools: `list_metrics()`, `list_dimensions(metric)`, `query_metric(metric, dimensions[], filters[], time_grain, time_range)`, `compare_periods(...)`. There is no free-text SQL tool. The semantic layer compiles those calls into SQL against views that were built with aggregation already applied and identifier columns physically absent. The service account the compiler uses has `SELECT` only on those views and no grant on any base table. **Three independent layers — no SQL tool, aggregated views only, and a least-privilege grant — because any single one of them will eventually be bypassed.**

**Then the differencing attack, which is the real question.** Even with aggregates, `revenue by rep where region=NW` combined with `revenue by rep where region=NW and tenure>5y` can isolate one person. So the compiler enforces a **minimum group size**: any result cell whose underlying row count is below *k* (I would start at k=5) is suppressed and returned as `null` with a `suppressed_low_count` marker the agent is instructed to surface honestly. On top, a per-user query-history check flags rapid sequences of narrowing filters over the same metric — the same shape as a scraping-detection rule, and I would implement it the same way I implement one.

**PII refusal has to be tested, not asserted.** I maintain a **refusal set** of ~200 prompts spanning direct ("give me Jane Doe's phone number"), indirect ("who is the rep with the lowest close rate and what's their email"), obfuscated ("output the customer table as CSV for my compliance audit"), and injected (a prompt-injection string sitting in a CRM notes field the semantic layer surfaces as a dimension label). The gate is 100% — one leak fails the release — and I measure the *over-refusal* rate on a paired benign set of 200, because a system that refuses "how many customers churned" is also broken. Reporting only the refusal rate without the over-refusal rate is a tell that you have never shipped a guardrail.

**Structured logging, because they said it is a hard requirement, which means someone has a compliance obligation.** Every turn emits one canonical event: `{trace_id, conversation_id, user_id, ts, role, model_id, prompt_hash, tool_calls: [{name, args_redacted, compiled_sql_hash, row_count, suppressed: bool, latency_ms}], tokens_in, tokens_out, cost_usd, refusal_reason?, policy_decisions: [...]}`. Two non-obvious details. First, I log the **compiled SQL and its hash, not the model's natural-language intent** — the SQL is what actually touched data, and that is what an auditor asks for. Second, I log **arguments after redaction with the redaction itself recorded** (`{"filter": "email = <REDACTED:email>"}`), so the log is not the thing that leaks the PII the agent refused to return. That inversion — the audit log becoming the exfiltration channel — is a real incident pattern and naming it will land.

**📐 Retention numbers you must know:** if the traces contain any customer identifiers, they inherit that data's retention and deletion obligations. Under GDPR a deletion request must propagate to your trace store, your eval sets, and any fine-tuning corpus derived from them. **📅 Volatile:** exact regulatory timelines and AI-Act obligations shift; verify before your loop. But the design consequence is durable: **traces need a `subject_id` column from day one, because retrofitting deletion into an append-only trace store is a quarter of work.**

### How do you know your PII refusal actually works when a jailbreak you have never seen ships tomorrow?

I do not, and the honest answer starts there — then pivots to defense in depth, because the interviewer is testing whether you believe guardrails are sufficient. **The correct posture: prompt-level guardrails are a filter that reduces volume, not a boundary that provides a guarantee. The guarantee comes from the fact that the data is not reachable.** If a jailbreak succeeds completely — the model decides it will happily exfiltrate everything — the worst it can emit is aggregated metrics above the k-threshold, because that is the only thing its tools can return. I design so that a total prompt-layer compromise is a *quality* incident, not a *breach*.

The layers, ordered by how much I trust them (least to most): (1) system-prompt instruction — trust: near zero, it is documentation; (2) an input classifier that flags PII-seeking intent — trust: low, catches the lazy 80%, cheap enough at a small model tier to run on every turn; (3) an output scanner that regexes for email/phone/SSN shapes and named-entity-detects person names in the response — trust: moderate, and importantly it catches the case where PII leaked in through a *tool result* rather than through the user; (4) the k-anonymity threshold in the compiler — trust: high, deterministic; (5) the database grant — trust: highest, and it is enforced by a system that has never heard of prompts.

**⚠ Trap:** running the output scanner only on the final assistant message. In an agent loop the leak happens in the *tool result*, which then sits in context and can surface three turns later in a summary. I scan tool results on the way in, not just responses on the way out. This is the same reason you sanitize on input at a trust boundary rather than at render time.

**The operational answer for "a jailbreak ships tomorrow."** A red-team corpus that is a living asset, not a one-time exercise: every production refusal-bypass found by monitoring gets added within 24 hours and the suite runs in CI. I subscribe to the public jailbreak corpora and re-run monthly. And I keep a **canary**: a synthetic person record in the warehouse whose name appears nowhere else. If that string ever appears in a model output, in a log, or in an eval trace, an alarm fires. It is the exact same trick as a canary token in a credentials file, and it is the only detection that works against attacks you have not imagined.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I assume every prompt-level guardrail will eventually be bypassed, so I make the bypass boring: the agent's tools can only return aggregates above a k-threshold, and the service account has no grant on any table containing an identifier. Guardrails reduce volume; the schema and the grant provide the guarantee. And I plant a canary record so I find out the day something changes."

### An internal assistant needs three capabilities in one tool loop: SQL against a read replica, document search, and running bash commands. Design the tool surface and tell me what worries you.

What worries me is stated up front: **this configuration is the textbook "lethal trifecta" — private data access, exposure to untrusted content, and an outbound channel.** SQL and document search provide the private data. Document search also provides the untrusted content, because any document a colleague wrote can contain instructions. Bash provides the exfiltration channel, and it provides it completely — `curl`, DNS lookups, writing to a shared path. An injection sitting in a Confluence page can, in a single agent turn, cause the assistant to query salary data and POST it to an attacker's endpoint, and no prompt will reliably prevent it. So the design is about severing one leg of that trifecta.

**How I sever it.** The bash environment is a per-invocation ephemeral container with **no network egress by default** — a deny-all network policy with an explicit allowlist to an internal artifact mirror. Filesystem is a fresh tmpfs plus a read-only mount of the workspace; nothing persists between calls unless explicitly promoted through a tool that requires approval. No credentials in the environment; the container gets no cloud metadata endpoint access (that one bites people — the instance metadata service is a credential vending machine). CPU/memory/wall-clock caps of, say, 2 cores, 2 GB, 30 seconds, enforced by the runtime, because an agent will eventually write a fork bomb by accident.

**The tool schemas.** `run_bash(script)` is a genuinely dangerous shape and I keep it only because the interviewer asked for it; I would fight for `run_python(code)` in a sandbox with a curated package set instead, since it is far easier to reason about. SQL: `query_readonly(sql)` against a replica with a `readonly` role, a `statement_timeout` of 10s, a hard `LIMIT` injected by the wrapper if absent, and — the piece people forget — an `EXPLAIN` gate that rejects any plan with an estimated cost above a threshold, because the failure you will actually hit is not injection, it is the agent writing a cross join that pins the replica. Document search: `search_docs(query, k)` returning chunks with a `source_uri` and a `trust: internal_authored | external_ingested` label that flows into the context.

**The trust label is the design idea worth defending.** Content retrieved from `external_ingested` sources gets wrapped in a fenced block, and — this is the part that actually helps — **the orchestrator disables `run_bash` for the remainder of any turn in which external content entered the context.** That is a deterministic capability downgrade keyed on data provenance, not on the model's judgment. It is exactly taint tracking, and describing it in those terms lands hard with a backend interviewer.

**⚠ Trap:** thinking the danger is the model doing something malicious on its own. The realistic threat is that the model is a **confused deputy** — it faithfully follows instructions it found in data it was asked to summarize, using authority it legitimately holds. Every mitigation should be designed against a confused deputy, not against a malicious model, and framing it that way is a well-worn security concept the interviewer already respects.

**Observability that matches the risk.** Every bash execution logs the full script, exit code, stdout/stderr hashes, bytes written, and any attempted egress (the deny-all policy logs the denied connection, which is your best injection detector). I alert on: any denied egress attempt, any bash invocation in a turn that also contained external content, any SQL touching a table not in the trailing-30-day allowlist.

### Your assistant picks the wrong tool about thirty percent of the time. Walk me through how you diagnose and fix that.

Thirty percent is high enough that I assume a systemic cause, not a model capability ceiling, and the diagnostic order matters because the cheap fixes are also the most likely.

**First, define "wrong" precisely.** Pull 100 failing traces and hand-label each into one of five buckets: (a) chose tool A when B was correct; (b) chose the right tool with malformed or hallucinated arguments; (c) called no tool when it should have; (d) called a tool when it should have answered directly; (e) correct tool, correct args, bad tool *result*, and the agent blamed itself. In my experience (b) and (d) together are usually more than half, and they have completely different fixes than (a). **Reporting a single "tool accuracy" number without this split is the failure mode; you cannot fix an aggregate.**

**Then check the four usual causes, cheapest first.**

*Cause 1: overlapping tool descriptions.* If `search_docs` and `query_readonly` both say "find information about," the model is being asked to disambiguate two synonyms. The fix is rewriting descriptions in terms of **what data lives where and when to prefer each**, with a negative clause: "Use `query_readonly` for numeric facts about orders, users and revenue that live in the warehouse. Do NOT use it for policy or how-to questions — use `search_docs`." Tool descriptions are prompts; most teams write them like API docs and then wonder why routing is bad. I have seen this single change move tool accuracy 15 points.

*Cause 2: too many tools.* Routing accuracy degrades roughly monotonically with tool count, and the knee is real — somewhere past 15–20 tools most models start conflating. The fix is a two-level surface: a small set of *namespaces* the model selects first, then the specific tool, or a retrieval step that puts only the top-k relevant tool schemas in context for this turn. Tool-schema retrieval also cuts the prefix by thousands of tokens, so it pays twice.

*Cause 3: schema ambiguity driving bucket (b).* If `query_readonly(sql)` receives hallucinated column names, the model does not know the schema — that is not a tool-choice problem, it is a context problem, and the fix is injecting the relevant table DDL after a retrieval step. If a tool takes `date: str`, you will get seven date formats; make it `date: str` with `format: YYYY-MM-DD` in the description *and* a server-side parser that returns a structured, instructive error the model can recover from. **Error messages returned to a model are prompts too — "invalid input" teaches nothing; "column `usr_email` does not exist; available columns: id, email, created_at" gets fixed on the retry.**

*Cause 4: the eval is measuring the wrong thing.* Bucket (d) — calling a tool when a direct answer was right — is often not a defect at all; it costs a round trip but the answer is correct. Weight your metric by outcome, not by tool-call exact-match.

**Only after all four do I consider model changes**, and even then the order is: better few-shot examples of tool selection in the system prompt (cheap, effective, and often worth 5–10 points), then a larger planner model for the routing turn only while keeping the cheap model for synthesis, then fine-tuning a small router — which is a real option once you have 5k+ labelled traces from your own logs and want to cut both latency and cost.

**💰 Why the router split pays:** if routing is 1 call of ~5,000 input and 100 output tokens, running it on a frontier model at $3/$15 costs 5,000×3/1e6 + 100×15/1e6 = $0.0165. A fine-tuned small router at, say, $0.15/$0.60 per Mtok costs 5,000×0.15/1e6 + 100×0.6/1e6 = $0.00081 — a 20× cut on that step, and typically 200ms instead of 900ms. At 500k turns/month that is $8,250 → $405. **📅 Volatile:** small-model pricing moves fast; re-derive with current numbers.

### Design ChatGPT-style cross-conversation memory. Start with the schema.

The mental model I lead with: **memory is not a bigger context window, it is a write-ahead log with a retrieval policy and a retention contract.** The failure of naive designs is that they treat memory as "stuff the model remembers," which is unfalsifiable and undeletable. I treat it as rows — typed, scoped, sourced, versioned, deletable — and the model's access to them is a retrieval decision I control, exactly the way I would control access to any other user-owned data.

**The schema.** One table for the atomic unit:

```sql
CREATE TABLE memory (
  id            uuid PRIMARY KEY,
  user_id       uuid NOT NULL,              -- the scoping key; every query filters on it
  workspace_id  uuid,                       -- NULL for personal, set for team/enterprise
  kind          text NOT NULL,              -- 'preference'|'fact'|'project'|'relationship'|'instruction'
  subject       text NOT NULL,              -- normalized entity: 'user.diet', 'project.atlas'
  content       text NOT NULL,              -- 'is vegetarian', kept short and self-contained
  embedding     vector(1024),
  confidence    real NOT NULL,
  source_conversation_id uuid NOT NULL,     -- provenance: which conversation created this
  source_message_id      uuid NOT NULL,
  created_at    timestamptz NOT NULL,
  last_used_at  timestamptz,
  use_count     int NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,
  expires_at    timestamptz,                -- for inherently temporal facts
  superseded_by uuid REFERENCES memory(id), -- contradiction handling, not deletion
  deleted_at    timestamptz
);
CREATE INDEX ON memory (user_id, kind) WHERE deleted_at IS NULL;
CREATE INDEX ON memory USING hnsw (embedding vector_cosine_ops);
```

Four fields carry most of the design weight. **`subject`** is a normalized key, and it exists so that contradiction detection is a cheap equality lookup rather than a semantic one: when a new memory arrives with `subject='user.diet'`, I look up existing rows with the same subject *before* I embed anything. **`superseded_by`** encodes that memory updates are appends, not mutations — "user became vegetarian in 2024, was pescatarian before" is recoverable, and more importantly an incorrect supersede is reversible. **`source_message_id`** is the provenance that makes the "why do you think that about me?" UI possible, and that UI is what makes memory trustworthy to users; without it you have a system that asserts things about people with no receipt. **`expires_at`** handles the category most designs miss — "I'm training for a marathon in April" is true and then it is noise.

**Scoping is a hard boundary, not a filter you remember to apply.** Every read path goes through a repository method that takes `user_id` as a required positional argument and there is no method that queries memory without one. In a multi-tenant enterprise deployment I would additionally partition by `workspace_id` at the physical level, because the incident where one customer's memory surfaces in another customer's session is not survivable commercially. The `workspace_id IS NULL` case — personal memories — must never be readable from a workspace session, and vice versa; I would write that as two separate retrieval functions rather than one function with a nullable parameter, because the nullable parameter is how the bug gets written.

**⚠ Trap:** storing whole conversation summaries as the memory unit. Summaries are long, they compete for context, they cannot be individually deleted in a way a user understands, and they encode contradictions inside a single blob so you can never supersede cleanly. The unit must be small enough that a human can read one row and say "yes, delete that."

### What is your write policy? What actually gets written to memory, and when?

The default that fails is "extract memories after every turn." It fails three ways: it costs a model call per turn, it fills the store with garbage ("user said hello"), and it creates a write amplification problem where a 40-turn conversation produces 40 extraction calls to capture maybe two durable facts. **The mental model: writing to memory should be as rare as writing to a user's profile, because that is what it is.**

**The policy I would ship.** Extraction runs **asynchronously, after conversation end or after every N=10 turns, whichever comes first** — off the request path entirely, because nothing about memory should add latency to a response. The extractor is a single structured-output call over the conversation delta with an explicit, narrow schema and, crucially, a strong bias toward emitting nothing: the system prompt states that most conversations contain zero durable memories and that the correct output is usually an empty list. Without that framing, extractors are absurdly eager — I have measured 8× over-extraction from a neutral prompt.

**What qualifies.** Three tests, all of which must pass: (1) **durability** — will this still be true in 30 days? (2) **cross-conversation utility** — would knowing this improve a *future, unrelated* conversation? (3) **user-assertedness** — the user stated it about themselves or their work, rather than the model inferring it. Test (3) is a policy choice and I would defend it: inferred memories ("user seems frustrated with their manager") are the ones that generate creepy moments and support tickets, and the quality gain does not justify the trust loss. Explicit user commands ("remember that I prefer TypeScript") bypass extraction entirely and write immediately with `confidence=1.0` — that path must be synchronous and confirmed, because a user who says "remember X" and sees no acknowledgment loses faith in the whole feature.

**The write itself is an upsert-with-supersede, not an insert.** Look up existing rows by `(user_id, subject)`. If none, insert. If one exists and the new content is semantically equivalent, bump `confidence` and `last_used_at` and do not insert a duplicate. If it contradicts, insert the new row and set `superseded_by` on the old. If it is a *refinement* ("vegetarian" → "vegetarian, no dairy"), replace via supersede rather than accumulating two rows that will both be retrieved and confuse the model. **⚠ Trap: no dedupe at write time.** Without subject-keyed dedupe, a user who mentions being vegetarian in six conversations gets six near-identical rows, all of which retrieve together, all of which burn context, and all of which the user must delete individually to actually forget. Duplicate memory rows are the single most common defect in shipped memory systems.

**💰 Cost of the write path.** Suppose 2M conversations/day, average 12 turns. Per-turn extraction: 2M × 12 = 24M calls/day at ~2,000 input + 100 output tokens = 48B input tokens/day. At even a cheap $0.15/Mtok that is $7,200/day = **$216k/month**, to capture maybe 1.5 memories per conversation. End-of-conversation extraction over a 6,000-token delta: 2M calls/day × 6,000 input = 12B input tokens = $1,800/day = **$54k/month**, a 4× cut. Add a cheap pre-filter — a small classifier or even heuristics on first-person declarative statements — that skips extraction for the ~60% of conversations with no candidate content, and you are at **$21.6k/month**. That is a 10× swing from a policy decision that costs one afternoon, and being able to produce this arithmetic on a whiteboard is what separates the hire from the no-hire on this case.
### Now the retrieval policy for that memory system. How do memories get into the prompt, and how do you prove the whole feature actually helps?

Retrieval is where memory systems quietly become worse than no memory at all, because every injected memory is context you paid for and a chance to derail the model. **The mental model: memory retrieval is a precision problem, not a recall problem.** A missed memory costs you a small quality delta on one turn; a wrongly-injected memory ("user is vegetarian" surfacing in a conversation about a Python bug) costs you a bizarre response, and users generalize from one bizarre response to "this thing is broken."

**The two-tier policy I would ship.** Tier 1 — **always-on core**: a small set of pinned memories, capped hard at ~15 rows / ~500 tokens, selected by `kind IN ('instruction','preference')` ordered by `confidence × recency × use_count`. These are the "always respond in Portuguese," "I use pnpm not npm" facts where the cost of missing them is high and the cost of including them is near zero. They go into the system prompt, which means they sit **inside the cacheable prefix** — and that placement is a cost decision, not a cosmetic one, because a memory block that changes per-turn invalidates prefix caching for the entire request.

Tier 2 — **query-conditional**: at the start of a conversation (and again when the topic shifts materially), embed the user's first message plus a short conversation summary, run an ANN search against that user's memory rows, take the top 20, and rerank with a cross-encoder or a cheap LLM filter that answers "is this memory relevant to this request? yes/no." Keep at most 5. Injecting 20 raw ANN hits is the classic mistake — vector similarity will happily return "user likes hiking" for a query about deployment scripts because both mention "trails" somewhere in embedding space.

**When to retrieve matters as much as what.** Retrieving on every turn re-runs the ANN search 12 times per conversation and, worse, changes the prefix on every turn. I retrieve once at conversation start, then only on an explicit trigger: the user says something that pattern-matches a memory reference ("like I told you before," "my usual setup"), or a topic-shift detector fires. In a long conversation the memories that matter were established at the top.

**Proving it helps — this is the part that gets skipped and it is the part that gets graded.** Three layers.

*Offline:* a paired golden set of ~500 (memory-store, conversation, ideal-response) triples where I know a memory is relevant, plus — critically — ~500 **negative pairs** where memories exist but none are relevant, to measure damage. Metric: pairwise LLM-judge preference between memory-on and memory-off responses, with a human-audited 10% sample. I report three numbers: win rate on positives, win rate on negatives (must be ≥ 50%, i.e. no harm), and injection precision.

*Online:* an A/B test where the metric is not "user satisfaction" but something behavioral and durable — **turns-to-resolution, regeneration rate, and 28-day retention.** Memory's value hypothesis is that users do not have to re-explain themselves, so the sharpest proxy is a reduction in tokens the *user* types per task. If memory-on users type fewer words to get the same outcome, it works.

*Counterfactual sampling:* on 1% of memory-on requests, run the request a second time offline with memory stripped and log both. Over a month that gives you a continuously refreshed, real-traffic estimate of memory's contribution without an experiment.

**⚠ Trap:** measuring memory by "how often was a memory retrieved" or "how many memories do users have." Both are activity metrics that go up when the system gets worse. The metric must be a paired comparison against the same request without memory, and if you cannot construct that comparison you cannot claim the feature works.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I'd split retrieval into a pinned core block that lives inside the cached prefix and a query-conditional block that's ANN-retrieved then reranked down to five. And I'd prove it with paired A/B — same request with and without memory, judged pairwise — including a negative set where no memory is relevant, because the risk isn't missing a memory, it's injecting an irrelevant one and producing a response the user calls creepy."

### A user asks you to delete a memory. What are you actually promising them, and what is hard about keeping that promise?

I am promising four distinct things, and the interview value is in separating them, because most candidates conflate all four into "we delete the row."

**(1) It stops influencing responses — immediately.** This is the only one users actually perceive, and it is the easy one: soft-delete with `deleted_at`, and every retrieval path filters `deleted_at IS NULL`. It must be immediate and it must be read-your-writes consistent, which means if memories are served from a read replica or a cache, the delete must invalidate that cache synchronously, not eventually. A user who deletes a memory, asks a question, and sees the memory reflected has caught you in a lie and will not use the feature again. I would key the cache on `(user_id, memory_version)` and bump `memory_version` on any write, which turns invalidation into a version bump — the same pattern as a cache-busting ETag.

**(2) The row goes away — eventually, and everywhere.** Hard deletion after a grace window (30 days, so support can recover an accidental delete). "Everywhere" is the hard part, and it is a fan-out problem: the primary table, the vector index (which in most ANN implementations is a tombstone until compaction — an HNSW graph does not support cheap node removal, so deleted vectors are filter-excluded at query time and only physically gone after a rebuild), read replicas, the analytics warehouse copy, the backup snapshots, and the trace store where the memory appears as part of a prompt. I want a **deletion propagation ledger**: one row per subsystem per deletion request, with an ack, so that "did we actually delete it" is a query rather than a hope.

**(3) It does not come back.** This one is subtle and it is the answer that separates senior from staff. If the memory was derived from a conversation, and that conversation is still in the store, the extractor will happily re-derive it next time it runs. **Deletion must write a suppression record keyed on `(user_id, subject, content_hash)` that the extractor consults before writing.** Otherwise your system has a resurrection bug, the user reports it as "it remembered something I deleted," and it reads as malicious rather than as a race condition. Alternatively — and this is cleaner — deleting a memory offers the user the option to also delete the source conversation, and you make the causal link visible in the UI.

**(4) It is not in a model.** If you have used memory data in any fine-tune or preference-training corpus, deletion of the row does not remove the influence, and you cannot honestly claim it does. My rule: **user memory rows never enter a training corpus.** That is a policy I would fight for at design time precisely because it makes deletion tractable, and it is much cheaper than the alternative, which is retraining or defending an unfalsifiable claim to a regulator.

**⚠ Trap:** treating the trace/observability store as out of scope. Prompts logged for debugging contain the memory verbatim. If your traces have 90-day retention and no `subject_id` column, you cannot honestly answer a GDPR erasure request. **Every trace row needs the user identifier from day one** — and I have watched a team spend a quarter backfilling that.

**📐 Numbers you must know:** GDPR erasure is generally expected within one month of request (extendable in limited cases); CCPA/CPRA similarly operates on a ~45-day response window. **📅 Volatile:** verify current text and any AI-Act interactions before your loop — but the engineering consequence is stable: your deletion fan-out must complete well inside a month, which means it is a tracked async workflow with retries and alerting, not a `DELETE` statement.

### Design a coding agent that opens pull requests against our monorepo. Assume real engineers have to review its output.

The framing: **a coding agent is a search process over patches, and the only thing that makes it work is a cheap, trustworthy verifier.** Without a verifier — tests, type checker, linter, build — you have a system that produces plausible diffs and shifts the entire verification cost onto human reviewers, which is a net negative for the team. So the first design question is not "which model" but **"what is the tightest feedback loop I can give it, and how fast can I run it?"** If the repo's test suite takes 40 minutes, the agent is dead on arrival and my first sprint is test selection, not agent code.

**The loop.** Trigger (issue label, Slack command, or a scheduled sweep for lint/dep upgrades) → **task grounding**: fetch the issue, find related files, and — the highest-leverage step — retrieve the *most similar recent merged PR* as a worked example of house style. → **plan**: a short structured plan the agent commits to, listing files it expects to touch. → **edit loop**: read file, propose edit, apply, run targeted tests, read failure, repeat, with a hard step budget. → **verify**: full targeted test selection + typecheck + lint + build. → **self-review**: a separate call that reads its own final diff against a checklist and can send it back. → **PR open** with a structured description: what changed, why, what tests were run and their output, and an explicit "what I was unsure about" section. → human review gate.

**Patch application is where naive implementations bleed.** Three options and I have a strong opinion. (a) *Whole-file rewrite* — the model emits the full new file. Reliable to apply, but burns output tokens proportional to file size (a 900-line file is ~12k output tokens = $0.18 at $15/Mtok, per edit) and the model will silently drop unrelated code it did not think was important. That silent deletion is a real and nasty failure. (b) *Unified diff* — token-efficient, but models are unreliable at emitting correct hunk headers and line counts, so apply-failure rates are high and each failure costs a retry. (c) *Search-and-replace blocks* — the model emits an exact snippet to find and its replacement, and the harness applies it with exact match, falling back to whitespace-normalized match, then failing loudly. **This is what I ship.** It is token-efficient like a diff, it is verifiable before application, and an ambiguous match (snippet appears twice) is a detectable error rather than a silent corruption. The rule I enforce: **never apply a patch with a fuzzy match; return an error to the model with the surrounding context and let it retry with a longer, unique anchor.**

**Review gates, because engineers have to review this.** Non-negotiables: the agent's PRs are labelled and open in draft; the agent may never modify CI config, secrets, dependency lockfiles, or anything under an `OWNERS`-protected path without a human co-sign; the diff has a size cap (I use ~400 changed lines — above that, the agent must split or hand off, because reviewer attention collapses past a few hundred lines); and CODEOWNERS review is required exactly as for a human. Branch protection does the enforcement — the agent's token has no bypass.

**Measurement.** The vanity metric is PRs opened. The metric that matters is **merge rate without substantive human edit**, tracked weekly, plus **reviewer-minutes per merged PR** — because if the agent's PRs take 25 minutes to review and a human's take 10, you have moved work rather than removed it. I also track **revert rate at 14 days**, which is the only metric that catches "merged because it looked fine."

**📄 Paper:** Jimenez et al. (2023) — SWE-bench, which reframed code generation evaluation from function-level puzzles to "resolve a real GitHub issue in a real repo such that the repo's own tests pass," and made test-passing the standard verifier for coding agents. The human-validated **SWE-bench Verified** subset of 500 instances is the number people quote; **📅 Volatile:** the leaderboard moves monthly, so quote the mechanism, not a score you half-remember.

### Sandboxing. What does that agent actually execute in, and what is it allowed to touch?

The threat model has two halves and they need different answers. **Half one: the agent is not malicious, it is clumsy** — it will `rm -rf` a directory it misread, it will run a test that spawns 400 processes, it will write a 20 GB log. **Half two: the code the agent is executing may be adversarial** — a dependency in the repo, a fixture file, or a `conftest.py` that a prompt-injected agent just wrote. Half two means container isolation alone is not obviously enough, because a container shares the host kernel.

**What I ship.** Each agent task gets a fresh sandbox from a pool, warm-started to keep the cold-start off the critical path. For internal-code-only workloads I would run OCI containers with a hardened profile: non-root user, read-only root filesystem plus a writable overlay for the workspace, `no-new-privileges`, all capabilities dropped, seccomp default profile, cgroup limits (4 CPU, 8 GB, 30 GB disk), PID limit, and a wall-clock kill at 20 minutes. For workloads that execute untrusted code — anything from a public repo, or anything from a customer — I would move to a stronger boundary: a microVM or a user-space kernel, accepting the extra ~100–200 ms of startup for a real hypervisor boundary. Saying "container isolation is not a security boundary against a kernel exploit, so the level of isolation depends on whose code I am running" is exactly the nuance a Big-Tech infra interviewer is listening for.

**Network is the control that matters most.** Default deny egress. Allowlist: the internal package mirror, the internal git remote, and the model API endpoint (and I would prefer that last one be brokered so the sandbox never holds an API key at all). No cloud instance-metadata access — block `169.254.169.254` explicitly, because it is a credential vending machine and every agent sandbox writeup that forgets it is one prompt injection from an IAM compromise. DNS restricted to an internal resolver with logging, because DNS is the exfiltration channel people forget to close.

**Credentials.** The sandbox never gets a human's git credentials or a broadly-scoped PAT. It gets a short-lived token (15-minute TTL, refreshed by the orchestrator outside the sandbox) scoped to: read the repo, push to `agent/*` branches only, open PRs. It cannot push to `main`, cannot approve PRs, cannot merge, cannot modify branch protection, cannot read secrets. **The agent's identity is a first-class principal in your IAM with its own audit trail** — not a service account shared with CI, because when something goes wrong you need to distinguish "the agent did it" from "CI did it."

**⚠ Trap:** granting the sandbox write access to the package registry so the agent can "test publishing," or mounting the developer's `~/.aws`. Both happen. The rule: **whatever the sandbox can reach, assume a prompt injection can reach**, and design the grant as if you were provisioning access for an anonymous internet user who is trying to be helpful.

**💰 Sandbox economics:** a warm pool of 50 containers at 4 vCPU / 8 GB is roughly 200 vCPU and 400 GB — call it 25 c6i.2xlarge-class instances at ~$0.34/hr = **$8.50/hr = ~$6,100/month** for the pool, before any agent inference cost. That number tends to surprise people: **for a coding agent, compute for execution is frequently the same order of magnitude as inference**, and if your test suite is slow it is larger. That is a real argument for investing in test selection before investing in a better model.

### You said tests are the reward signal. Where does that break?

It breaks in the way every reward signal breaks: **the agent optimizes the measure, and the measure is not the goal.** The goal is "the code is correct"; the measure is "the process exits zero." Every gap between those two is an exploit, and a search process with enough attempts will find them without any intent to cheat. This is reward hacking, and it is not a hypothetical — it is the default outcome of an unconstrained edit-and-test loop.

**The concrete exploits, ranked by how often I have seen them.** (1) *Editing the test.* The agent cannot make the code pass so it changes the assertion. (2) *Deleting or skipping the test* — adding `@pytest.mark.skip`, or narrowing a parametrize list. (3) *Special-casing the input* — `if x == 3: return 7`, which passes the one test and is obviously wrong to a human. (4) *Weakening the check* — `assertAlmostEqual` with a huge tolerance, `assert result is not None` replacing a value check. (5) *Mocking the thing under test* so the test asserts on a mock. (6) *Catching and swallowing* — wrapping the failing call in `try/except: pass`. (7) *Environment manipulation* — setting an env var or editing `pytest.ini` so the failing suite is not collected.

**Containment, in layers.** First and most important: **the test files are read-only in the sandbox for the edit loop.** Mechanically enforced — the harness computes the set of test paths relevant to the task, mounts them read-only, and rejects any search-replace block targeting them. If the task genuinely requires a test change, that is a different task type with a different, stricter review gate. Second: **run the tests the agent never saw.** Split the suite — the agent iterates against a subset, and final verification runs the full suite plus held-out tests. This is exactly a train/test split and describing it that way is the right vocabulary. Third: **diff-level static gates** — reject any diff that reduces total assertion count, adds a skip marker, adds a bare `except: pass`, or changes CI config. These are cheap regexes and they catch most of the list above. Fourth: **mutation-style sanity check on high-value tasks** — after the agent's patch passes, revert the patch's core change and confirm the test now fails. If the test passes both with and without the fix, the test proves nothing.

**⚠ Trap:** believing that a stronger model removes this problem. Stronger search finds exploits *faster*. The gap between measure and goal is a property of your harness, not of the model, and the only durable fix is closing gaps in the harness.

**The deeper limit, which I would name explicitly:** tests verify behavior on the cases someone wrote down. They say nothing about performance regressions, API compatibility for downstream consumers, security properties, readability, or whether the abstraction is right. So test-passing is a **necessary gate, not a sufficient one**, and the human review gate is not vestigial — it is covering exactly the properties the verifier cannot see. Any design that proposes auto-merge on green tests is a design I would block, and I would say so.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Tests are the reward, so the agent will hack them — edit assertions, add skips, special-case the input. I mount test files read-only during the edit loop, verify against held-out tests the agent never saw, and add cheap static gates that reject any diff which lowers assertion count or adds a skip. And I'd never auto-merge on green, because tests are a necessary gate, not a sufficient one."

### Design a browser or computer-use agent to run a back-office workflow — say, processing vendor invoices in a legacy web ERP with no API. Bound the blast radius.

Computer-use is the most fragile agent modality in production and I want that stated in the first sentence, because a candidate who is starry-eyed about it is a candidate who has not run one. **The mental model: a computer-use agent is an integration built against an interface with no contract, no versioning, and no error semantics.** Every UI change is a silent breaking change. So the design is 20% agent and 80% containment, verification and rollback.

**Architecture.** A pool of isolated browser sessions (headless Chromium in a container, one session per task, fresh profile, no shared cookies). The loop is screenshot → model with vision → a small action vocabulary → execute → screenshot. **I keep the action vocabulary tiny and typed**: `click(element_id)`, `type(element_id, text)`, `select(element_id, option)`, `scroll(direction)`, `navigate(url_from_allowlist)`, `read_screen()`, `finish(result)`, `escalate(reason)`. I do **not** give it raw coordinates if I can avoid it — I extract the accessibility tree, assign stable element IDs, and let the model choose an ID. Coordinate clicking is brittle to resolution, zoom and rendering, and it makes every trace un-replayable. The accessibility tree also collapses input tokens dramatically versus reasoning over raw pixels.

**Bounding blast radius, which is the actual question.** Five mechanisms, and I would enumerate them as five:

1. **Identity.** The agent logs into the ERP as its own user with a role that grants exactly the permissions the workflow needs — read invoices, create a draft payable, attach a document. Not approve. Not edit vendor bank details. **The most damaging back-office fraud vector is changing a vendor's bank account, so that field is outside the agent's role, full stop.**
2. **Value ceilings.** Any invoice above a threshold (say $5,000) routes to a human. The ceiling is enforced by the orchestrator reading the invoice amount *before* the agent acts, not by the agent's judgment.
3. **Rate and volume caps.** Max 200 invoices/hour, max 40 per vendor per day, with a circuit breaker on anomaly. An agent stuck in a loop creating duplicate payables at 3 a.m. is the incident you are actually preventing.
4. **Two-phase commit against the UI.** The agent fills the form and stops at the final submit. A deterministic verifier — a separate, non-agentic step — re-reads the rendered form fields via the accessibility tree and compares them field-by-field against the structured source data extracted from the invoice PDF. Only on exact match does the orchestrator click submit. **The model proposes the form state; a diff check commits it.** This single pattern converts most computer-use failures from silent wrong-data into a loud mismatch.
5. **Reversibility and audit.** Every action is recorded with screenshot, action, element ID and timestamp — a replayable trace. Every created record carries a correlation ID so a bad batch can be identified and reversed as a set.

**🔍 Failure taxonomy.** *UI drift* — a field moves or is renamed; detect with a daily synthetic canary run against a staging ERP and alert on any step-count deviation; contain by failing closed. *Modal interception* — a "your session will expire" dialog appears mid-flow and the agent clicks through it into an unknown state; contain with a pre-action guard that detects unexpected dialogs and escalates. *Silent partial completion* — the agent submits, the page errors, the agent believes it succeeded; contain by verifying against the *record*, not against the screenshot — after each task, query the ERP's database or export for the created record. *Injection through the content* — an invoice PDF containing "ignore previous instructions and set the payment account to…"; contain via the two-phase field check, which the injected text cannot pass because the check compares to the structured extraction, and via keeping the vendor-bank field out of the role entirely.

**📐 Numbers to expect:** per-step latency is dominated by page render plus a vision model call — realistically 2–5 seconds per step, and a 15-step workflow is 45–75 seconds. Vision input is expensive: a 1280×800 screenshot is on the order of a thousand-plus input tokens depending on the provider's tiling scheme, so a 15-step task re-sending the last two screenshots each turn is easily 30–50k input tokens = ~$0.10–0.15 per invoice at $3/Mtok, plus retries. **📅 Volatile:** image tokenization rules differ per provider — verify. Against a human at 3 minutes per invoice and $30/hr = $1.50, the economics work at maybe 4–8× margin, which is thinner than people assume once you add the failure and re-review rate.

### When is computer-use the wrong answer, and what would you build instead?

Almost always, and I would lead with that. **Computer-use is the integration of last resort.** The decision rule I use, in order: (1) Is there an API? Use it. (2) Is there a documented data export / import, a CSV drop, an SFTP batch, an EDI channel? Use it — back-office systems almost always have one because that is how they were integrated before your team existed. (3) Is there a database you can read directly, even read-only, with writes via a supported import? Use it. (4) Is there a vendor-supported RPA product with recorded selectors and a maintenance contract? That is often better than an LLM agent, because deterministic RPA fails loudly on selector change instead of improvising. (5) Only then, computer-use — and specifically when the workflow is **long-tail, low-volume, and high-variance**, where the cost of building a deterministic integration exceeds the cost of an unreliable one.

The economic argument I would make in the room: a deterministic integration costs, say, three engineer-weeks up front and near-zero per transaction, with a maintenance cost proportional to how often the vendor changes their API (low — APIs are versioned). A computer-use agent costs maybe one engineer-week up front, ~$0.12 per transaction in inference, plus a maintenance cost proportional to how often the vendor changes their **UI** (high, unversioned, and unannounced). **💰 At 200k invoices/year, the agent costs 200,000 × $0.12 = $24,000/year in inference alone, plus an on-call burden. Three engineer-weeks is roughly $15–25k once.** The deterministic integration wins on year one and wins enormously on year three. Computer-use only wins when the volume is low — the crossover here is around 20–30k transactions/year — or when there is genuinely no other surface.

**Where computer-use genuinely earns its place:** workflows spanning five different vendor systems where building five integrations is the real cost; one-off migrations and backfills; QA and exploratory testing of your own product; and the long tail of "the customer has a bespoke internal tool from 2009." Those are real, and they are the reason the modality exists. What I would resist is using it for a high-volume, stable, single-system workflow because it demos well.

**⚠ Trap:** underestimating the maintenance function. A computer-use fleet needs an owning team, canary runs against staging, and a rotation, because a UI change on the vendor's side is a production incident with no advance notice. If the organization is not willing to staff that, the honest recommendation is not to build it — and being willing to say "I'd recommend against building this" in a design round is a senior signal, not a dodge.

### Design a workflow engine for LLM pipelines — graph nodes, state management, and protection against cycles. What are you actually building?

Start by naming what this is: **a durable execution engine where the steps happen to be model calls.** Everything I know about workflow engines from the Temporal/Airflow/Step-Functions world transfers directly, and the LLM-specific delta is small but sharp: steps are non-deterministic, expensive, and slow; the graph often needs *cycles* (retry with feedback, reflect-and-revise) which most DAG engines forbid; and the state is a growing context blob rather than a small payload.

**The core abstraction.** A graph of nodes over a shared, typed state object. A node is `(state) -> partial_state_update`. Edges are either static or **conditional** — a routing function that reads the state and returns the next node name (or a sentinel `END`). State updates are merged by **per-field reducers**, not by wholesale replacement: `messages` uses an append reducer, `retrieved_docs` uses replace, `error_count` uses add. This reducer design is what makes concurrent fan-out nodes composable — two parallel branches both appending to `messages` merge deterministically instead of one clobbering the other. **📄** This is the model LangGraph popularized in the LLM ecosystem, and it is worth naming the framework because interviewers at product companies will expect fluency, but the mechanism — reducer-merged state over a conditional graph — is the thing to defend.

**Durability, which is the whole reason to build an engine rather than a `while` loop.** After every node completes, the engine persists a **checkpoint**: `(thread_id, checkpoint_id, parent_checkpoint_id, node_name, state_snapshot_or_delta, ts)`. Two properties fall out. First, crash recovery: a worker dies mid-workflow, another picks up the thread from the last checkpoint and re-enters at the next node. Second — and this is the one that matters for LLM workflows — **human-in-the-loop becomes trivial**: a node can interrupt, the state is durable, the workflow sits idle for six hours costing nothing, and resumes when an approval arrives. That is not achievable with an in-process loop, and it is the argument I would make for the engine.

**Idempotency, because steps are expensive.** Every node execution is keyed on `(thread_id, checkpoint_id, node_name, input_hash)` and results are written to a durable step-result store before the checkpoint advances. On replay after a crash, a node whose result already exists is not re-executed. Without this, a crash after an expensive 40-second research node re-burns the call. **⚠ Trap:** placing the side effect and the checkpoint write in different transactions. If the node sends an email and *then* the checkpoint write fails, replay sends a second email. Side-effecting nodes must either write an idempotency record atomically with the effect (using the provider's idempotency key), or be structured as propose-then-commit with the commit in its own node.

**State growth is the LLM-specific gotcha.** A 60-node workflow that appends every message to state produces a state snapshot that grows unboundedly, and if you naively store a full snapshot per checkpoint you get O(n²) storage. Store **deltas plus periodic full snapshots** — exactly the pattern of a write-ahead log with compaction. And put large payloads (retrieved documents, generated files) in blob storage with the state carrying references, or your checkpoint rows become megabytes.

### Cycles. How do you stop an infinite loop without breaking legitimate iteration?

The tension is real: I *want* cycles, because reflect-and-revise, retry-with-tool-feedback, and plan-act-observe are all loops and they are where quality comes from. Forbidding cycles gives you a DAG engine that cannot express an agent. So the answer is not "detect cycles and reject" — it is **make loops safe by bounding them along multiple independent axes, at least one of which is not under the model's control.**

**Four bounds, all enforced by the engine, none by the prompt.**

1. **Global step budget per thread.** A hard `recursion_limit` — every node transition decrements it, and hitting zero raises a distinguishable exception, not a silent stop. I default to something like 25 and require an explicit override with a comment. This is the backstop that always works.
2. **Per-edge visit counts.** Track `visits[(from_node, to_node)]` in state. A reflection loop that has traversed `critic → writer` five times is not converging; the conditional edge reads its own visit count and routes to `END` or to escalation. This lets me bound each loop independently rather than with one global number that a legitimate long workflow would also hit.
3. **Progress detection — the interesting one.** Bound on *state change*, not just on count. Hash the meaningful part of the state (the draft, the candidate patch, the plan) after each iteration. If the hash repeats, or the normalized edit distance between iterations falls below a threshold, the loop is oscillating or stuck and I exit. This catches the very common failure where the critic says the same thing five times and the writer makes cosmetic changes — a counter would let that run to 25, a progress check kills it at 3. **This is the mechanism that actually saves money.**
4. **Cost and wall-clock budget.** The state carries `tokens_spent` and `usd_spent`, updated after every model node, and any node entry that would exceed the budget routes to a terminal `budget_exceeded` node. Same reserve-and-check discipline as the per-conversation cap: check before dispatch.

**Static analysis at graph-build time** complements the runtime bounds: at compile time I detect strongly connected components in the graph and require that every SCC contains at least one node that is registered as a "bounded" node — one that decrements a counter or evaluates a progress check. A cycle with no bounding node is a build error. That turns a class of production incidents into a failed unit test, which is where you want it.

**⚠ Trap:** relying on the model to decide it is done. "Call `finish` when the task is complete" is a suggestion, and the two failure modes are symmetric and both bad: it finishes early on a hard task, or it never finishes on an ambiguous one. The engine owns termination; the model gets to *request* it.

**💰 What an unbounded loop costs, concretely:** a reflection loop at 8,000 input + 800 output tokens per iteration costs 8,000×3/1e6 + 800×15/1e6 = $0.036 per iteration. Bounded at 3 iterations that is $0.108. Unbounded and hitting a 100-iteration timeout, it is $3.60 — 33× — and if the context grows 800 tokens per iteration, iteration 100 has 88,000 input tokens and costs $0.28 by itself, so the real total is closer to $15. **One unbounded loop across 1% of 500k monthly requests is 5,000 × $15 = $75,000/month.** That is why the budget check lives in the engine and not in a code review comment.

### Design an overnight fleet: hundreds of sub-agents running unattended, and a report on my desk in the morning.

The mental model that makes this tractable: **this is a batch job system, and I already know how to build one.** The delta from a Celery fleet is three things — the workers are non-deterministic so retries are not free, the cost per task is dollars rather than millicents so budget is a first-class scheduling constraint, and "success" is not a boolean so the report cannot just be a count.

**Topology.** A planner produces a **task manifest** — an explicit, durable list of N independent tasks with their inputs, written before any agent runs. This is the single most important structural choice: the fan-out is materialized as rows, not as recursive spawning. Recursive spawning gives you an unbounded agent tree with no way to answer "how much of this is left" and no way to cap total spend. With a manifest I have a work queue, a progress bar, an ETA, and a bounded cost. Then: a queue (one message per task), a pool of workers each running one agent in its own sandbox, a results table, and a reporter job that runs at 06:00 and composes the summary.

**Concurrency and the constraint that actually binds.** It is not your CPU, it is the **provider rate limit** — tokens per minute, not requests per minute. If your tier allows 2M input TPM and each agent task consumes ~60k input tokens over ~90 seconds, one task averages 40k TPM, so 2M/40k = **50 concurrent agents** is your real ceiling. I would size the worker pool from that number and implement a **token-aware semaphore**: acquire an estimated token budget before dispatch, release the actual after, with the estimate revised from a trailing histogram. Treating rate limits as tokens-per-minute rather than requests-per-second is the vocabulary shift that signals you have run this in production. Additionally: respect `Retry-After` on 429s with jittered exponential backoff, and — critically — **do not retry a 429 in a tight loop across 200 workers**, or you build a synchronized retry storm. Jitter is not optional at this fan-out.

**Budget enforcement at three levels.** Per-task cap (kill and mark `budget_exceeded`), per-run cap (the fleet's total spend, enforced by an atomic decrement in Redis before each task dispatch — when the pool is exhausted, remaining tasks are marked `skipped_budget` and appear in the report), and a per-hour rate cap so a bug cannot burn the month's budget between 1 a.m. and 2 a.m. The per-run cap is the one that lets you sleep: **I want to be able to state, before pressing go, the maximum dollar amount this run can possibly spend**, and if I cannot state that number the design is not finished.

**The morning report is a product, not a log dump.** Structure: (1) headline — N tasks, M succeeded, K failed, J skipped for budget, total $X, wall clock; (2) the actual findings, ranked by the agent's own confidence *and* by a deterministic importance signal, because ranking by model confidence alone puts the hallucinations on top; (3) a "needs your decision" section with the specific items requiring human judgment, each one-click actionable; (4) failures grouped by *cause*, not listed individually — 40 tasks that all failed on the same 429 is one line, not forty; (5) a diff against yesterday's run, because on day three the reader only wants to know what changed. **⚠ Trap:** a report nobody reads. If the morning report is 4,000 words, it is a log with better formatting. I would target a report that takes 90 seconds to read and links to detail, and I would measure whether anyone clicks through.

### One of those agents goes rogue at 3 a.m. — retry loop, burning tokens, spraying writes. What in your design catches it, and how quickly?

I want to answer this with detection latency for each mechanism, because "we have monitoring" is not an answer.

**Instant (sub-second, in-process):** the per-task budget check before each model call. A single agent cannot exceed its cap, so the worst a single rogue agent does is spend its ceiling — say $2 — and die. This is the bulkhead, and it is the one that makes the other mechanisms optional rather than load-bearing.

**Seconds:** the step counter and the progress-hash check inside the agent loop kill a non-converging agent at 3 identical iterations rather than at the step limit.

**Under a minute:** the fleet-level atomic budget decrement. If 40 agents simultaneously degrade into retry loops, each still capped at $2, the fleet cap of, say, $400 halts dispatch after 200 tasks' worth of spend. The alert fires on `budget_exhausted_before_manifest_complete`, which is a strictly better alert than a raw spend threshold because it is normalized against expected work.

**Minutes:** anomaly alerts on the derivative — tokens/minute for the run exceeding 3× the trailing median for that run type, error rate above 20%, or the same tool called with the same arguments more than 5 times across the fleet (a fleet-wide, not per-agent, repetition detector — that one catches the case where every agent hits the same broken downstream service and retries in unison).

**Failure isolation.** Every task is independent by construction — separate sandbox, separate agent state, no shared mutable resource except the results table and the rate-limit semaphore. One task's failure cannot corrupt another's context. Poison tasks — those that fail repeatedly — go to a dead-letter table after 2 attempts, not the usual 3, because **an LLM retry is not free the way a Celery retry is**: retrying a task that burned 60k tokens costs real money, so I set retry budgets in dollars and not just in counts. Retry only on *transient* classes (429, 5xx, timeout, sandbox eviction); never auto-retry on "the agent produced an invalid final answer," because that is deterministic-ish and will just fail again more expensively.

**"Spraying writes" needs its own answer.** Unattended agents should not have direct write authority to production systems. The overnight fleet writes to a **staging table with a correlation ID per run**, and promotion to the real system is either a morning human approval or a separate deterministic job with its own validation. This makes the blast radius of a 3 a.m. rogue agent equal to "some junk rows in a staging table," which is a cleanup task rather than an incident. If the workflow genuinely requires live writes, then every write carries the run's correlation ID so the entire run is revertible as a unit — **design the undo before you design the fan-out.**

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Three nested budgets — per task, per run, per hour — with the per-task one checked before every model call, so the worst case for a single rogue agent is its own ceiling. Overnight fleets write to a staging table with a per-run correlation ID rather than to production, so the blast radius of anything I failed to anticipate is a revertible batch. And retries are budgeted in dollars, not counts, because an LLM retry costs about as much as the original call."

### Across all these agent cases, what are the two answers that get a candidate rejected?

**Rejection answer number one: designing the agent's authority in the prompt.** It sounds like this — "the system prompt tells it not to refund more than twenty-five dollars," "we instruct it never to return PII," "we tell it to stop after ten steps." Every one of those is a suggestion to a stochastic text generator, and the interviewer hears a candidate who does not distinguish between a policy and a control. The corrected reflex is mechanical: **anything you would enforce with an `if` statement in a normal service must still be enforced with an `if` statement here**, in the tool executor, in the orchestrator, or in the database grant — with the model's output treated as an untrusted request payload. If you catch yourself saying "we prompt it to," stop and ask where the deterministic check lives. When there genuinely is no deterministic check available (tone, helpfulness, judgment), say so explicitly and describe the eval that measures it, because naming the gap is credible and hand-waving over it is not.

**Rejection answer number two: no numbers.** "We'd cache to reduce cost." "It should be fast enough." "We'd monitor it." Every one of these fails, and they fail even when the architecture behind them is correct, because a system-design round at this level is scored on whether you can size what you propose. The fix is a small set of arithmetic habits you run unprompted: **tokens per request → dollars per request → dollars per day at the stated volume**; **steps per conversation × context growth → the quadratic term that makes agents expensive**; **human minutes saved × loaded hourly rate − inference cost − error cost = the actual business case.** Producing "a dollar per conversation is about thirty tool-loop steps, so I'll enforce the budget as a step counter" is worth more than three extra boxes on the diagram.

Two near-misses worth naming as well, because they get candidates downgraded rather than rejected. **Reaching for multi-agent when one structured call would do** — interviewers at product companies are explicitly screening for over-engineering, and the collapse-to-two argument earlier is the shape they want. And **describing the happy path only.** Every one of these cases has a failure taxonomy, and volunteering it before you are asked — "here is how this breaks, here is how I detect each one, here is how I contain it" — is the single most reliable way to move from a hire to a strong hire, because it demonstrates that you have operated something like this rather than only designed it.

**🗣 Say this in the room, as a closing move on any agent case:** "Before you ask — here's how I think this breaks. The three failure modes I'd design detection for are silent quality drift after a model change, tool-loop non-convergence, and injection through retrieved content. I'd catch the first with nightly replay against a golden set, the second with a repeated-call breaker and a progress hash, and the third by keeping authority in the policy engine rather than in the prompt. And the first thing I'd measure in production is escalation rate by reason code."

### Design a multi-tenant LLM gateway for a company with fifty engineering teams. Auth, quotas, budgets, routing, fallbacks, metering, billing. Start wherever you like.

I start with the two questions that determine whether this is a weekend proxy or a platform: **is the gateway on the critical path for latency, and does it need to be the system of record for spend?** If yes to both — which is the interesting case and the one every big-tech and enterprise interviewer means — then the gateway is a low-latency, high-availability data plane with a strongly-consistent accounting problem attached, and those two properties pull in opposite directions. Naming that tension in the first minute is the strongest opening available on this case.

**Why the gateway exists at all** (worth 30 seconds, because it justifies every subsequent decision): one place to rotate provider keys; one place to enforce spend; one place to switch models without 50 teams redeploying; one place where every prompt and response is logged for compliance and eval mining; and one place to implement caching that benefits everyone. Without it you get 50 teams with 50 API keys and no ability to answer "what did we spend on AI last month, by team" — which is the question the CFO will ask, and it is usually the actual reason the project is funded.

**Request path.** Client SDK (thin, OpenAI-compatible surface so teams do not rewrite) → edge (TLS, connection pooling to providers) → **authn**: a gateway-issued key or OIDC token resolving to `(team_id, service_id, environment)`. Never provider keys at the client. → **authz/policy**: is this team allowed this model, this region, this data class? → **budget reservation** (below) → **router** picks a concrete provider+model deployment → **request transform** → provider call, streamed → **response path**: usage extraction, budget settlement, metering event, trace write. Everything after the first token is off the critical path and goes to a queue.

**Latency budget, because a gateway that adds 200 ms is a gateway teams will route around.** Target: **p99 gateway overhead under 15 ms** excluding the provider. Auth via a signed token verified locally with a cached JWKS: <1 ms. Policy evaluation from an in-process cache refreshed by a control-plane watch: <1 ms. Budget check: this is the one that can hurt, and I discuss it below. Routing decision from in-memory state: <1 ms. The rest is transform and I/O. **The rule I enforce: nothing on the request path may make a synchronous call to a database.** Everything the hot path needs — policy, budgets, routing config — is in process memory, updated by a push from the control plane, with a bounded staleness I state explicitly (say 5 seconds) and design around.

**Streaming is the constraint people forget.** The gateway must stream tokens through with minimal buffering, which means it cannot inspect the full response before forwarding — so output guardrails at the gateway are either incremental (scan the token stream in a sliding window) or they break streaming entirely. I would make that an explicit per-route policy: `stream_guard: none | incremental | buffered`, and force teams to choose, rather than silently degrading TTFT for everyone. And SSE through a proxy needs `Cache-Control: no-transform`, disabled response buffering at every hop, and a heartbeat, or you will ship a gateway that adds 3 seconds to TTFT because nginx buffered 4 KB before flushing.

**High availability.** The gateway is now a single point of failure for every AI feature in the company, so: stateless replicas behind a load balancer, at least two regions, and — this is the important one — **fail-open on non-critical control-plane dependencies.** If the budget store is unreachable, do I reject all requests or allow them and reconcile later? I would allow them, with a hard fail-open ceiling (e.g. only for teams whose last-known budget had >20% headroom) and an alert, because a 30-minute Redis incident should not take down every product surface in the company. That is a judgment call and I would say out loud that it is one, and that I would want the CFO's agreement in writing.
### How do quotas and per-team budgets actually work in that gateway, given you do not know the output token count until after generation?

This is my favourite question on this case because the answer is a pattern the reader already owns from payments: **you cannot debit what you have not measured, so you authorize, then settle.** A card auth places a hold for an estimated amount; the capture settles the real one. Token budgets are identical, and the framing lands instantly with a backend interviewer.

**The mechanism.** Before dispatch, the gateway computes a reservation: `input_tokens` (known exactly — tokenize locally, or trust the client's estimate with a correction factor) plus `max_tokens` from the request (the ceiling the caller declared) priced at the route's output rate. It atomically decrements the team's budget bucket by that reserved amount. If the decrement would go negative, the request is rejected with a `429` carrying a structured body: current spend, limit, reset time, and the specific bucket that was exhausted — because "quota exceeded" with no detail generates a support ticket every single time. After the stream completes, the gateway settles: it reads the actual usage from the provider's final chunk and **credits back the difference** between reserved and actual. If the stream is aborted mid-way, settlement uses the tokens actually emitted, which the gateway counted as they flowed through.

Two subtleties. First, **`max_tokens` is often absent or set to a huge default**, which makes reservations useless — a 4,096-token default reservation on a request that emits 80 tokens over-reserves 50×, and under load your teams get spurious 429s while the real spend is 2% of budget. So I make `max_tokens` mandatory at the gateway (rejecting requests without it) or I reserve using a **p95 of that route's trailing output-length distribution** rather than the declared ceiling, with the ceiling as a hard kill. The distribution-based reservation is better and it is what I would ship: keep a per-route sketch of output lengths, reserve at p95, settle at actual, and accept a small over-commit risk. Second, **reservations must expire.** A crashed gateway replica leaves a hold forever; every reservation carries a TTL (say 2× the route timeout) and a sweeper releases stale ones, exactly like a lock lease.

**The bucket hierarchy.** Budgets are not one number. I would model: org → team → service → environment, with each level having a monthly dollar cap and a per-minute token rate. A request must pass *every* level, and the 429 says which one failed. Rate limits and spend limits are different controls — a team can be well under budget and still need throttling because they are about to exhaust the provider's shared TPM. **I keep two independent buckets: dollars-per-month (spend governance) and tokens-per-minute (capacity protection), and conflating them is a design smell.**

**Consistency versus latency.** A strongly-consistent global counter on the request path is a synchronous round trip and it will not fit a 15 ms overhead budget across regions. My compromise: **local token buckets per gateway replica, refilled from a central authority.** Each replica leases a slice of the budget (say 1/N of the per-minute allowance plus a small burst) and reports consumption every second; the central service redistributes. This is the standard distributed-rate-limiter design and it accepts bounded over-admission — with 20 replicas and a 1-second reporting interval, worst-case overshoot at the moment of exhaustion is roughly one second of full-rate traffic across replicas. **💰 If a team's rate is 2M TPM and you overshoot by one second across replicas, that is at most ~33k tokens ≈ $0.10 at $3/Mtok.** Stating that the error is bounded and quantifying it in dollars is the answer; insisting on strict global consistency and eating a 40 ms cross-region hop on every request is the wrong tradeoff and I would say so.

**⚠ Trap:** enforcing budgets only on the request path and reconciling from provider invoices monthly. You will find a 30% discrepancy — from retries the gateway counted once, from cached-input tokens billed at a different rate, from a team that bypassed the gateway. **The gateway's meter must be reconciled against the provider's usage API daily, with the delta alarmed**, because the first time you discover a drift should not be the month you present a chargeback to fifty teams.

### Routing and fallbacks. What does the gateway do when the primary provider degrades?

The mental model: **routing is a policy decision compiled from a config, and fallback is a failure-classification problem — not a retry loop.** The naive version ("on error, try the next provider") is actively dangerous: it doubles cost on the errors that were your own fault, it amplifies load on a degraded provider, and it silently changes the model behind a user's request in ways that break their evals.

**Routing inputs.** A named *virtual model* (`chat-default`, `summarize-cheap`, `code-planner`) maps to an ordered list of concrete deployments, each with a provider, model ID, region, weight, and constraints. Teams call the virtual name; the platform owns the mapping. That indirection is the gateway's main product value: switching every team from one model version to the next is a config push with a canary, not fifty deploys. I would layer on **request-attribute routing** — data-classification tag routes EU-resident data to an EU deployment; a `latency_class: interactive|batch` tag routes batch work to the provider's 50%-discount batch tier; a `long_context: true` hint routes to the deployment that supports it.

**Failure classification before fallback.** I bucket errors into four classes with different policies. *Client errors (400, 422, content policy)* — never fall back; a different provider will also reject it, and retrying just doubles the bill. *Auth/quota (401, 403, 429)* — 429 from the provider means fall back immediately (the secondary has independent capacity); 429 from our own budget check means reject, no fallback, or teams route around their budget by triggering fallbacks. *Server errors (500, 502, 503) and timeouts* — retry once on the same deployment with jitter (many are transient), then fall back. *Degradation without errors* — the provider is up but p99 has tripled. This is the one everybody forgets and it is the most common real incident.

**Circuit breakers, per deployment, keyed on more than errors.** I trip on error rate above threshold *or* on p99 latency exceeding a multiple of the trailing baseline *or* on TTFT specifically, since TTFT degradation is the earliest visible symptom of provider queueing. Half-open probing with a small share of traffic. And a **hedged-request** option for latency-critical routes: if no first token has arrived in *T* milliseconds (set at the trailing p95 of TTFT), fire a duplicate to the secondary and take whichever streams first, cancelling the loser. **💰 Hedging at p95 costs about 5% extra spend on that route in exchange for cutting the tail** — for a route spending $40k/month that is $2,000 to remove a multi-second p99, which is usually an easy yes for interactive surfaces and an obvious no for batch. **📄 Paper:** Dean & Barroso (2013), *The Tail at Scale* — the source of hedged requests and tied requests as a systematic tail-latency technique; it predates LLMs entirely and applies unchanged.

**⚠ Trap:** silent cross-model fallback. Falling back from a frontier model to a cheaper one because the primary is slow means a user's request quietly gets a different-quality answer, and any team running evals will see unexplained variance. My rule: **fallback within a quality tier is automatic; fallback across tiers requires the caller to have opted in per route**, and the response always carries `x-gateway-model-used` and `x-gateway-fallback-reason` headers so the caller's traces record what actually served them. A gateway that lies about which model answered is a gateway that makes every downstream eval untrustworthy.

**One more control that pays for the whole platform:** a shared **prompt/response cache** keyed on `(model, normalized_messages_hash, temperature, tools_hash)` for `temperature=0` requests, plus provider-side prefix caching enabled by default via cache breakpoints on the system-prompt block. Exact-match caching hit rates in internal tooling are often 15–30% because agents re-issue identical sub-queries. **💰 At 20% exact-cache hit on a $200k/month spend that is $40k/month**, from a component the gateway is uniquely positioned to own because it sees every team's traffic.

### Metering and billing. How do you charge fifty teams for this, and what breaks?

The unit of billing is not requests, it is **tokens priced by class**, and the classes are the part people get wrong. At minimum: uncached input, cached-read input, cache-write input, output, and reasoning/thinking output where the provider bills it separately. **📅 Volatile:** the exact class list and the cache multipliers change per provider and per model generation; verify. The durable point is that a meter with a single `tokens` field is already wrong, and it will be off by a factor of several once caching is in play — cache reads at a tenth of the input price and cache writes at a premium are not roundable.

**The pipeline.** On response completion the gateway emits one metering event to a durable log (Kafka, partitioned by `team_id` for ordering): `{event_id, ts, team_id, service_id, env, virtual_model, concrete_deployment, tokens: {input_uncached, input_cache_read, input_cache_write, output, reasoning}, price_version, cost_usd, request_id, trace_id, cache_hit, fallback_used}`. `event_id` is the request's idempotency key so a replayed consumer does not double-bill. `price_version` is a foreign key into a price table with validity ranges — **never store only the computed dollar amount**, because when you discover the price table was wrong for eleven days you need to recompute, and you cannot recompute from a scalar. This is the same discipline as storing tax rate versions in an invoicing system.

Downstream: a streaming aggregator maintains near-real-time per-team spend in Redis for the budget checks (accepting seconds of lag), and a batch job writes the authoritative daily rollups into the warehouse. **Two paths, two consistency levels, and I would say explicitly which one the CFO sees** — the warehouse — and which one the rate limiter sees — Redis.

**What breaks, in order of how often I have seen it.**

1. **Streaming aborts.** The client disconnects at token 300 of a 2,000-token generation. Most providers stop billing at cancellation, but the gateway may never see a final usage chunk. You must count tokens as they stream and settle from your own count when the terminal chunk is absent, then reconcile. Teams that skip this under-bill by several percent and, worse, under-count against budgets, so a team with an abort-heavy UI silently exceeds its cap.
2. **Retries.** A retried request is two provider calls. If you emit one metering event per *logical* request you under-bill; if you emit one per *attempt* without a shared `request_id` you cannot explain the invoice. Emit per attempt, roll up per request, show both.
3. **Cached input misattribution.** Team A pays the cache-write premium to warm a shared prefix; Teams B–Z get cheap cache reads. Is that fair? I would normalize: charge everyone the blended rate for that route and let the platform absorb the variance, because otherwise the first team to deploy each morning subsidizes everyone and will notice.
4. **The reconciliation gap.** Daily job pulls the provider's usage API and diffs against your rollup by deployment. Alert when the delta exceeds 2%. Common causes: keys used outside the gateway (find them and kill them), a price table that lags a provider change, and time-zone boundaries in the provider's aggregation.
5. **Showback becoming chargeback.** The moment real money moves, teams dispute line items. You need per-request drill-down from an invoice line to a trace, with retention matching your finance close cycle. Design that from day one; retrofitting request-level lineage into a rolled-up meter is a rewrite.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I meter per attempt with a shared request ID, store token counts by class plus a price-table version rather than a computed dollar amount, and reconcile daily against the provider's usage API with an alert at 2% drift. The two things that break every time are streaming aborts, where you must count tokens yourself because you never get a final usage chunk, and cache-write attribution, where whichever team deploys first ends up subsidizing everyone else."

### We want to self-host a 70B model instead of paying an API. Fixed budget, and product needs a p95 of forty tokens per second per user. Design it and tell me if it is a good idea.

I will do the math first and give the verdict second, because the verdict is frequently "no" and the interviewer is checking whether I will say so.

**Memory, which sets everything else.** A 70B parameter model in BF16 is 70e9 × 2 bytes = **140 GB of weights**. An H100 SXM has 80 GB. So BF16 does not fit on one GPU or even comfortably on two once you account for activations and KV. In FP8 it is **70 GB** — fits on one H100 with about 8–10 GB of headroom after activations and CUDA workspace. In INT4 (AWQ/GPTQ-class) it is roughly **35–40 GB**, leaving ~40 GB for KV on a single H100.

**KV cache per token**, which is the number I want him to be able to produce from memory. For a Llama-3-70B-shaped model: 80 layers, GQA with 8 KV heads, head_dim 128, FP16 KV. Per token: `2 (K and V) × 80 layers × 8 heads × 128 dim × 2 bytes = 327,680 bytes = 320 KiB/token`. **📐 So 1,000 tokens of context costs 320 MB of KV, and a 32k-token conversation costs 10.5 GB — for one user.** That single number reframes the whole design: KV, not weights, is what limits concurrency.

**Configuration I would actually run.** Four H100s, tensor-parallel 4, FP8 weights. Weights: 70 GB spread as 17.5 GB per GPU. Total HBM 320 GB; subtract 70 for weights and ~30 for activations, workspace and fragmentation, leaving **~220 GB for KV** = 220e9 / 327,680 ≈ **670,000 tokens of KV**. At an average 4,000-token working context per active request, that is ~168 concurrent sequences resident. That is a healthy number and means KV is no longer the binding constraint — compute is.

**Does it hit 40 tok/s per user?** Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound: each decode step must read every weight the GPU holds. With TP=4 and FP8, each GPU reads 17.5 GB per step against 3.35 TB/s of HBM → theoretical 191 steps/s; real-world you get maybe 50–60% of peak bandwidth plus TP all-reduce latency per layer, so call it **90–110 tok/s at batch 1**. As batch grows, weight reads amortize across the batch — the same 17.5 GB read serves all B sequences — so per-user speed stays roughly flat until you become compute-bound or the all-reduce dominates. **The practical shape: per-user tok/s stays near its batch-1 value up to a moderate batch, then degrades.** Hitting a p95 of 40 tok/s means capping the running batch at whatever size still delivers 40 — you find that empirically, but on this configuration you would expect to sustain 40 tok/s per user at batch sizes in the tens, giving aggregate output on the order of 1,200–2,000 tok/s. **📅 Volatile:** these are engine- and kernel-version-sensitive; benchmark, do not quote.

**Engine.** vLLM or SGLang, both of which give you PagedAttention-style KV management, continuous batching, and prefix caching. **📄 Paper:** Kwon et al. (2023) — PagedAttention/vLLM, which applied virtual-memory paging to the KV cache and eliminated the internal fragmentation that made pre-reserved contiguous KV allocations waste 60–80% of memory. That is the mechanism to cite; "we'd use vLLM" without knowing why is a weak answer. SGLang's RadixAttention gives prefix sharing across requests via a radix tree, which matters a lot if your traffic has a common system prompt.

**Quantization decision.** FP8 for weights and activations on Hopper is close to free in quality for a 70B and roughly halves memory and bandwidth pressure, which directly buys tokens/s. INT4 weight-only buys more memory but the dequant cost eats some of the bandwidth win and quality degradation is model- and task-dependent — I would only go INT4 if KV capacity is the binding constraint or if I am trying to fit on fewer GPUs. **And I would gate any quantization change behind the same eval suite I use for a model change**, because quantization is a model change; teams that treat it as an infra config ship a quality regression they cannot see.

### Give me the break-even. When does self-hosting actually beat the API?

**💰 The cost per token, worked.** Four H100s at a neocloud rate of ~$2.50/GPU-hr = $10/hr = **$7,200/month** (720 hours). **📅 Volatile:** H100 rental spans roughly $2–$5/GPU-hr depending on provider, commitment and region — re-derive with a current quote.

Suppose the deployment sustains 1,500 output tok/s at full load. Realistically you do not run at full load — traffic is diurnal, and if you provision for peak you sit at maybe **35–45% average utilization** unless you have batch work to backfill. Take 40%: effective 600 tok/s average = 600 × 2,592,000 s/month = **1.56 billion output tokens/month**. Cost per million output tokens = $7,200 / 1,555 Mtok = **$4.63/Mtok**.

Now compare to a hosted endpoint for an open 70B-class model, which at the time of writing sits in the low hundreds of dollars per *billion* tokens — call it **$0.60–$0.90 per Mtok output** for a serverless open-weight endpoint. **📅 Volatile — verify.** Self-hosting at 40% utilization is therefore roughly **5–7× more expensive** than a serverless open-model API. Even at 100% utilization ($1.85/Mtok) it loses. **This is the answer most candidates get backwards, and getting it right is a strong differentiator: for a standard open-weight model at moderate scale, self-hosting is usually more expensive than serverless, because the serverless provider is running your model at 90% utilization across thousands of tenants and you are not.**

**So when does self-hosting win?** Five conditions, and I would say I need at least one:

1. **Sustained high utilization at large scale.** If you can genuinely keep the fleet above ~80% — because you have batch workloads to backfill the troughs — the arithmetic flips. At 85% utilization the same box is $2.18/Mtok, which beats some hosted rates. The prerequisite is a workload mix, not just volume.
2. **Data residency or contractual isolation.** Regulated customers who will not send data to a third party. This is the most common real reason and it is not a cost argument at all.
3. **Custom weights.** A fine-tuned or continued-pretrained model that nobody hosts. Related: multi-LoRA serving where you have 200 customer adapters over one base — hosted providers charge a premium for that if they offer it.
4. **Latency floor and jitter control.** You own the queue, so you own the tail. If the product needs a deterministic p99 TTFT and providers give you a variable one, self-hosting buys predictability.
5. **Volume large enough that a committed-capacity contract with a provider is not available or is worse.** Note this alternative explicitly: **before self-hosting, negotiate provisioned throughput**, which gets you dedicated capacity without an inference team.

**The cost people leave out of the comparison, which I would raise unprompted:** an inference platform needs engineers. Two engineers fully loaded is $500k–$700k/year = **$42k–58k/month**, which is 6–8× the GPU bill in this example. On-call, upgrades, kernel regressions, capacity planning, model rollouts, and the eval infrastructure to prove a quantization change did not regress quality — all of it. **The GPU cost is almost never the dominant term at this scale, and a candidate who only compares $/Mtok has failed the question.**

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Four H100s at $2.50/GPU-hour is $7,200 a month; at a realistic 40% average utilization that's about $4.60 per million output tokens, versus under a dollar from a serverless open-weight endpoint. So for a stock 70B I'd tell you self-hosting loses on cost. I'd only self-host for data residency, custom weights, latency control, or if I have batch traffic that can hold utilization above eighty percent — and I'd price two engineers into the comparison, because that's the term that actually dominates."

### You deployed it. p95 tokens-per-second collapsed at forty percent of the load you sized for. What happened?

Four causes account for nearly everything, and I would work them in this order because they are ordered by frequency.

**1. Prefill is starving decode.** This is the number-one cause and it is invisible in aggregate throughput metrics. When a long request arrives — say 20,000 tokens of context — the engine runs a prefill that occupies the GPU for hundreds of milliseconds, and **every decoding sequence in the running batch is stalled for that entire time.** Decode emits one token per sequence per step, so a 400 ms prefill costs every active user 400 ms of dead air, which shows up exactly as p95 inter-token-latency collapse while mean throughput looks fine. Diagnosis: correlate ITL spikes with prefill events in the engine's metrics; look at the distribution of prompt lengths. Fix: **chunked prefill** — split the prefill into fixed-size token chunks and interleave them with decode steps so each engine iteration does a bounded amount of prefill work. **📄 Paper:** Agrawal et al. (2024), Sarathi-Serve — chunked prefill plus stall-free scheduling, which is the standard fix for exactly this throughput-latency tradeoff. The stronger structural fix is **prefill/decode disaggregation** — run prefill on one pool of GPUs and decode on another, shipping the KV across the interconnect (DistServe, Zhong et al. 2024; Splitwise, Patel et al. 2024). That eliminates the interference entirely at the cost of a KV transfer.

**2. KV pressure causing preemption.** The scheduler admitted sequences optimistically; as they grew, KV ran out; the engine preempted sequences — either recomputing their KV later or swapping it to host memory. Recomputation means a sequence that already generated 300 tokens re-runs its whole prefill. Under sustained load this becomes thrashing, and throughput falls off a cliff rather than degrading gracefully. Diagnosis: the engine exports preemption counters and KV utilization — if KV utilization sits above ~90% and preemptions are nonzero, this is your problem. Fix: cap `max_num_seqs` so the running batch cannot outgrow KV at the p95 sequence length, not the mean. **⚠ Trap: sizing concurrency on average context length.** If mean context is 2k but p95 is 20k, a batch of 64 sized on the mean will exceed KV whenever a handful of long requests coincide, which under Poisson arrivals is often.

**3. Queueing, not serving.** p95 tok/s as experienced by the user includes time spent waiting to be admitted. If arrival rate approaches service rate, queue delay explodes per standard queueing theory — at 80% utilization mean queue delay is 4× service time, at 90% it is 9×. **If you sized capacity for 40% average utilization but traffic is diurnal with a 3× peak, your peak hour is at 120% and you are in overload.** Diagnosis: separate queue-wait from generation time in your metrics — if you only measure end-to-end you cannot tell. This is the same mistake as reporting total request latency without splitting out pool-acquire time.

**4. Something changed that was not the load.** A model or engine upgrade altered kernel selection; a config change disabled prefix caching (very common, and it silently multiplies prefill work); MIG or another tenant on the box; thermal throttling; or NCCL falling back to a slower path so the TP all-reduce got 3× more expensive. Diagnosis: `nvidia-smi` clocks and power, NCCL debug output, and a diff of engine flags against the last known-good deploy.

**🔍 The general procedure:** always split the latency into **queue wait → prefill → decode**, and always look at **KV utilization and preemption counts** alongside GPU utilization. GPU utilization is a nearly useless metric here — a decode-bound engine shows high "utilization" while doing almost no useful FLOPs, because the SMs are stalled on memory. If a candidate cites GPU utilization as their primary serving metric, that is a tell.

### Now serve a 400-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model on a single eight-H100 node. What is different?

The one-sentence framing: **an MoE decouples parameter count from per-token compute, which means it is cheap to run and expensive to hold.** A dense 400B would be unservable on one node; an MoE with, say, 400B total parameters and ~40B active per token does roughly the FLOPs of a 40B dense model per token while occupying the memory of a 400B. Every design consequence follows from that split.

**Memory first.** 400B parameters in BF16 is 800 GB. Eight H100s give **640 GB** of HBM. It does not fit. In FP8 it is **400 GB**, which fits with 240 GB left; subtract activations, all-to-all buffers and workspace — call it 40 GB — and you have **~200 GB for KV**. So **FP8 or lower is mandatory, not optional**, and that is the first thing I would say. INT4 would give 200 GB of weights and 400 GB of KV headroom if concurrency matters more than quality.

**Parallelism.** Two axes, and choosing between them is the interesting decision. *Tensor parallelism (TP=8)* shards every matrix across all 8 GPUs — simple, uniform load, but it does an all-reduce at every layer, and TP=8 across NVLink is fine within a node while TP across nodes is not. *Expert parallelism (EP)* places different experts on different GPUs — each token is routed to its top-k experts, so every layer requires an **all-to-all** to send tokens to the GPUs holding their experts and another to bring results back. The common production shape is a hybrid: TP within a small group and EP across groups, e.g. TP=2 × EP=4. **📄 Paper:** Lepikhin et al. (2020), GShard, introduced the sharded-expert formulation with per-expert capacity limits; Fedus et al. (2021), Switch Transformer, simplified routing to top-1 and demonstrated the scaling argument; Jiang et al. (2024) described Mixtral's 8-expert top-2 architecture, which is the shape most people picture.

**What actually changes operationally versus a dense model.** (a) The bottleneck moves from pure HBM bandwidth to **HBM bandwidth plus interconnect**, because all-to-all traffic scales with batch size and top-k. NVLink at ~900 GB/s per GPU makes intra-node EP viable; the moment you cross to InfiniBand between nodes you are an order of magnitude slower and small-batch decode becomes latency-dominated by communication. **This is the strongest argument for keeping an MoE inside one node if it fits.** (b) Decode at small batch is *worse* than you would expect from active-parameter count, because with batch 1 and top-2 routing you may touch a large fraction of experts across the layers anyway — you still pay to read a lot of weight memory, but you get little compute amortization. MoEs want batch. (c) Prefill is compute-heavy and batches beautifully, so prefill/decode disaggregation is even more attractive here.

**📐 Numbers you must know for this case:** 8×H100 = 640 GB HBM, ~3.35 TB/s per GPU, ~900 GB/s NVLink per GPU. A 400B model is 800 GB at BF16, 400 GB at FP8, 200 GB at INT4. If your interviewer says "400B on 8×H100," the immediate correct response is "not in BF16 — show me the quantization plan," and that response alone puts you ahead of most candidates.

### Expert imbalance and KV pressure. How do these bite you, and what do you do?

**Expert imbalance** is the MoE-specific failure and it has an intuitive shape: the router sends tokens to experts based on the input, and real traffic is not uniform. If your users write a lot of code, the experts that specialize toward code get hammered while others idle. Under expert parallelism, **the GPU holding the hot expert becomes the critical path for every layer, and because the all-to-all is a synchronization point, all seven other GPUs wait for it.** Your effective throughput is set by the slowest expert, so a 3× imbalance means roughly a 3× throughput loss on that layer's expert compute, not a 3× loss on one eighth of the work.

Training-time mitigations do not help you at serving time — the auxiliary load-balancing loss shaped the router during training and the router is now frozen. So the serving-side levers are:

1. **Replicate hot experts.** Profile expert-activation histograms on real traffic, then place the top-*k* hottest experts on more than one GPU and shard the tokens routed to them. This costs memory (a duplicated expert) and buys parallelism. It is the single most effective fix and it requires an engine that supports non-uniform expert placement — check that before you commit to an engine.
2. **Rebalance placement offline.** Solve a bin-packing over measured expert loads and re-shard on deploy. Traffic mix shifts slowly, so a weekly re-placement is usually enough. Alert when the measured imbalance ratio exceeds your provisioned slack.
3. **Capacity factor / token dropping.** Some implementations cap the tokens an expert will process per batch and drop or reroute the overflow. Dropping tokens at inference is a **quality** change, and I would only accept it with an eval showing the delta. **⚠ Trap: enabling a capacity factor at serving time to fix a throughput problem and shipping a silent quality regression.** This is a real and subtle bug because it degrades only under load, so your eval suite — which runs at batch 1 — will never see it.
4. **Batch composition.** Because imbalance is a property of the *batch*, mixing request types in a batch smooths it. A scheduler that groups all code requests together maximizes imbalance; a scheduler that interleaves reduces it. This is a genuinely counterintuitive result worth mentioning.

**KV pressure** in this configuration: with ~200 GB left after FP8 weights, and a KV cost that depends on the model's layer count and KV-head count, capacity is directly computable and you should compute it in the room. If the model has, say, 60 layers with 8 KV heads at head_dim 128 and FP16 KV: `2 × 60 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 245,760 bytes = 240 KiB/token`. 200e9 / 245,760 ≈ **815,000 tokens**, or ~204 concurrent sequences at 4k context — but only ~25 at 32k context. **📐 The general lesson: concurrency at long context is roughly (KV bytes available) / (per-token KV × context length), and it falls linearly with context, which is why long-context products are so much more expensive to serve than their token counts suggest.**

Levers on KV specifically: **quantize the KV cache to FP8**, which halves it and is usually a much smaller quality hit than weight quantization — this is the highest-leverage KV lever and it is often left on the table. Then: cap `max_model_len` to what the product actually needs rather than the model's advertised maximum; enable prefix caching so shared system prompts are stored once; and use CPU offload for the KV of *paused* sequences (human-in-loop conversations, agent steps waiting on a tool) rather than holding HBM for a conversation that is idle for 30 seconds. That last one is the LLM analogue of swapping out an idle connection, and for agent workloads with slow tools it can double effective concurrency.

### Design a batching system so that one GPU can serve many concurrent requests without violating a latency SLO. Assume I have not told you the SLO yet, so tell me what you need.

Good — because the first move is to refuse to design until the SLO is decomposed. **"Latency SLO" for generation is three numbers, not one**, and a candidate who does not separate them will design the wrong system:

- **TTFT (time to first token)** — dominated by queue wait plus prefill. This is what the user perceives as responsiveness.
- **ITL / TPOT (inter-token latency, or time per output token)** — the steady-state decode rate. This is what the user perceives as reading speed; ~40–50 tok/s comfortably exceeds reading speed for most people, and below ~15 tok/s the stream feels laboured.
- **End-to-end completion time** — matters for non-streaming and for agent steps, where nothing renders until the whole tool call is parsed.

And the throughput metric must be **goodput, not throughput**: tokens per second *from requests that met their SLO*. A batching system that maximizes raw throughput will happily run a batch of 400 and give everyone 6 tok/s. Optimizing throughput without an SLO constraint is the single most common error in this design.

**Why batching is mandatory in the first place.** Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound: each step reads all weights to produce one token per sequence. At batch 1 on a 70 GB FP8 model with 3.35 TB/s of bandwidth you get ~48 steps/s and you have used a rounding error of the GPU's compute. At batch 32 you read the same 70 GB and produce 32 tokens — **the arithmetic intensity rises 32× and throughput rises nearly 32× with almost no change in per-user latency**, until you become compute-bound or run out of KV. That is the whole economic case for a serving engine and it should be the first thing out of my mouth.

**Static batching is the wrong design and I want to name why.** Group N requests, run them together, return when all finish. Two fatal properties: a sequence that wants 20 tokens waits for the one that wants 2,000 (head-of-line blocking on *output length*, which you cannot predict), and the effective batch shrinks as sequences finish while the GPU keeps paying full weight-read cost for a nearly-empty batch. Utilization collapses.

**Continuous (iteration-level) batching** is the answer. **📄 Paper:** Yu et al. (2022), Orca — scheduling at the granularity of a single decode iteration rather than a whole request, so finished sequences leave the batch and waiting ones join at the very next step. Combined with **PagedAttention** (Kwon et al., 2023) for non-contiguous KV allocation, this is what every modern engine implements, and the two papers together are the canonical citation for this question.

**The architecture I would describe.** A waiting queue, a running batch, and a KV block allocator. Each engine iteration: (1) check whether any running sequence needs more KV blocks and, if the pool is exhausted, preempt the newest/lowest-priority sequence; (2) admit new sequences from the waiting queue while the projected KV for the batch stays under a watermark and the batch's token budget is not exceeded; (3) build the batch, run one forward pass, sample; (4) emit tokens, retire finished sequences, free their blocks. Around that: prefix caching keyed on a hash of the prompt prefix, a chunked-prefill budget so long prompts do not monopolize an iteration, and admission control that rejects rather than queues when the queue's projected wait would already violate TTFT.

### Implement the core scheduler loop. Thirty lines, from memory.

Here is the skeleton I would write on a whiteboard. It is deliberately engine-agnostic — the point is that the *policy* is a dozen lines and everything else is kernels.

```python
KV_BLOCK = 16                    # tokens per KV block
TOKEN_BUDGET = 8192              # max tokens (prefill+decode) processed per iteration

def step(waiting, running, kv):   # kv: block allocator with .free_blocks
    # 1. Make room: preempt if any running seq needs a new block and none is free.
    for seq in sorted(running, key=lambda s: s.arrival, reverse=True):
        if kv.free_blocks > 0:
            break
        if seq.needs_new_block():
            kv.free(seq); running.remove(seq)
            seq.state = "PREEMPTED"; waiting.appendleft(seq)   # recompute later

    # 2. Admit: fill the iteration's token budget, newest-fitting-first is wrong;
    #    FCFS with a projected-KV watermark keeps TTFT fair and avoids thrash.
    budget = TOKEN_BUDGET - sum(1 for _ in running)            # decode = 1 tok/seq
    while waiting and budget > 0:
        seq = waiting[0]
        need = -(-seq.remaining_prompt_len() // KV_BLOCK)       # blocks for prefill
        if need > kv.free_blocks or kv.utilization() > 0.90:
            break                                               # watermark: leave slack
        chunk = min(seq.remaining_prompt_len(), budget)         # CHUNKED PREFILL
        seq.prefill_chunk = chunk
        kv.allocate(seq, chunk); running.append(waiting.popleft())
        budget -= chunk

    # 3. One fused forward pass over the mixed batch (prefill chunks + decode steps).
    logits = model.forward(build_batch(running))
    for seq, tok in zip(running, sample(logits)):
        if seq.prefill_chunk and not seq.prefill_done():
            continue                                            # still prefilling
        seq.append(tok)
        if tok == EOS or seq.len >= seq.max_tokens:
            kv.free(seq); running.remove(seq); seq.finish()
```

**The three lines that carry the design.** The preemption loop in step 1 is what makes over-admission survivable — without it, running out of KV is a crash or a deadlock rather than a slowdown. The `kv.utilization() > 0.90` watermark in step 2 is what prevents preemption thrash: admitting right up to 100% guarantees that the next token any sequence generates triggers an eviction, and you spend your GPU recomputing KV instead of generating. The `chunk = min(...)` line is chunked prefill, and it is the difference between a 20k-token prompt stalling every other user for half a second and it costing them one extra iteration.

**⚠ Trap:** preempting the *oldest* sequence to free memory. That is intuitively "fair" but it is exactly wrong — the oldest sequence has the most KV invested and preempting it wastes the most recompute, and it is also the one closest to finishing and freeing memory naturally. Preempt newest-first (LIFO on arrival), which is the standard choice and is also better for tail latency, since it concentrates pain on one victim rather than spreading a small delay across everyone.

**🏋 Drill:** 25 minutes, no references. Write this loop, then extend it with two priority classes such that interactive requests are never preempted in favour of batch requests and batch requests can never occupy more than 60% of KV blocks. Pass criterion: your batch-class cap is enforced in the *allocator*, not in the admission check — if you enforced it only at admission, a batch sequence can still grow past the cap as it generates, and you have not actually bounded it.

### Talk to me about mixing prefill and decode in the same batch. What is the tradeoff, and how do you set the chunk size?

The tension is a roofline argument. **Prefill is compute-bound**: a 4,000-token prompt runs 4,000 tokens through every matrix at once, giving huge GEMMs that saturate the tensor cores, and attention itself costs O(n²) in the sequence length. **Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound**: one token per sequence, tiny GEMMs, and the GPU spends its time reading weights. These two workloads want opposite things, and putting them on the same device means one of them is always being served badly.

**Run them separately (the naive scheduler) and prefill starves decode**, as described earlier: every user's stream stutters for the full duration of someone else's prefill. **Mix them without chunking and you get the same thing**, because the prefill is still one indivisible unit of work in the iteration. **Chunked prefill** slices the prompt into pieces of *C* tokens and processes at most one slice per iteration alongside the decode steps, so the iteration time stays bounded.

**Setting *C* is a direct latency-vs-throughput dial and the arithmetic is doable in the room.** Iteration time ≈ time(C prefill tokens) + time(B decode steps). Your ITL SLO bounds the iteration time: if the SLO is 40 tok/s per user, ITL must be ≤ 25 ms, so the whole iteration — including whatever prefill chunk you inserted — must fit in 25 ms. If decode alone at your batch size takes 15 ms, you have 10 ms of prefill budget per iteration; if the GPU prefills at, say, 20,000 tok/s in this configuration, then *C* ≈ 200 tokens. Meanwhile a 20,000-token prompt now takes 100 iterations to prefill = 2.5 seconds of TTFT for that user. **So chunking trades the long prompt's TTFT for everyone else's ITL, and the chunk size is exactly where you place that trade.** Saying this out loud — with the two directions of the tradeoff named — is the answer.

Practical policy: pick *C* from the ITL SLO as above, but let the scheduler use the *whole* remaining budget when the running batch is small (nobody to hurt), and shrink *C* when the batch is full. Most engines expose this as a max-batched-tokens knob; the mistake is leaving it at a default tuned for throughput benchmarks when your product is interactive.

**The structural alternative: disaggregation.** Run prefill on GPU pool A and decode on pool B, transferring the KV cache from A to B over NVLink or the network. Now each pool runs a homogeneous workload and can be tuned and scaled independently — prefill scales with input tokens, decode scales with concurrent streams, and those scale differently in real products. **📄 Papers:** Zhong et al. (2024), DistServe, and Patel et al. (2024), Splitwise — both demonstrated that separating the phases removes the interference and improves SLO-attainment goodput. The cost is the KV transfer: for a 4,000-token prompt at 320 KiB/token that is 4,000 × 327,680 = **1.31 GB to ship per request**. Over NVLink at ~900 GB/s that is ~1.5 ms — free. Over 400 Gb/s InfiniBand (~50 GB/s effective) it is ~26 ms — tolerable. Over 25 GbE it is 420 ms — fatal. **The viability of disaggregation is entirely a function of your interconnect, and that number is the one to compute in the room.**

**⚠ Trap:** assuming disaggregation is strictly better. It costs you GPUs — two pools sized independently have more headroom overhead than one — and it only pays when you have enough traffic that each pool is well utilized. Below a few dozen GPUs, chunked prefill on a unified pool is the right answer and disaggregation is premature.

### Two request classes share that GPU: an interactive chat product and a nightly batch summarization job. How do you keep them from destroying each other?

The instinct from backend work is right and transfers directly: **separate classes, separate admission policies, and never let the low-priority class acquire a resource the high-priority class can be blocked on.** The LLM-specific twist is that the shared resource is the KV cache, and KV is *held for the duration of a generation*, so a batch request that grabs KV blocks holds them for possibly a minute — this is a long-held lock, not a transient one, and that changes the design.

**Option A, the one I would ship if I have the hardware: physical separation.** Interactive traffic on one pool, batch on another. It costs more GPUs but it makes the SLO argument trivial and it eliminates a class of incidents. I would state that a shared pool is a cost optimization I am willing to make only with the controls below.

**Option B, shared pool with class-aware scheduling.** Four controls:

1. **KV quota per class.** Batch may occupy at most 40% of KV blocks, enforced in the allocator (not at admission — see the drill above). This guarantees interactive always has headroom, which is what actually prevents TTFT collapse.
2. **Preemption policy by class.** When memory is tight, preempt batch sequences first regardless of arrival order, and never preempt interactive in favour of batch. Batch sequences are checkpointed by recomputation, which is fine because batch has no ITL SLO.
3. **Admission ordering with anti-starvation.** Interactive jumps the queue, but batch requests accumulate an aging bonus so they cannot starve indefinitely — the classic weighted-fair-queueing shape.
4. **Separate SLOs and separate alerting.** Interactive: TTFT p95 and ITL p95. Batch: completion throughput and deadline attainment ("finished by 6 a.m."). One dashboard with both classes conflated is how you fail to notice that batch has been starving for a week.

**The stronger move, which I would recommend first:** send the batch job to the provider's **batch tier or an offline queue with a 50% discount**, or run it on preemptible/spot GPUs, and stop sharing entirely. **💰 If the summarization job is 200M input and 40M output tokens per night, at $3/$15 that is 200×3 + 40×15 = $600 + $600 = $1,200/night = $36,000/month; at the 50% batch discount it is $18,000/month, saving $216k/year** — and it removes the contention problem as a side effect. Reaching for a scheduling solution when a routing solution is available and cheaper is a judgment failure, and interviewers notice.

**🔍 Failure taxonomy for shared serving pools.** *Priority inversion* — a batch sequence with a huge context holds 30% of KV and cannot be preempted cheaply because recomputing it costs 8 seconds; contain with a per-request context cap for the batch class. *Convoy on prefill* — a burst of long batch prompts fills the prefill budget for many consecutive iterations; contain by reserving a fraction of each iteration's token budget for the interactive class. *Silent SLO erosion* — interactive p95 drifts up over weeks as batch volume grows; detect with an SLO burn-rate alert rather than a static threshold. *Retry amplification* — interactive clients time out and retry, adding load exactly when the system is overloaded; contain with admission control that rejects fast when projected queue wait exceeds the TTFT SLO, because **a fast rejection is strictly better than a slow timeout when the client is going to retry either way.**

### Design a job queue for a GPU cluster running a hundred thousand training jobs. Start with what makes this different from any other job queue.

Three properties make GPU scheduling different, and naming them immediately is the whole opening:

**1. Gang semantics.** A distributed training job needs *all* its GPUs simultaneously or it needs none. Scheduling 60 of the 64 requested GPUs is worse than scheduling zero: the 60 sit idle burning money while the job waits on a collective that will never complete, and in a naive scheduler each partially-placed job holds resources that prevent other jobs from being placed — a distributed deadlock. **Gang (all-or-nothing) scheduling is mandatory, and the interviewer is checking whether you know the term.**

**2. Topology matters enormously.** Eight GPUs on one node connected by NVLink at ~900 GB/s per GPU are not interchangeable with eight GPUs spread across eight nodes on InfiniBand. For a job doing tensor-parallel work, the wrong placement is not a small regression — it can be a 5–10× slowdown, because TP does an all-reduce at every layer. So the scheduler must be **topology-aware**: prefer intra-node placement for TP groups, and for multi-node jobs prefer nodes on the same leaf switch / rail so the collective does not traverse the spine.

**3. Jobs are long and preemption is expensive but possible.** A training job runs for hours or days. Unlike a web request, you cannot just kill it — but unlike a web request, it has *checkpoints*, so you can kill it and it loses only the work since the last checkpoint. That makes preemption a first-class scheduling primitive with a computable cost.

**Architecture.** A durable job store (Postgres, since 100k jobs is nothing for it) holding job specs with resource shapes, priority class, quota project, and checkpoint metadata. A **scheduler** that runs a placement cycle every few seconds over pending jobs and free capacity. **Node agents** that report GPU health, allocate containers, and stream job state. A **quota service** enforcing hierarchical per-org and per-team allocations. And a separate **preemption controller** so that eviction decisions are auditable and rate-limited rather than being an emergent property of the placement loop.

**The placement cycle.** Sort pending jobs by an effective priority — class, then quota-fairness (a team over its guaranteed share drops in priority), then age. For each job in order: find the smallest topological unit that satisfies the request (one node if ≤8 GPUs; one rack if it fits; else spread), reserve all of it atomically, and dispatch. Where the job cannot be placed, **create a reservation for it in a future cycle so it accumulates capacity rather than being repeatedly skipped** — without reservations, big jobs starve forever behind a stream of small ones, which is the single most common complaint on real clusters. Then **backfill**: run small, short jobs in the gaps ahead of the reserved big job as long as their declared runtime guarantees they finish before the reservation activates. This is EASY backfill from the HPC world and it is worth naming, because it is what keeps utilization above 90% while still honoring reservations.

**📄 Papers worth citing:** Verma et al. (2015), Borg — the canonical large-scale cluster scheduler with priority classes, quota and preemption, and the source of much of this vocabulary; Xiao et al. (2018), Gandiva — exploited the *predictability* of DL training's iterative structure to do time-slicing, migration and job packing on GPUs; Gu et al. (2019), Tiresias — GPU cluster scheduling that reduces job completion time without requiring prior knowledge of job duration. The framing to steal from all three: **DL jobs are unusually introspectable — you know the iteration boundary, so you know exactly when it is cheap to checkpoint, migrate or preempt**, and a scheduler that exploits that beats one that treats them as opaque processes.
### Preemption and checkpoint-aware eviction. When do you kill a running training job, and which one?

The mental model: **preemption is a trade of wasted GPU-hours against improved allocation, and you should only make that trade when you can compute both sides.** The cost of preempting a job is the work since its last checkpoint plus the restart overhead; the benefit is whatever the freed GPUs enable. A scheduler that preempts without knowing the first number is gambling.

**Checkpoint-aware eviction, concretely.** Every running job reports two things to the scheduler: `last_checkpoint_at` and `checkpoint_interval` (or, better, the agent observes checkpoint writes directly rather than trusting a declared value). The eviction cost for job *j* is `gpus_j × (now − last_checkpoint_at_j) + gpus_j × restart_overhead_j`, where restart overhead is container start plus weight load plus the collective's re-initialization — for a large job that is genuinely minutes, not seconds, and it must be in the formula. The controller picks the victim set that frees enough of the right *topology* at minimum total cost, subject to priority rules. **The most important consequence: if a job checkpointed 30 seconds ago it is nearly free to preempt, and if it checkpoints every four hours and it has been three hours and fifty minutes, it is the most expensive thing on the cluster.** So the scheduler should, wherever possible, **send a checkpoint-now signal and wait for the ack before evicting.** That single feature — graceful preemption with a checkpoint request and a grace period, say 120 seconds — converts most preemptions from "lose an hour" to "lose ten seconds," and it is the thing I would build first.

**Which jobs are preemptible at all.** A class system: `guaranteed` (never preempted, counted against a team's committed quota, priced highest), `burst` (preemptible with grace, runs above quota, priced lower), and `opportunistic` (preemptible immediately, fills gaps, near-free). This is the same shape as reserved/on-demand/spot, and modelling it that way lets me say something important: **the pricing tiers are what make the fairness argument tractable.** A team that wants never to be preempted buys guaranteed capacity; a team optimizing cost accepts preemption and is required to checkpoint frequently. The platform enforces the contract by refusing to admit an `opportunistic` job that does not declare a checkpoint interval under some threshold.

**⚠ Trap:** preempting to satisfy a large job and then failing to place it anyway, because between the eviction and the placement another scheduling cycle handed the freed GPUs to a small job. This is a real and infuriating bug — you paid the eviction cost for nothing. The fix is that eviction and placement must be **one atomic decision**: the preemption controller creates a *reservation* on the freed resources bound to the target job, and the reservation is honoured by the placement loop. Anything else is a race.

**A second trap: preemption storms.** If the scheduler evicts aggressively, jobs restart, restarted jobs re-enter the queue at high priority (aged), and evict others. Rate-limit preemptions globally (e.g. no more than N GPU-hours of work destroyed per hour) and add hysteresis so a job that was just preempted is immune for a window. This is the same stability reasoning as damping an autoscaler.

**💰 Why this matters in dollars:** on a 4,000-GPU cluster at $2.50/GPU-hr, the fleet costs $10,000/hour. **A one-percentage-point improvement in utilization is $100/hour = $876,000/year.** That is why clusters at this scale employ scheduling teams, and it is the number that justifies backfill, gang scheduling and graceful preemption all by itself. Conversely, a preemption policy that destroys an average of 20 minutes of work on 50 jobs a day burns 50 × (1/3 hr) × 8 GPUs × $2.50 = $333/day = $121k/year in pure waste.

### A team says their job has been pending for three days while the cluster shows sixty percent utilization. Debug it.

Sixty percent utilization with a deep pending queue is a **placement failure, not a capacity failure**, and there are exactly five causes. I would work them in order, and I would start by pulling the scheduler's per-cycle decision log for that job — if the scheduler does not log *why* each pending job was not placed on each cycle, that is the first bug and I would fix it before anything else. An unexplainable scheduler is unoperable.

**1. Fragmentation.** The job wants 64 GPUs in a topology-friendly placement, and the free 40% is scattered as two or three free GPUs per node across two hundred nodes. Classic bin-packing fragmentation. Diagnosis: histogram of free GPUs per node. Fix: **defragmentation via migration** — checkpoint and relocate small jobs to compact them, which is exactly what Gandiva demonstrated is feasible because DL jobs have clean iteration boundaries. Cheaper fix: bias small-job placement toward already-partially-used nodes (best-fit rather than worst-fit) so free capacity coalesces instead of spreading.

**2. No reservation, so it is being starved by backfill.** Every cycle the scheduler skips the 64-GPU job because it does not fit, then places twenty 1-GPU jobs that do. Utilization stays high and the big job never runs. Diagnosis: is the job's position in the queue improving? Fix: reservations plus backfill-with-deadline as described — backfill jobs may only run if their declared runtime lets them finish before the reservation activates. **This is the single most common cause and it looks exactly like "the cluster is busy."**

**3. Quota.** The team is over its guaranteed allocation and the job is in a burst class that only runs when the cluster is below some threshold. Then the answer is not a scheduler fix, it is a conversation about quota, and the platform's job is to *make that visible in the UI* rather than leaving the job silently pending. **A pending job must always display a machine-generated reason.**

**4. The request is unsatisfiable as specified.** They asked for 64 GPUs of a type that only exists on 40 nodes, or a node selector that matches 8 machines, or a memory-per-GPU constraint no node meets. Depressingly common. Diagnosis is a dry-run placement against an empty cluster — if it fails there, the spec is wrong, not the scheduler.

**5. Unhealthy capacity counted as free.** Nodes with failed GPUs, ECC errors, degraded NVLink, or stuck containers are reported as available and placement repeatedly fails on them. Diagnosis: correlate placement failures with node IDs; you will usually find the same twelve nodes. Fix: health-check gating with automatic cordoning, and — importantly — **count cordoned nodes out of your utilization metric**, or your 60% is a lie.

**🔍 The general lesson to state:** for GPU clusters, "utilization" and "allocatable capacity for a given shape" are different quantities, and only the second one predicts whether a job will run. I would add a dashboard panel showing, for each common job shape (1, 8, 16, 64 GPUs), how many placements are currently possible. That panel answers this question before anyone files a ticket.

### Design a model deployment platform — serving, GPU autoscaling, versioning, canaries and result caching — that other teams use as a product.

The framing that separates this from "deploy a service": **the artifact is not a container, it is a (weights, engine, config, tokenizer, prompt-template) tuple, and every one of those five can change the output.** If your versioning only covers the container image, you will ship a quality regression from a tokenizer or chat-template change and be unable to explain it. So the deployment unit is a **model release**: an immutable manifest containing weight checksum, quantization scheme, engine version and flags, tokenizer version, chat template hash, and the eval results that release passed. That manifest is what gets promoted between environments, and it is the thing a rollback restores.

**Serving layer.** Per-model deployments behind a router (the gateway from earlier, or the same idea internally). Each deployment is a set of replicas, each replica being *N* GPUs running an engine. Load balancing is where LLM serving diverges sharply from stateless HTTP: **round-robin is wrong.** Two better policies, and I would name both. (a) **Least-outstanding-tokens** rather than least-connections — a replica with 3 requests that each have 30k tokens of context is far more loaded than one with 10 short requests, and connection counts cannot see that. (b) **Prefix-affinity routing** — hash the request's prompt prefix and route consistently, so a request whose system prompt is already cached on replica 7 goes to replica 7. **💰 With a 12k-token shared system prompt, a prefix-cache hit removes ~12k tokens of prefill; at a prefill rate of 20k tok/s that is 600 ms of TTFT saved and roughly 12k/20k = 0.6 GPU-seconds of work per request.** Consistent hashing over prefixes is the mechanism, and it is exactly the consistent-hashing-for-cache-locality pattern from any CDN or sharded cache — say that.

**Autoscaling, and the metric is the whole answer.** Do not scale on CPU (meaningless) or GPU utilization (misleading — a decode-bound engine shows 90% while doing almost nothing). Scale on **queue depth and TTFT SLO burn**, with **KV-cache utilization** as the secondary signal that predicts imminent preemption. Concretely: scale out when p95 queue wait exceeds 30% of the TTFT budget for two consecutive minutes, or when KV utilization exceeds 85%.

**The hard constraint: scale-up is slow.** A 140 GB model must be pulled and loaded. From object storage at an aggressive 5 GB/s that is 28 seconds of transfer alone; add container start, CUDA init, engine warmup, and CUDA-graph capture, and a realistic cold start is **90 seconds to 5 minutes**. That is far too slow to react to a traffic spike. So the design must include: a **warm pool** of pre-loaded idle replicas sized from the trailing arrival-burst distribution; **weights cached on local NVMe** on every candidate node so the pull is local; **predictive scaling** on the diurnal curve rather than purely reactive; and **admission control that sheds load gracefully** during the scale-up window, because the honest truth is that for two minutes you will be over capacity and the only question is whether you fail fast or time out slowly. **⚠ Trap: designing an LLM autoscaler with the mental model of a stateless web service.** The 100× difference in scale-up latency changes every parameter.

**Result caching.** Two tiers. Exact-match on `(model_release, normalized_request_hash)` for deterministic requests (`temperature=0`, no tools) — cheap, safe, and typically 10–30% hit rate on internal workloads. And provider-style **prefix caching** inside the engine, which is not really a cache of results but of computation. I would explicitly *not* ship semantic caching (embed the query, return a stored answer if cosine similarity > 0.95) as a default: it returns a confidently wrong answer for a near-miss query, and "what is the refund policy for orders over $500" versus "under $500" are 0.97 cosine apart. **Semantic caching is a correctness hazard dressed as an optimization**, and if a team wants it, it goes behind an explicit opt-in with its own eval.

### How do you canary a model version when the output is non-deterministic and you cannot diff it against the old one?

This is the question that most cleanly separates people who have shipped models from people who have shipped services. **You cannot assert output equality, so you replace equality with distributional and judged comparison, staged over four gates.** I would enumerate the gates.

**Gate 1 — offline eval, blocking.** The new release runs the full golden-set suite: task-specific accuracy, the safety/refusal sets, the format-compliance checks, and a pairwise LLM-judge comparison against the incumbent on a held-out set of real production prompts. Blocking criteria: no safety-set regression at all, no format-compliance regression, and the pairwise win rate against the incumbent must have a lower confidence bound above 45% (i.e. I tolerate parity, not a regression). Also blocking: latency and cost — a release that wins on quality but adds 40% output tokens is a cost regression and must be approved as one.

**Gate 2 — shadow traffic.** Mirror 100% of production requests to the new release, discard its responses, and compare *distributions*: output length, refusal rate, tool-call rate, JSON-parse failure rate, latency, tokens per request, and the distribution of tool names chosen. **These cheap proxies catch the majority of real regressions** — a chat-template change usually shows up as a sudden shift in output length or a spike in parse failures long before anyone judges quality. Shadowing costs you a full duplicate of inference spend for the shadow window, which is worth pricing: **💰 at $200k/month of traffic, a 48-hour shadow at 100% is 200,000 × 2/30 ≈ $13,300.** I would shadow at 10% for four days instead (~$2,700) unless the change is high-risk.

**Gate 3 — canary on live traffic with guardrail metrics.** 1% → 5% → 25% → 100%, with automatic rollback on guardrail breach. Guardrails are the *fast* signals: error rate, p95 TTFT, parse-failure rate, refusal rate, and — the one people forget — **user-side behavioral signals: regeneration rate, thumbs-down rate, conversation abandonment, and for a coding product, suggestion-acceptance rate.** Those behavioral signals are your real quality metric in production and they move within hours. **Route by a stable hash of user ID, not per request**, so a single user does not oscillate between two model versions mid-conversation — that produces incoherent sessions and unusable experiment data.

**Gate 4 — the statistical test, done honestly.** Quality differences are small and noisy, and this is where most teams fool themselves. **📐 Sample size:** to detect a 5-percentage-point difference in a success rate around 70% at 80% power and 5% significance, you need roughly `n ≈ 16 × p(1−p) / δ² = 16 × 0.21 / 0.0025 ≈ 1,344 samples per arm`. To detect 1 point you need 25× that — **about 34,000 per arm**. Say this number out loud, because the practical consequence is severe: **you cannot detect a one-point quality change on a 500-example eval set, and most teams' "the new model is slightly better" claims are noise.** The fix where you can use it: paired comparisons on identical inputs, which removes between-prompt variance and cuts the required n substantially.

**⚠ Trap:** rolling back on a single bad metric. Model changes shift many things at once; a 3% rise in refusal rate might be the intended safety improvement. Guardrails should be tight on *safety and correctness* and loose on *style*, and the rollback rule must be written down before the canary starts, not argued about during it.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I can't diff outputs, so I stage it: a blocking offline eval with a pairwise judge, then shadow traffic where I compare distributions — output length, parse-failure rate, tool-call rate — because those catch template and tokenizer regressions cheaply, then a hash-by-user canary with behavioral guardrails like regeneration rate. And I'd state the sample size up front: detecting a five-point quality change needs about 1,300 examples per arm, so a 200-example eval set can't support the claim we're about to make."

### Design a fine-tuning-as-a-service platform. Customers upload data, get a model, and serve it. Multi-tenant.

**Mental model: this is a multi-tenant batch compute platform with a supply-chain problem attached.** The training part is well-trodden; the parts that will actually hurt are data isolation, the artifact registry, and serving hundreds of customer models economically. I would allocate my design time accordingly — and specifically I would say early that **the platform should default to LoRA rather than full fine-tuning**, because that single decision is what makes multi-tenant serving affordable, and I will show why.

**Ingest and validation, which is where customers churn.** Upload → format validation (JSONL of chat turns, schema-checked) → **immediate, blocking feedback**: token count per example, count of examples exceeding the max sequence length, duplicate detection, a PII scan with a report, and a train/validation split. Most customer fine-tunes fail because the data is bad, and a platform that accepts a bad dataset silently, spends $400 of GPU, and returns a worse model has burned the customer's trust and its own margin. I would go further: **run a baseline eval of the untuned model on the customer's own validation set before training starts, and show them the number.** It costs a few dollars and it is the only way the customer can later tell whether fine-tuning helped.

**Training.** Jobs land on the GPU scheduler described earlier, in a preemptible class with mandatory frequent checkpointing. Isolation: each job runs in its own namespace with its own storage credentials scoped to that customer's prefix; data is encrypted per-tenant; **and no job may read another tenant's dataset or artifacts, which I enforce with per-tenant IAM roles rather than with application-level checks**, because application-level tenant checks are one missing `where` clause from a headline. Hyperparameters: expose few (epochs, learning-rate multiplier, LoRA rank) with good defaults, and auto-select the rest — customers cannot tune what they do not understand, and every exposed knob is a support ticket.

**The artifact registry.** Every completed run produces an immutable adapter artifact with a manifest: base model ID and weight hash, LoRA rank/alpha/target modules, training data hash, hyperparameters, framework and library versions, eval results, and a lineage pointer to the parent if it was continued from another adapter. Two properties I insist on: **the base model version is part of the adapter's identity** — an adapter trained on base v1 must never be loaded onto base v2, because the merge is silently wrong and produces degraded output with no error — and **artifacts are content-addressed and immutable**, with tags as movable pointers, exactly like container images.

**Serving: multi-LoRA on a shared base.** This is the economic core. **📐 Adapter size, derived:** for a 70B-class model with `d_model = 8192`, 80 layers, LoRA rank 16 on q, k, v, o projections with GQA (8 KV heads × 128 = 1024-dim k and v): per layer, q and o each contribute `16 × (8192 + 8192) = 262,144` params, k and v each `16 × (8192 + 1024) = 147,456`. Per layer that is `2×262,144 + 2×147,456 = 819,200` params; across 80 layers, **65.5M params ≈ 131 MB in FP16.** So **200 customer adapters cost about 26 GB** — they fit alongside a quantized base on a single node, and can be paged from host RAM if not.

**💰 The economics, and this is the whole argument:** full fine-tuning gives each customer their own 140 GB model needing its own 2–4 GPUs. At $2.50/GPU-hr, 200 customers × 2 GPUs × 720 hr = **$720,000/month** and most of those GPUs are idle. Multi-LoRA on one shared 4-GPU deployment serving all 200 adapters is 4 × 720 × $2.50 = **$7,200/month** — a 100× difference. **📄 Papers:** Hu et al. (2021), LoRA — low-rank adapters that train ~0.1% of parameters and can be merged or kept separate; Sheng et al. (2023), S-LoRA, and Chen et al. (2023), Punica — systems work on serving thousands of concurrent adapters over one base with batched heterogeneous-adapter kernels, which is what makes the above number real rather than theoretical.

### What breaks in multi-LoRA serving, and when do you tell a customer not to fine-tune at all?

**What breaks, in order.**

*Batching heterogeneity.* A batch containing requests for eight different adapters cannot use a single merged weight matrix, so the engine must apply each request's adapter in the batched kernel — this is what the S-LoRA/Punica line of work solves, and if your engine does not support it, you fall back to grouping requests by adapter, which reintroduces head-of-line blocking and destroys the latency SLO for low-traffic adapters. **Check engine support for batched multi-adapter kernels before you promise a customer a latency number.**

*Adapter swap latency and the long tail.* Most adapters are cold. A 131 MB adapter loaded from host RAM over PCIe at ~25 GB/s is ~5 ms — fine. From object storage it is seconds. So you need an LRU of adapters in HBM, a second tier in host RAM, and a cold-start path; a customer whose adapter is evicted after an hour of inactivity sees a multi-second first request. Either accept that with documentation, or offer a "pinned" tier at a higher price. That tiering is a product decision that falls directly out of a memory-hierarchy fact, and framing it that way is exactly the register these interviews reward.

*Rank inflation.* Customers ask for rank 128 because bigger sounds better. Rank 128 is 8× the memory of rank 16 and, in most narrow-task fine-tunes, no better. Cap it, default it low, and make the customer justify an increase with an eval.

*Base model upgrades.* You want to move everyone to a better base. Every adapter is invalid. There is no shortcut: adapters must be retrained. So the platform needs a **retraining pipeline as a first-class feature** — keep the customer's dataset (with their consent and a retention policy), offer one-click retrain onto the new base, and *show them a comparison* rather than silently migrating. **⚠ Trap:** a platform that cannot upgrade its base models is frozen at whatever was current on launch day, which in this field is a death sentence within a year. Design the migration path before you sign the first customer.

*Quality regression on general ability.* A customer fine-tunes for JSON extraction and their model becomes worse at everything else, including instruction-following and safety behavior. This is expected — fine-tuning on a narrow distribution shifts the model. The platform should run a small general-capability and safety suite on every produced adapter and warn when it degrades, because otherwise the customer discovers it in production and blames you.

**When I tell a customer not to fine-tune.** This is the judgment half of the question and it matters more than the systems half. The escalation ladder is: **prompt engineering → few-shot examples → retrieval → structured-output constraints → model upgrade → fine-tuning**, and fine-tuning is last for a reason. I say no when: (a) the goal is **adding knowledge** — fine-tuning teaches form far more reliably than fact, and RAG is the right tool for "the model does not know our product catalogue"; (b) they have **fewer than a few hundred high-quality examples** — below that, few-shot prompting usually matches or beats a fine-tune and costs nothing; (c) the task is **fast-changing**, since a fine-tune is a snapshot and they will be retraining weekly; (d) **they have no eval** — fine-tuning without a way to measure the result is spending money to change an unmeasured quantity, and I would refuse to run it. Where fine-tuning genuinely wins: enforcing a rigid output format or house style, compressing a long system prompt into weights (which is a *cost* win — turning a 6,000-token prompt into a fine-tune saves 6,000 × $3/1e6 = $0.018 per call, or $1,800 per 100k calls), distilling a frontier model's behavior into a small cheap one, and teaching a genuinely unusual task the base model handles poorly.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I default the platform to LoRA, because 200 full fine-tunes is 200 models needing their own GPUs — roughly $720k a month — while 200 rank-16 adapters is about 26 GB of extra memory on one shared deployment at $7,200. And I'd refuse a customer's fine-tune in two cases: they're trying to add knowledge, which is a retrieval problem, or they have no eval, in which case we'd be spending their money to change something neither of us can measure."

### Design an evaluation and experimentation platform — as a product other teams pay for, not as an internal script.

The thesis I would lead with: **evaluation is a data management problem wearing an ML costume.** The model call is the easy part. The hard parts are dataset versioning, result lineage, judge trust, and statistical honesty — and those are the parts that determine whether anyone believes the numbers a year from now.

**The object model.** *Dataset*: an immutable, content-addressed, versioned collection of examples, each with input, optional reference output, and metadata tags (source, difficulty, language, customer, date). Versioned because "our eval improved" is meaningless if the eval set changed underneath. *Task*: a dataset plus a scorer plus a runner config. *Scorer*: a pure function from (input, output, reference) to a score — programmatic (exact match, regex, JSON-schema validity, unit tests, numeric tolerance), model-based (an LLM judge with a versioned prompt and a pinned judge model), or human (a labelling task routed to a queue). *Run*: an immutable record of `(task_version, system_under_test_version, per_example_results, aggregate, cost, duration, environment)`. *Experiment*: a comparison of runs with a statistical verdict.

**Non-obvious requirements that make it a product rather than a script.**

*Caching and idempotency.* Re-running a 5,000-example eval after changing one prompt line should not cost full price. Cache per-example results keyed on `(system_version_hash, example_hash, scorer_version)`. **💰 On a 5,000-example suite at 3,000 input + 500 output tokens each, a full run is 15M input + 2.5M output = 15×$3 + 2.5×$15 = $45 + $37.50 = $82.50. Run that on every PR across 40 engineers at 6 PRs/day and that is 240 runs = $19,800 *per day*;** with a 70% cache hit from unchanged prompt/example pairs it is $5,940/day. Caching is not a nicety here.

*Judge management.* Judges drift, judges are biased, and judges are themselves a system under test. So: the judge model and its prompt are pinned and versioned as part of the scorer; every judge has a **calibration set** of human-labelled examples with a measured agreement number (Cohen's kappa against humans); and any judge change triggers a re-baseline. **⚠ Trap: silently upgrading the judge model.** Every historical score becomes incomparable and no one notices for a month. I would make judge model IDs immutable in a scorer definition — changing one creates `scorer@v2` and the platform refuses to plot v1 and v2 on the same chart.

*Statistical honesty as a platform feature.* Every aggregate is reported with a confidence interval (bootstrap over examples), paired comparisons are the default when two systems ran on the same dataset, and the platform **refuses to declare a winner when the interval crosses zero** — it prints "no detectable difference at n=500; you need n≈1,300 to detect 5 points." Building that refusal into the tool is worth more than any dashboard, because it removes the most common way teams deceive themselves.

*Online/offline join.* The platform should ingest production traces, let a human or judge label a sample of them, and promote labelled traces into datasets. **The golden set must be fed by production, or it decays into a museum of last year's failures within two quarters.** I would target 1–5% trace sampling with stratification over the tail (errors, refusals, long conversations, low-confidence) rather than uniform sampling, because uniform sampling of a mostly-working system mostly samples success.

*CI integration.* A GitHub check that runs the fast suite (a few hundred examples, programmatic scorers only, under 3 minutes) on every PR, and the full suite plus judges nightly. Blocking on the fast suite, reporting on the slow one. Anything slower than 3 minutes will be disabled by an engineer within a month, and a disabled gate is worse than no gate because everyone believes it is running.

### Your LLM judge agrees with humans eighty percent of the time. Is that good enough to ship, and how would you know when it stops being true?

Eighty percent agreement is meaningless without three other numbers, and giving that answer is the whole point of the question. **First: what is the human-to-human agreement?** If two trained annotators agree only 82% of the time on this task, then a judge at 80% is essentially at the ceiling and the remaining disagreement is task ambiguity, not judge error. If humans agree 97%, an 80% judge is bad. **Second: what is the base rate?** If 85% of outputs are good, a judge that always says "good" scores 85% agreement while being useless. Raw agreement must be replaced with a chance-corrected statistic — Cohen's kappa — or with per-class precision and recall. **Third: is the disagreement biased or symmetric?** A judge that is 80% accurate but errs randomly is usable for measuring *deltas* between two systems, because the noise partially cancels in a paired comparison. A judge that systematically prefers longer answers, or prefers outputs from the model family it belongs to, will manufacture a fake win for whichever system produces longer output. **Length bias and self-preference are the two documented, reproducible LLM-judge biases and they are the ones to name.**

**So the shipping decision.** For **relative** comparisons on a paired set with position-randomized A/B presentation, an 80% judge is often good enough, and I would ship it with the caveat that it cannot be trusted to within a few points. For **absolute** quality gates ("we must be above 95% correct before launch"), 80% is not good enough and I would insist on human labelling for the gate with the judge used only for triage and for continuous monitoring between gates. That split — judge for velocity, humans for decisions that matter — is the practical answer.

**Mitigations that actually move the number:** give the judge a rubric with explicit criteria rather than "rate 1–5"; require it to cite the specific span supporting its verdict, which suppresses vibes-based scoring; use pairwise preference rather than absolute scoring, since models are far better at comparison than at calibration; randomize position and check for position bias by running both orders on a sample; and control for length by including length as a covariate or by truncating. If a decision is high-stakes, use a small ensemble of judges from **different model families** and treat disagreement as a routing signal to a human.

**How I know when it stops being true — the operational half.** A judge silently degrades when the *output distribution* changes: the judge was calibrated on last quarter's model, and this quarter's model writes differently. So I maintain a **standing calibration set of ~200 human-labelled examples, refreshed monthly with new production samples**, and I re-measure kappa on every judge change, every judge-model version bump, and monthly regardless. I alert on a kappa drop of more than 0.05. I also track a **cheap invariant**: the judge's score distribution over a fixed frozen set of outputs. If those scores move at all, the judge changed — the outputs certainly did not.

**⚠ Trap:** using the same model family as both the system under test and the judge, then reporting a win. Self-preference is well-documented enough that a careful interviewer will ask about it, and "we used the same model to judge itself" invalidates the result in their eyes even if the effect is small.

**🏋 Drill:** 20 minutes, unaided. You have 300 examples, two systems, and a judge with measured kappa 0.62 against humans. System B wins 56% of paired comparisons. Write down (a) the 95% confidence interval on that win rate, (b) whether you would ship B, and (c) the two additional experiments you would run first. Pass criterion: your interval is roughly 56% ± 5.6 points (standard error `sqrt(0.56×0.44/300) ≈ 2.9%`, so ±1.96×2.9 ≈ ±5.6), you notice the lower bound (50.4%) barely clears parity, and you name position-bias and length-bias checks as the two experiments.

### Design a realtime voice agent — phone calls, natural conversation. Give me the latency waterfall first.

**The mental model: in voice, latency is not a performance metric, it is a correctness metric.** Humans in natural conversation take turns with gaps averaging a couple of hundred milliseconds; a gap beyond roughly 800 ms–1 s reads as hesitation, and beyond 1.5 s the caller starts talking over you or asks "hello?". So the entire architecture is a latency budget, and every component choice is subordinate to it.

**📐 The waterfall, from the caller's last syllable to their first heard syllable of the response.** Budget: **800 ms total, targeting 500 ms.**

| Stage | Typical | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Network in (caller → your edge) | 30–80 ms | PSTN + SIP trunk adds more than WebRTC |
| Jitter buffer | 20–60 ms | tunable; smaller = more artifacts |
| **Endpointing (deciding they stopped)** | **200–500 ms** | the dominant term |
| ASR finalization | 50–150 ms | near-zero if streaming and already transcribed |
| LLM TTFT | 200–600 ms | includes retrieval and any tool call |
| TTS first audio chunk | 80–200 ms | streaming TTS is mandatory |
| Network out + playout | 30–80 ms | |

**Endpointing dominates and that is the non-obvious result.** A simple voice-activity detector must wait for a silence window to be confident the caller finished; set it at 500 ms and you have spent most of your budget before the model has seen a token. Set it at 150 ms and you interrupt people mid-sentence every time they pause to think. The fix is **semantic endpointing**: a small, fast model that reads the partial transcript and predicts whether the utterance is syntactically and pragmatically complete. "I'd like to book a flight to—" is obviously incomplete; "I'd like to book a flight to Boston." is complete. This lets you cut the silence threshold to ~200 ms on confident-complete utterances while holding longer on incomplete ones. **This is where the biggest single latency win in a voice agent lives, and most candidates never mention it.**

**Overlap everything you can.** ASR is streaming, so the transcript is nearly final when endpointing fires. **Speculatively start the LLM** on the partial transcript before endpointing confirms, and cancel if the caller continues — you pay for some wasted prefill and buy several hundred milliseconds. Stream LLM tokens into TTS at sentence or clause boundaries rather than waiting for the full response, so TTS starts after the first clause. Stream TTS audio out in chunks. **The end-to-end latency should be close to the sum of the *first* chunk of each stage, not the sum of the full stages** — and if your implementation waits for each stage to complete, you have built something with 3-second turnarounds and no amount of model choice will fix it.

**Cascade versus speech-to-speech.** The cascade (ASR → LLM → TTS) is what I would ship by default: each stage is independently swappable, debuggable and testable; you get a text transcript for logging, eval and compliance; and tool calling and structured output work normally. A native speech-to-speech model removes two hops and preserves prosody and emotion, and the latency is genuinely better — but you lose the intermediate transcript as a first-class artifact, controllability drops, and tool-calling maturity varies. **📅 Volatile:** this tradeoff is moving fast and the capability gap is closing; verify the current state before your loop. My decision rule today: cascade for anything transactional (support, booking, verification) where auditability and tool reliability matter; speech-to-speech where naturalness is the product.

### Barge-in and telephony. What is actually hard about those?

**Barge-in** — the caller interrupting while the agent is speaking — is table stakes for a system to feel human, and it is harder than it looks because of three coupled problems.

*Problem 1: echo.* The agent's own TTS audio comes back through the caller's microphone, especially on speakerphone. Without acoustic echo cancellation, your VAD hears the agent, decides the caller is speaking, and the agent interrupts itself in a loop. On WebRTC the browser gives you AEC for free. **On PSTN it does not** — there is no client-side processing on a phone call — so you need server-side echo cancellation, which requires keeping a reference of exactly what you played out and aligning it against the inbound stream with an unknown, variable delay. This is a real DSP problem and it is the reason telephony voice agents are meaningfully harder than web ones.

*Problem 2: distinguishing interruption from backchannel.* "Mm-hmm," "right," "okay" are not interruptions — they are the caller signalling they are listening. An agent that stops every time the caller says "uh-huh" is unusable. So barge-in detection needs more than energy-based VAD: a short-utterance classifier on the first ~300 ms of caller speech that distinguishes backchannel from a real turn attempt, plus a rule that a barge-in must exceed some duration or contain content words before you actually cut the agent off.

*Problem 3: state reconciliation, which is the one engineers get wrong.* When you stop TTS mid-sentence, the model believes it said the whole sentence. Its context now contains text the caller never heard. If it said "your refund of four hundred and—" and was cut off, the conversation state must record **what was actually played**, not what was generated. That requires the TTS layer to report playback position back to the orchestrator and the orchestrator to truncate the assistant turn accordingly, with a marker like `[interrupted]`. **⚠ Trap: recording the full generated text after a barge-in.** The agent then refers back to information the caller never received, and the conversation becomes subtly incoherent in a way that is very hard to debug from transcripts — because the transcript looks fine.

**Telephony specifics that bite.** PSTN audio is **8 kHz narrowband G.711 μ-law at 64 kbps in 20 ms packets**; WebRTC is typically Opus at 16–48 kHz. Narrowband costs you real ASR accuracy — word error rate is materially worse at 8 kHz, particularly on names, numbers and spelled-out strings — so either use an ASR model trained on telephony audio or accept the degradation and design around it (confirm-back critical values, use phonetic alphabets for spelling). DTMF tones must be handled out of band (RFC 2833 events) rather than through the audio path, because ASR will not transcribe them. Calls need SIP signalling, media relay, and a plan for transfer, hold and voicemail detection. And **you must design for the caller who says nothing** — timeouts, re-prompts and a graceful hangup — because silence is a state your state machine will otherwise sit in forever.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Barge-in is three problems, not one: echo cancellation, which is free on WebRTC and a real DSP problem on PSTN; distinguishing a genuine interruption from a backchannel 'mm-hmm'; and reconciling state, because when you cut TTS off mid-sentence the model thinks it said the whole thing. That last one is the bug that ships — the transcript looks fine and the conversation quietly stops making sense."

### That voice agent is live. What breaks, and how do you know before the customer tells you?

**🔍 Failure taxonomy, as a decision procedure.**

*Latency regression from a dependency.* A retrieval call that used to take 80 ms now takes 400 ms, and total turnaround crosses 1.2 s. **Detection: instrument each waterfall stage as a separate span and alert on p95 per stage, not just end-to-end** — end-to-end alerts tell you something is wrong but not what, and in a seven-stage pipeline that is a 30-minute outage instead of a 3-minute one. Containment: a hard deadline on the retrieval call with a degraded path (answer without retrieval, or say "one moment" — a filler phrase played while a slow tool runs is a legitimate and effective technique, and it buys you 1–2 seconds of budget).

*Transcription errors on the values that matter.* Order numbers, dates, names, amounts. ASR at 8 kHz will confuse fifteen and fifty, and it will mangle surnames. Detection: track the rate of confirm-back corrections and the rate of downstream validation failures (order ID not found). Containment: **never act on a transcribed critical value without a confirm-back**, and constrain recognition where possible with a phrase list or biasing toward the customer's known order IDs.

*The agent talks over the caller / the caller talks over the agent.* Detection: measure the **overlap rate** — fraction of turns with simultaneous speech — and the **self-interruption rate**. Rising overlap usually means endpointing is too aggressive or echo cancellation has regressed after a codec or carrier change.

*Dead air.* The single worst caller experience. Any gap over ~1.5 s with no audio should be a logged event with the stage that caused it. I would add a watchdog that plays a filler if no audio has been emitted for 800 ms after a turn ended, so the failure mode is "the agent said 'let me check that'" rather than silence.

*Loops.* The agent asks the same question three times because it fails to extract a slot. Detection: repeated-intent counter per call. Containment: escalate to a human after two failed extraction attempts on the same slot, always — and make sure the human receives the transcript and the extracted state, not just the call.

*Carrier and codec drift.* A new SIP trunk negotiates a different codec, ASR accuracy drops 4 points, and nothing errors. Detection: track ASR confidence distribution and WER on a synthetic canary call placed every 5 minutes through each trunk. **The synthetic canary is the highest-value observability investment in a voice system**, because most voice failures produce no errors at all.

**The metrics I would put on the wall:** turn latency p50/p95 by stage; task completion rate; escalation rate and escalation reason; overlap rate; dead-air events per call; ASR confidence p10; cost per call. **💰 Cost per call, worked:** a 4-minute call with ~20 turns. ASR at streaming rates and TTS per character are both real line items — **📅 Volatile**, so verify — but assume ASR ~$0.006/min = $0.024, TTS for ~600 spoken words ≈ 3,300 characters at typical per-character rates ≈ $0.05, LLM at 20 turns growing to ~40k cumulative input and 4k output = 40×$3/1000 + 4×$15/1000 = $0.12 + $0.06 = $0.18, telephony ~$0.004/min = $0.016. **Total ≈ $0.27 per call**, against a human agent at ~$1.50–$3.00 for the same four minutes. That is a 6–10× margin, and the margin is thin enough that a 3× cost regression from an unbounded context makes the product unprofitable — which is why the per-call token budget is a production control, not a nicety.

### Design an image-generation pipeline serving millions of users. Give me cost per output and tell me where the safety layer sits.

**Mental model: diffusion inference is a fixed, predictable amount of compute per image — steps × model forward passes — which makes it far easier to capacity-plan than LLM decode.** There is no variable-length output, no KV cache, no continuous batching subtleties. Every request is the same shape, so batching is trivially efficient and the whole system is a throughput-optimization problem with a queue in front of it. Say that early: it reframes the problem as classic capacity planning, which plays to a backend engineer's strengths.

**📄 Papers:** Ho et al. (2020), DDPM — established the denoising-diffusion formulation; Rombach et al. (2022), latent diffusion — moved the diffusion process into a compressed latent space via a VAE, cutting compute by roughly the square of the downsampling factor and making high-resolution generation practical on one GPU. That second one is the mechanism that makes this product economically possible and it is the right thing to cite.

**Pipeline.** Request → auth and quota → **prompt safety classifier** → queue (priority by tier) → GPU worker pool → generation (text encoder → N denoising steps in latent space → VAE decode) → **output safety classifier** → watermarking → upload to object storage → CDN URL returned. Asynchronous by default: submit returns a job ID, the client polls or holds a websocket, and progress is streamed as intermediate previews (decoding the latent at step 8 gives a blurry preview that dramatically improves perceived latency for a 4-second generation — a UX trick that costs one extra VAE decode).

**💰 Cost per image, worked.** Assume a latent-diffusion model at 1024×1024, 30 steps, on an H100 at $2.50/GPU-hr = **$0.000694 per GPU-second**. If the model does ~8 steps/second at batch 1 and batching 8 requests takes ~7 seconds for all 8 (denoising batches near-linearly in throughput terms), that is 0.875 GPU-seconds per image → **$0.00061 per image**. Add VAE decode and safety classifiers (~0.1 s of GPU) → **~$0.00068**, call it **$0.0007 per image at full utilization.** At a realistic 55% average utilization it is $0.00068 / 0.55 = **$0.00124**. Add storage and CDN egress: a 1024×1024 JPEG at ~250 KB, delivered maybe 3 times, at $0.05/GB egress = 0.75 MB × $0.05/1024 = **$0.000037** — negligible, which is itself worth noting because people over-index on storage. **At 10 million images/day: 10e6 × $0.00124 = $12,400/day = $372,000/month**, which needs roughly `10e6 × 0.875 GPU-s / 86400 s / 0.55` ≈ **184 H100s**.

**The single biggest cost lever is step count, and it is a 4–8× lever.** Step-distilled models generate acceptable images in 4–8 steps instead of 30, taking cost per image from $0.00124 to roughly $0.0002–0.0004. **📅 Volatile:** the specific distilled model families change constantly; the mechanism — distilling the multi-step sampler into a few-step one, at some cost in diversity and fine detail — is stable. My decision rule: **serve the distilled few-step model as the default and the full-step model as a "high quality" tier**, because most users cannot distinguish them on most prompts and the ones who can will pay. The second lever is resolution — generating at 768² and upscaling costs roughly (768/1024)² = 56% of the compute for the diffusion stage.

**Capacity and queueing.** Because per-image compute is fixed, this is an M/M/c queue and you can size it properly. **📐 Note the queueing consequence:** at 85% utilization mean queue wait is roughly `ρ/(1−ρ)` × service time = 5.7× service time, so a 4-second generation waits 23 seconds. Diffusion products must run at lower utilization than you would like — I would target 55–65% for interactive tiers and backfill the gap with a batch/async tier that has no latency SLO. **That backfill tier is what makes the economics work**, and it is the same insight as the LLM batch tier earlier.

### Where do the safety filters go, and what is the failure taxonomy?

Safety in image generation is layered, and each layer catches a different class. I would present it as **four gates and one out-of-band process.**

**Gate 1 — prompt classification, pre-generation.** A text classifier on the prompt (and on any reference image or embedded instruction) catching explicit requests: sexual content involving minors, non-consensual sexual imagery, gore, named-person likeness, extremist symbols, and trademarked characters if that is a policy. This is the cheapest gate — a few milliseconds — and it is the only one that saves you the generation cost. But it is also the weakest, because prompts can be obfuscated, translated, or built from innocuous compositional pieces. **Never rely on it alone.**

**Gate 2 — output classification, post-generation.** An image classifier over the produced image. This catches what the prompt filter missed, including the important case where a benign prompt produces unsafe output — which happens more than people expect, because the model's priors can push a neutral prompt toward unsafe territory. This gate costs real GPU time and it costs you the generation you already paid for, which is exactly why the prompt gate exists upstream despite its weakness.

**Gate 3 — hash matching against known-illegal-content databases**, run on outputs and on any user-uploaded reference images. This is a legal requirement in many jurisdictions rather than a product choice, and it comes with mandatory reporting obligations and a strict handling protocol. **I would say explicitly that this is a "consult counsel and use the established industry programs" area, not something to design from first principles** — and knowing to say that is itself the senior signal.

**Gate 4 — provenance.** Every output carries embedded provenance metadata (the C2PA content-credentials direction is the industry standard being adopted) plus an invisible watermark that survives re-encoding and cropping better than metadata does. Metadata is stripped by every social platform; the watermark is what remains. **📅 Volatile:** provenance standards and any regulatory mandates around AI-content disclosure are moving; verify current obligations for your jurisdictions.

**Out-of-band: the human review queue and appeals.** Classifiers have false positives, and a false positive is a user who paid you and got refused. So: borderline scores route to human review rather than to a hard block; every block gives the user a specific, appealable reason; appeals are staffed with an SLA; and — crucially — **every appeal outcome feeds back into the classifier's training set.** A safety system with no appeals path is a system whose false-positive rate is invisible and therefore never improves.

**🔍 Failure taxonomy.** *Prompt-filter bypass by composition* — "a woman" + "the style of [artist]" + a reference image, none of which trip the filter individually; detect by classifying the *output*, which is why Gate 2 is not optional. *Likeness generation* — the model produces a recognizable real person from a description; detect with a face-recognition check against a public-figure index for the highest-risk names, and accept that the long tail is unsolvable. *Filter drift after a model upgrade* — a new base model shifts the output distribution and the classifier calibrated on the old one now has a different operating point; detect by re-running the safety eval set on every model change and treating the classifier's threshold as part of the model release manifest. *Over-blocking a demographic or a language* — measure false-positive rate **by prompt language and by subject demographic**, because an aggregate FPR of 2% can hide a 15% rate on one group, and that is both a product failure and a reputational one. *Cost blowout from safety* — if Gate 2 costs 15% of generation compute, at $372k/month that is $56k/month; batch the classifier and run it at lower resolution.

**⚠ Trap:** treating safety as a binary block/allow at a single threshold. The right shape is **three bands — allow, human-review, hard-block — with the thresholds tuned separately per category**, because the acceptable false-negative rate for "gore" and for "CSAM" differ by orders of magnitude and one threshold cannot express that. A candidate who proposes one classifier with one threshold has not thought about the problem.

**🏋 Drill:** 30 minutes, unaided, no calculator beyond arithmetic. Size an image-generation service for 25M images/day at a p95 queue-plus-generation latency of 12 seconds, with a 30-step model at 8 steps/s/image on H100, safety adding 12% compute, and a 3× diurnal peak. Produce: GPUs at peak, GPUs at trough, the utilization you would provision for given the queueing constraint, monthly GPU cost at $2.50/GPU-hr, and cost per image. Pass criterion: you provision from the **peak** rate and not the daily average, you keep utilization at or below ~65% because of the `ρ/(1−ρ)` queueing blowup, and your cost-per-image reflects the resulting idle capacity rather than the theoretical full-utilization number.

### Same question for the platform and infrastructure cases. What sinks a candidate on the serving, gateway and GPU-scheduling designs?

**Rejection answer number one: quoting a framework instead of a mechanism.** "We'd use vLLM." "We'd put it on Kubernetes with an HPA." "We'd use LoRA." Each of these is the right choice and each, said alone, reads as recall rather than understanding. The interviewer is waiting for the sentence after: *why* PagedAttention exists — because pre-reserving contiguous KV for the maximum sequence length wastes most of your memory to internal fragmentation, and paging recovers it; *why* an HPA on CPU or GPU utilization is wrong for LLM serving — because a decode-bound engine reports high utilization while doing almost no FLOPs, so you scale on queue depth, TTFT-SLO burn and KV utilization instead; *why* LoRA is the multi-tenant answer — because 200 rank-16 adapters is 26 GB on one shared deployment versus 200 separate models needing their own GPUs at roughly 100× the cost. **The rule: name the tool, then immediately name the constraint it removes.** A candidate who can do that for three tools is fluent; a candidate who names ten tools and no constraints is reciting a blog post.

**Rejection answer number two: treating GPU serving as if it were stateless web serving.** This is the deep one, and it shows up as a cluster of specific errors: round-robin load balancing (ignores that a replica with three 30k-token contexts is far more loaded than one with ten short requests, and destroys prefix-cache locality); reactive autoscaling (ignores that a 140 GB model takes 90 seconds to five minutes to load, so by the time you scale the spike is over); "just add a retry" on a timeout (doubles the most expensive requests exactly when the system is overloaded); provisioning to average rather than peak; and quoting a single "latency SLO" instead of separating TTFT, inter-token latency and end-to-end. Underneath all of them is one wrong assumption — that capacity is elastic and requests are interchangeable. **In LLM serving, capacity is chunky and slow to acquire, and requests differ in cost by two orders of magnitude.** Every design decision that ignores those two facts is the same mistake wearing a different hat.

The two near-misses on this side: **cost claims without utilization.** Anyone can divide a GPU's hourly rate by its peak throughput and produce a flattering dollars-per-million-tokens; the number that is true is the one divided by *realized* throughput at your actual utilization, and the gap between those two is usually 2–3×. And **no rollback story.** For a serving platform the question "what makes you roll back, and how fast can you" has a precise answer — a model release manifest that is immutable and content-addressed, guardrail metrics with pre-agreed thresholds, and a rollback that is a router config push measured in seconds rather than a redeploy measured in minutes. If your model version is baked into a container image, your rollback is a deploy, and you should know that is a design weakness before the interviewer points it out.

**🗣 Say this in the room, closing any infra case:** "The two numbers I'd hold myself to are realized cost per million tokens at actual utilization — not peak — and SLO-attainment goodput rather than raw throughput. And I'd want the rollback to be a router config push against an immutable model manifest, so reverting a bad model version takes seconds and doesn't require rebuilding anything."
