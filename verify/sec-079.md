## 79. AI Product Sense, Streaming UX and Designing for Model Failure

*Mastering this proves you can pass the product round that Cursor, Perplexity, Notion, Figma, Sierra and Ramp run *for engineers* — and that nobody prepares for.*

### You've spent your career on backend systems. Why does a company like Cursor or Sierra put a *backend* engineer through a product round at all, and what are they actually grading?

Because at an AI product company the model is the commodity and the interaction is the moat. Every competitor can call the same three frontier APIs at the same published price. What they cannot copy cheaply is the decision of *when* to show the user a suggestion, *what* the user sees in the 900 ms before the first token, and *what happens* on the 8% of turns where the model is confidently wrong. Those are engineering decisions with a product surface, and they are made by the engineer building the feature, not by a designer in Figma. So the round exists to find out whether you will ship a feature that survives contact with a component that is wrong somewhere between 2% and 30% of the time — because that is the defining property of the systems you are now being paid to build, and nothing in your backend career has that property. Postgres is not wrong 8% of the time.

What they are actually grading, in the order the debrief will discuss it:

1. **Do you start from the user's task and its latency budget, or from the model?** The failing candidate says "I'd use a frontier model with RAG." The passing candidate says "the user is mid-keystroke, so I have 200 ms, which rules out anything but a small model on a warm prefix cache."
2. **Do you have an explicit theory of being wrong?** Not "we'd add evals" — that is the eval round. Here it means: what does the *screen* look like when the answer is wrong, and does the design make the error cheap to notice and cheap to reverse?
3. **Did you name the metric before the design?** Accept-rate, containment, edit-distance-after-accept. If you design for 20 minutes and then get asked "how would you know it's working," you have already lost half the round.
4. **Do you know what an interaction costs?** In dollars, per user, per month, against the price you charge. Product engineers at Ramp and Notion are expected to be able to do this arithmetic out loud.

**⚠ Trap:** treating the product round as the soft round and coasting on charm. At Cursor, Perplexity, Sierra and Harvey this round is frequently the *highest-weight* stage for an engineer, because the coding and systems rounds are largely commoditized across candidates and this one is not. It is also the round with the least prep material in circulation, which means it separates candidates enormously.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I think of an AI feature as a normal feature wrapped around a component with a nonzero, non-deterministic error rate. So my design has three parts: the happy path, the latency mask that hides the model's variance, and the recovery surface for when it's wrong. If I only design the first one, I've built a demo."

### Walk me through the interaction patterns available for an AI feature, and what latency and trust budget each one implies.

The mental model that organizes all of this: **an interaction pattern is a contract about how much of the user's attention you are borrowing, and the latency budget and the accuracy bar are both derived from that number.** Borrow 200 ms of attention and you may be wrong often, because rejection is free. Borrow twenty minutes and take an irreversible action and you had better be right, because the user has stopped supervising you.

**Inline / ghost text** (Cursor tab, Gmail Smart Compose, Notion's inline continuation). The user is mid-task and has not asked for anything. Budget: **p50 under ~200 ms, p95 under ~500 ms**, because a fast typist emits a keystroke every 150–250 ms and a suggestion that lands after the next keystroke is not a suggestion, it is a flicker. Accuracy bar is *low* — a 25% accept rate is a good inline product — because the cost of a wrong suggestion is one ignored grey string. Blast radius: zero, until the user presses Tab. This pattern is the cheapest place in the entire taxonomy to be wrong, and that is exactly why it is the highest-volume, highest-cost surface.

**Chat.** The user has explicitly asked and will wait. Budget: **TTFT under ~1 s, total 3–20 s** depending on whether tools run. Accuracy bar is medium-high because the user reads the whole thing and forms a judgment about your product from it. Blast radius is still zero — it is text — but the *trust* blast radius is large: one confidently wrong chat answer is remembered for weeks.

**Command palette / selection action** ("rewrite this", "explain this error", "turn this into a table"). The user has selected a target and picked a verb, which is enormously valuable: the intent is unambiguous and the scope is bounded. Budget: **under 2 s to first visible change**. Accuracy bar is high because the user asked for something specific and can compare against what they meant. Blast radius: one document region, and it must be a single Cmd-Z.

**Background agent** (Cursor background agents, a PR-fixing bot, an inbox triage worker). The user delegated and left. Budget: **minutes to hours**, but with a hard requirement you did not have before — *progress must be legible on return*, and the user must be able to reconstruct what happened without watching it happen. Accuracy bar is high and, crucially, so is the *auditability* bar. Blast radius is whatever tools you gave it, which is why this pattern is where approval gates live.

**Batch review queue** (Harvey extracting clauses from 400 contracts, Ramp categorizing 50k transactions, a claims pipeline). There is no per-item latency budget at all; the model runs offline. The metric is **human throughput**: items reviewed per operator-hour. Accuracy bar is expressed as a *routing* decision rather than a threshold — high-confidence items auto-apply, low-confidence items enter the queue. This is the pattern backend engineers most often forget exists, and it is the one that pays for itself fastest in enterprise.

**📐 Numbers you must know:** the three durable human-factors thresholds, because they justify every budget above and they have not changed since the 1980s. **100 ms** — the limit for a response to feel *instantaneous* / directly caused by your action. **1 s** — the limit for uninterrupted flow of thought; beyond this the user notices a delay but stays on task. **10 s** — the limit for keeping attention on the task at all; beyond this people switch tabs and you need a background pattern with a notification. These come from Nielsen's response-time limits (restating Miller's 1968 work), and Doherty's later observation at IBM that dropping system response below ~400 ms produced superlinear gains in user productivity. Cite these; interviewers recognize them and it signals you did not invent your budgets.

**⚠ Trap:** picking chat by default. Chat is the *worst* interaction pattern for any task where the intent is already known from context, because it forces the user to type out something the app could have inferred from selection. In review I push back on any spec whose first screen is an empty chat box: "what does the user have selected when they open this, and why isn't that a verb instead of a prompt?"

### Design the latency budget for inline code completion. Give me the p50 target and tell me exactly how you hit it.

The mental model: **inline completion is not a request/response feature, it is a speculative background job whose result is usually thrown away.** Once you internalize that the modal outcome is discard, every design decision inverts — you optimize for cheap cancellation and cheap misses, not for quality per call.

Target: **p50 ≤ 200 ms, p95 ≤ 400 ms, measured keystroke-to-pixel**, not server-side. Server-side p50 is a vanity metric here; the user's clock starts at the keystroke and includes debounce, network RTT, and render.

Now the budget decomposition, and this is what the interviewer wants to hear:

- **Debounce: 30–80 ms.** You do not fire on every keystroke. You fire when typing pauses. This is your single largest cost lever and it is free.
- **Network RTT: 20–60 ms** if you have regional endpoints, 150–250 ms if the user is in Mumbai and your inference is in us-east-1. Regionalization is not optional for this pattern; it is the difference between shipping and not.
- **Prefill: 10–40 ms.** You are sending 2–8k tokens of context (current file, a few retrieved neighbours). At a prefill throughput of, say, 20k tokens/s per request on a modern accelerator with a batch, 4k tokens is 200 ms — far too slow. Which is why **prefix caching is mandatory, not an optimization**: the file prefix is nearly identical to the previous request's, so you re-prefill only the changed suffix. Cache-hit prefill drops to single-digit milliseconds.
- **Decode: 60–120 ms.** At an inter-token latency of 4–8 ms on a small model, 20 tokens is 80–160 ms. This is why inline models are 1B–7B, not frontier-size, and why you cap `max_tokens` aggressively (one line, or one block).
- **Render: ~16 ms**, one frame.

The engineering machinery that makes this work: **cancel-on-keystroke**. Every new keystroke aborts the in-flight request — client aborts the fetch, server aborts the generation and frees the KV block immediately. If you do not wire cancellation all the way through to the scheduler, you will pay for tokens nobody will ever see and, worse, those doomed requests will occupy batch slots and inflate the latency of the requests that matter.

```python
# Client-side shape. The important part is that the *old* task is cancelled
# before the new one starts, and that cancellation propagates over the wire.
class Completer:
    def __init__(self):
        self._task: asyncio.Task | None = None

    async def on_keystroke(self, doc_state):
        if self._task and not self._task.done():
            self._task.cancel()                 # -> aiohttp closes conn -> server sees disconnect
        self._task = asyncio.create_task(self._complete(doc_state))

    async def _complete(self, doc_state):
        await asyncio.sleep(0.05)               # debounce; cancellation lands here for free
        key = prefix_hash(doc_state)            # local memo: identical prefix -> no network at all
        if (hit := self.local_cache.get(key)) is not None:
            return self.render(hit)
        out = await self.client.complete(doc_state, max_tokens=64, timeout=0.4)
        self.local_cache[key] = out
        self.render(out)
```

Note the `await asyncio.sleep` *inside* the task rather than a timer before creating it — that is the trick that makes the debounce cancellable with the same mechanism as the request, and it is the kind of detail that reads as "has actually shipped this."

**💰 Math:** suppose 5,000 developers, each typing 4 hours/day. With an 80 ms debounce and realistic pause behaviour you get roughly **1 completion request per 3 seconds of active typing** — call it 1,200 requests/hour, 4,800/day/dev, **24M requests/day** across the fleet. At 4k input tokens and 24 output tokens per request, that is 96B input tokens/day. At a naive $0.30/Mtok for a small model that is **$28,800/day input alone = $864k/month** — which is why nobody serves this pattern on a per-token vendor API. With a 90%+ prefix-cache hit rate and self-hosted small models the marginal cost collapses to GPU-hours: roughly 24M requests/day ÷ 86,400 s = **278 req/s sustained**, which at ~60 concurrent short requests per accelerator and 150 ms each is on the order of 40–70 accelerators, or **$40k–$90k/month** at typical rental rates. Same feature, an order of magnitude apart, purely from architecture. **📅 Volatile:** all per-token prices and rental rates — verify before your loop.

**⚠ Trap:** measuring accept-rate without measuring *suppression*. The highest-leverage change to an inline product is usually deciding **not to show** a suggestion — after a `}`, in the middle of a word, when the model's own logprobs are flat. Teams that only optimize the model plateau; teams that build a suppression classifier see accept-rate move 5–10 points because the denominator shrank. If you mention suppression unprompted in this round, you sound like someone who has run this product.

### When would you pick a command palette over a chat box, and when is chat actually the right call?

Chat's only genuine advantage is that it is **unbounded in intent** — the user can ask for something you never anticipated. Everything else about it is a cost: the user must translate a want into English, you must infer scope, and you must handle the enormous tail of things you cannot do. So my decision rule is: **if the space of useful verbs is small and enumerable, ship verbs; if it is genuinely open, ship chat; if you don't know, ship verbs first, because the verbs teach you what chat needs to support.**

Command palette / selection actions win when: the user already has a target selected (so scope is free), the verbs are few (rewrite, summarize, translate, explain, fix), latency matters (no prompt-typing time, and you can *prefetch on selection* before the user even picks the verb), and discoverability matters — a palette advertises capability, an empty chat box hides it. This last point is underrated: the single most common complaint about chat-first AI features is "I don't know what to ask it." A palette answers that question by existing.

Chat wins when the task requires multi-turn negotiation (the user's first framing is wrong and they need to refine), when the artifact under discussion is the conversation itself (support, research), or when the tail of intents is fat and unenumerable. Perplexity is chat because "what do I want to know" is unbounded. Notion's AI is mostly verbs-on-selection because "what do I want to do to this block" is not.

The hybrid that most mature products converge on: **verbs as the front door, chat as the escape hatch, with the verb pre-filling the chat.** The user picks "Fix this error," gets a one-shot result in 1.2 s, and if it's wrong there's a text box already scoped to that error where they can say "no, it's a version mismatch." You get the palette's latency and discoverability with chat's recovery.

**⚠ Trap:** believing a bigger model removes the need for scoping. It does not — scoping is not about capability, it is about *latency and blast radius*. A palette action knows exactly which 40 lines to rewrite; a chat request has to infer it, and inference errors here produce edits in places the user wasn't looking, which is the single most trust-destroying failure mode in an editor.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I'd start with three verbs on selection rather than a chat box, because selection gives me scope for free — which means a tighter prompt, a smaller model, a 1-second response, and a diff the user can reject with one keystroke. I'd add chat once the telemetry tells me which fourth verb people keep trying to type."

### What changes about the UX when the agent runs for twenty minutes in the background instead of twenty seconds in front of the user?

Everything, because you lose the thing that makes streaming work: **a watching human.** Streaming is a latency mask that only functions while someone is looking at the screen. A background agent's user is in another tab, and by the time they come back the run is a *log*, not an experience. So the design problem shifts from "how do I make waiting tolerable" to "how do I make twenty minutes of machine work auditable in ninety seconds of human reading."

Four requirements, and I'd state them as requirements rather than nice-to-haves:

**A durable, resumable run record.** The user may close the laptop. This is straightforward backend work you already know — the agent loop's state is a row plus an append-only event log, not an in-memory Python object — but the product consequence is what matters: the run must be linkable, shareable, and re-openable from a notification.

**A summary written for the return, not the watch.** The single biggest mistake is showing the returning user a 400-line transcript. What they need, top to bottom: what it did (one sentence), what it changed (a diff or a list of side effects), what it wasn't sure about (the two decisions worth challenging), and what it couldn't do. The transcript goes behind a disclosure. I'd have the agent *emit* that summary as a final structured step rather than reconstructing it from logs, because a model summarizing its own trajectory with the trajectory in context is cheap and good, whereas your log formatter is neither.

**Approval gates positioned by blast radius, not by step count.** Reading files, running tests, drafting a PR — no gate. Pushing to a shared branch, sending an email, moving money, deleting anything — gate. And the gate must be *askable asynchronously*: the agent parks, notifies, and resumes on approval, rather than blocking a live connection for four hours.

**A notification that carries the verdict.** "Your agent finished" is a bad notification; "Fixed 3 of 4 failing tests, needs your call on the migration" is a good one, because it lets the user decide whether to context-switch now. This is a product decision with an engineering cost — you need the summary before you can notify, so the summary step is on the critical path of the notification, not an afterthought.

**🔍 Failure taxonomy — background agents.** (1) *Silent death*: the process crashed and the user learns twenty minutes later from absence. Fix: heartbeats plus a wall-clock deadline that emits a failure event, so absence is never the signal. (2) *Runaway loop*: the agent retries a failing tool 40 times. Fix: a per-run step budget and a per-run token budget, both surfaced in the UI as a consumed-of-total, because a user who sees "38 of 40 steps" understands the truncation and a user who sees a truncated result does not. (3) *Stale world*: the agent's plan was made against a repo state that changed. Fix: record the base revision in the run record and refuse or rebase on mismatch — exactly the optimistic-concurrency check you'd write for any long transaction. (4) *Unauditable success*: it worked but nobody can tell why, so nobody trusts it next time. This is the one teams under-weight, and it is why the summary step is not optional.

### Design the batch review queue for a document-extraction pipeline — say, pulling 12 fields out of 400 commercial leases. What does the operator actually see?

Mental model first: **in a batch review product the model is not the user-facing feature — the queue ordering is.** The model's job is to make most items not need a human; the product's job is to spend the human's attention on exactly the items where it changes the outcome. So the design target is not accuracy, it is **operator-minutes per hundred documents**, and every UI decision is judged against that number.

The pipeline shape: extract each field with a confidence signal, then route. High-confidence fields auto-apply and never appear in the queue. Mid-confidence fields appear pre-filled with a one-click confirm. Low-confidence fields appear blank with the source span highlighted so the operator reads the lease, not the model's guess. That three-way routing is the entire product, and the threshold placement is a business decision you should ask about out loud: "what does a wrong auto-applied field cost versus an extra operator-minute?"

What the operator's screen must contain, in priority order:

1. **The document, positioned at the evidence**, not from page one. If you cannot deep-link to the span, your product is a PDF viewer with a form next to it and you have saved nobody any time.
2. **The extracted value, editable in place, with keyboard-only confirm/reject.** Operators do 400 of these. Every mouse trip is 1.5 seconds; at 12 fields × 400 docs that is 4,800 mouse trips = **2 hours of pure pointing**. Keyboard-first is not polish, it is the feature.
3. **Field-level confidence rendered as a routing decision, not a number.** Never show "0.73." Show a visual tier — confirmed / check this / needs you — because a raw probability invites the operator to do calibration arithmetic they cannot do.
4. **A "this whole document is weird" escape** that ejects the doc to a specialist queue in one keystroke. Without it, one pathological scanned fax blocks the operator for six minutes and destroys their throughput average.
5. **Batch operations across documents by field**, not just per-document. "Show me all 400 governing-law values at once" catches systematic errors — the extractor read the wrong clause type in every Delaware lease — that per-document review structurally cannot surface. This is the single highest-value affordance in the whole design and almost no candidate mentions it.

**💰 Math:** unassisted, a paralegal extracting 12 fields from a 40-page lease takes ~25 minutes. 400 leases = 167 hours ≈ **$16,700 at $100/hr fully loaded**. With extraction at 80% auto-apply and mid-confidence confirms at ~15 s/field, review time is roughly 12 fields × 20% needing attention × 30 s = 72 s/doc plus 60 s of document orientation ≈ **2.2 min/doc → 14.7 hours → $1,470**. Model cost: 40 pages ≈ 20k tokens input, one pass, 400 docs = 8M input tokens; at $3/Mtok that is **$24**. So the model is 1.6% of the remaining cost and the entire economics live in the review UI. Say that number in the room — it reframes the whole conversation away from model choice.

**⚠ Trap:** setting the auto-apply threshold from the model's raw probability. Raw softmax confidence from an LLM is poorly calibrated and, worse, non-stationary — it shifts when the vendor updates the model underneath you. Calibrate against a labelled holdout, express the threshold as "the score at which observed precision is 99%," and **re-measure the mapping on every model change**, because the threshold that gave 99% precision last month may give 94% today with no code change and no alert.

### You keep saying "trust budget." Define it, and tell me how you'd measure it.

Trust budget is the observation that **a user's willingness to accept your product's output is a depleting resource that errors withdraw from and correct-and-verifiable outputs deposit into, and the withdrawal is much larger than the deposit.** It is not a metaphor I use to sound thoughtful — it has a measurable behavioural signature, and designing without it is how teams ship features with excellent offline metrics that nobody uses in week three.

The asymmetry is the important part. Roughly, one memorable wrong answer costs you what ten right answers earned, because right answers are unmemorable (the user expected it to work) and wrong answers are diagnostic (the user updates their model of *your model*). This is why "we improved accuracy from 91% to 93%" often produces zero behavioural change: the user's policy is set by the worst thing they've seen, not the mean.

How I measure it, in order of how quickly you can instrument it:

- **Verification behaviour.** Does the user check the output before using it? In a citations product, hover-to-source rate; in a code product, time between suggestion accepted and the block being re-read or edited. Falling verification rate over a cohort's lifetime is trust accumulating. *Rising* verification rate after a model change is the earliest warning you will get that you shipped a regression — earlier than thumbs, earlier than churn.
- **Depth of delegation.** Does the user let the agent take bigger actions over time? Track the distribution of tasks by blast radius per user-week. A cohort that starts with "summarize" and never graduates to "draft and send" has not gained trust regardless of what the NPS survey says.
- **Recovery after a bad turn.** Instrument the session immediately following a thumbs-down or a rejected diff. Do they retry, rephrase, or leave? A product with a good trust design shows *retry*; a product without one shows session end. This single metric — **post-error retry rate** — is the cleanest available proxy and I would put it on the dashboard on day one.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I treat trust as a metric with a leading indicator, not a vibe. The one I instrument first is post-error retry rate — what fraction of users try again after a bad answer. If that number is healthy, my recovery UX is working even though my model isn't perfect. If it's falling while accuracy is flat, my *presentation* of errors is the bug, not the model."

**⚠ Trap:** believing trust is won by hiding errors. It is won by making errors *cheap and legible*. A product that says "I couldn't find this in your documents" builds more trust than one that fabricates a plausible answer 5% of the time, even though the first one is measurably "worse" on any answer-rate metric. If your PM optimizes answer-rate, that is the number that will silently destroy the product.

### Take me through what the user sees at 0 ms, 200 ms, 1 second, and 5 seconds after they hit enter. Be specific.

The mental model: **streaming is not a way of being faster, it is a way of converting one long unexplained wait into a sequence of short explained ones.** Total latency barely moves; perceived latency collapses, because the user's clock resets every time the screen changes meaningfully. Your job is to guarantee that something meaningful changes at least every ~1 second, and to make each change *informative about what will happen next*.

**0 ms — acknowledge, unconditionally and locally.** The input clears or greys, the send button becomes a stop button, and the user's message appears in the transcript. No network involved. If your first pixel change waits for a server round trip, you have already spent 80 ms of your 100 ms instantaneity budget on nothing. This is also where you commit the user's message optimistically to local state so a failed request doesn't lose their typing.

**~200 ms — show the shape of the answer.** Not a spinner. A skeleton that reflects the structure you expect: if this is a RAG answer, three source cards in a loading state; if it's a code edit, the file header and a collapsed diff frame. The skeleton is doing real work — it is a *prediction* the user can start reading, and it makes the eventual arrival of content feel like filling in rather than replacing.

**~200–800 ms — show what the system is doing, if it isn't generating yet.** This is where retrieval and tool calls live. "Searching your workspace…" then "Found 8 documents" then "Reading Q3-planning.md". Each of these is a real event from your pipeline, not a fake progress animation, and each one resets the user's patience clock. Perplexity's entire perceived-speed advantage is built here: it turns a 4-second answer into four 1-second steps.

**~1 s — first token.** TTFT is the number the user actually feels, and it is the one to defend in your SLO. Past this point the wait is over psychologically even if generation runs another 8 seconds, because the user is now reading and reading consumes the time.

**~5 s — the answer is still streaming and the user has caught up to the tokens.** Two things must be true here. First, **inter-token latency must exceed reading speed but not by much**: a comfortable adult reads roughly 250 words/min ≈ 5.5 tokens/s, so any ITL under ~180 ms/token keeps the text ahead of the eye. Streaming much faster than that has diminishing returns for chat — but do not slow it down, because the user's *scan* speed for code and structured output is far higher than their prose reading speed. Second, if you are *not* streaming at 5 s — because a tool is running — you owe the user a progress statement with an expectation ("Running 340 tests, ~40 s") and a cancel button that actually works.

**Beyond 10 s** you are outside the attention limit and should have converted to a background pattern with a notification, or you should have shipped a partial result and continued refining it.

**⚠ Trap:** streaming markdown as raw text and re-parsing on every chunk. You get flickering tables, a code fence that renders as prose for 300 ms before the closing backticks arrive, and a layout that jumps every frame. The fix is a streaming-tolerant renderer that (a) parses incrementally, (b) treats an unclosed fence as an open code block rather than as text, and (c) reserves layout for known-structure elements so nothing below the cursor moves. Layout shift during streaming is the most common self-inflicted quality complaint in AI products and it has nothing to do with the model.

### Skeleton, spinner, or streamed tokens — how do you choose, and can you get it wrong?

You can get it wrong badly, and the rule is simple enough to state as a policy: **a spinner is an admission that you know nothing about what's coming; a skeleton is a claim about the shape of the result; streamed tokens are the result itself. Always use the most informative one you can honestly support.**

Use **streamed tokens** whenever the model is actually generating user-facing text and the output is linear. It dominates everything else because it is not a proxy for progress, it *is* progress.

Use a **skeleton** when you know the structure but not the content — the retrieval phase before an answer, a table whose column count you know, a card grid. The skeleton must match the real layout closely, or the content arriving causes a reflow and the user perceives it as a glitch. A skeleton that lies about shape is worse than a spinner.

Use a **spinner** only when you have neither structure nor content and the wait is short (< 1 s) — e.g. an authentication round trip. A spinner that runs for 8 seconds is a UI that has given up.

Add a fourth option candidates forget: **staged status text** ("Searching… Reading 8 sources… Drafting…"). For any pipeline with distinguishable phases this beats all three, because it converts dead time into information and doubles as your debugging surface. I've shipped features where the status text was the difference between "this is slow" and "this is thorough" with identical p95.

**⚠ Trap:** the fake progress bar. A bar that advances on a timer rather than on real events is a lie that works exactly once. Users calibrate on it within a session — they learn that 60% means nothing — and the next time your bar sits at 80% for 12 seconds they conclude the product is broken. If you cannot compute real progress, use an indeterminate indicator and a phase label, never a percentage you made up.

**🏋 Drill (20 minutes, unaided):** take a RAG chat endpoint you've written and enumerate every server-side event worth surfacing — query rewrite issued, retrieval started, k documents returned, reranker finished, first generation token, tool call started/finished, done. Write the SSE event schema for it (event name + payload) and sketch the client reducer that maps each event to a UI state. **Pass criterion:** every event is one your backend genuinely emits, no event is a timer, and the client renders something distinct for each. If you find yourself inventing an event to fill a gap in the timeline, you have found a real latency hole in your pipeline — that's the point of the drill.

### We shipped a change that made total generation latency 30% faster, and users told us it felt slower. What happened?

Almost certainly you traded TTFT for throughput, and the user's clock only counts TTFT.

The mechanism: total latency for a streamed response decomposes as `TTFT + (n_output_tokens − 1) × ITL`. Anything that improves the second term while worsening the first — larger continuous batches, aggressive chunked prefill that deprioritizes new arrivals, a bigger model with better tokens-per-second on a faster accelerator, a reranker added before generation, a "cheap classifier first" router that adds a serial hop — makes the *number* go down and the *feeling* go worse. Users experience waiting-before-anything as painful and waiting-while-reading as free. A response that starts at 400 ms and takes 9 s total feels dramatically faster than one that starts at 2.5 s and takes 6 s total, even though the second is objectively 33% quicker.

Second candidate cause, and check it before you touch the model: **you regressed the pre-generation phase without regressing generation.** Adding a query-rewrite call in front of retrieval costs a full extra round trip — a 600 ms model call plus 40 ms network — and it lands entirely inside TTFT. Teams do this all the time to improve retrieval quality, watch offline metrics improve, and never look at the latency histogram of the phase they inserted.

Third: **variance, not mean.** If your change halved p50 but doubled p99, users notice the p99 because a single 14-second turn poisons a session. LLM latency distributions are extremely long-tailed — output length is itself a random variable, so the tail of `n_tokens` becomes the tail of latency. This is the specific reason I insist that any AI feature's SLO be written on **TTFT p95 and ITL p95 separately**, never on end-to-end p50.

**📐 Numbers you must know:** the three metrics and their definitions, because interviewers use them as a shibboleth. **TTFT** = enter to first token, dominated by queueing + prefill, scales with input length. **ITL / TPOT** = inter-token latency, dominated by memory bandwidth during decode, roughly independent of input length once the cache is warm. **Throughput/goodput** = tokens/s across all concurrent requests under an SLO — the operator's metric, in direct tension with the first two, because the batching that raises it is the queueing that raises TTFT.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Total latency is the wrong SLO for a streaming product. I'd split it into TTFT and inter-token latency and defend them separately, because users pay attention to the first and read through the second. My guess is we improved decode throughput by batching harder and pushed 400 ms of queueing into TTFT — I'd check the TTFT p95 histogram before and after the deploy, and I'd expect the regression to be entirely there."

### An agent turn fires six tool calls and takes 40 seconds. What do you render while that's happening?

The governing principle: **show activity at the granularity of user-meaningful nouns, not at the granularity of your implementation.** "Calling `search_documents(query='q3 revenue', k=20)`" is a log line. "Searching your workspace for Q3 revenue" is a product. The user is not debugging your agent; they are deciding whether to keep waiting.

What I render, concretely: a vertical timeline where each tool call is one collapsed row with a verb, an object, a state (running / done / failed), and a duration once complete. Running rows get a subtle indeterminate indicator; completed rows collapse to a single line with a result count ("Found 8 documents"). Rows are expandable to show the actual arguments and a truncated result — that disclosure is what makes the feature debuggable by power users and by your own support team, and it costs nothing when collapsed. Failures do not disappear: a failed tool call stays visible with its error and, ideally, what the agent did next ("Retried with a broader query").

Three rules I enforce in review:

**Never let the timeline be silent for more than about a second.** If a tool genuinely takes 25 seconds — a test suite, a large query — that row needs its own sub-progress or at minimum an elapsed timer, because a static "Running tests…" for 25 s is indistinguishable from a hang.

**Stream the reasoning-to-tool transition, not just the tools.** The most trust-building single element in an agent UI is the one-line intent that precedes each call: "I need the Q3 numbers, so I'll search the workspace." That sentence is cheap to generate (you often already have it in the model's output), and it converts the timeline from a machine log into a legible plan.

**Cap the visible depth.** An agent that fires 40 tool calls should not render 40 rows; it should render the last few plus a collapsed "12 earlier steps." Otherwise the user's viewport is consumed by machinery and the answer, when it comes, is off-screen. Auto-scroll behaviour here needs care: scroll to keep the *newest* content visible, but stop auto-scrolling the moment the user scrolls up, and never yank them back. Getting this wrong is one of the most-complained-about details in every agent product I've used.

**⚠ Trap:** rendering raw tool arguments to end users. Beyond being unreadable, it leaks your tool schema, your internal IDs, and often your prompt structure — which is a genuine prompt-extraction and competitive-intelligence surface, not a theoretical one. Render a human phrasing by default and put the raw payload behind a developer toggle that is off in production for non-admin users.

**💰 Math:** the cost of that 40-second agent turn is worth having in your head. Six tool calls means seven model invocations (six deciding + one final answer). If context grows from 4k to 18k tokens across the turn, the total *input* billed across all seven calls is roughly the sum of the growing prefixes — about 4+6+8+10+13+15+18 = **74k tokens**, not 18k. At $3/Mtok that is $0.222 in input; add ~2k output tokens at $15/Mtok = $0.030. **~$0.25 per agent turn.** With 90% prefix caching on the stable prefix at a 10% cache read rate, input falls to roughly $0.05 and the turn lands near **$0.08**. At 50k turns/day that is the difference between **$12,500/day and $4,000/day** — $3.1M/yr versus $1.0M/yr. This arithmetic — that agent cost grows *quadratically* in turns because every step re-sends the transcript — is the single most important cost fact in agent product design, and stating it unprompted is a strong signal.
### Should you show the user the model's reasoning tokens? Argue both sides and then tell me your rule.

The honest answer is that this is genuinely contested and the industry has not converged, so say that first and then give a decision rule — an interviewer who is themselves unsure will respect the framing and distrust a confident "always show it."

**The case for showing.** Reasoning is the highest-density trust signal available to you. When a legal research product shows "I'm checking whether this statute was amended after 2019," the user learns three things at once: the system understood the question, it knows what would make the answer wrong, and it is doing work proportional to the difficulty. It also converts a 30-second silence into readable content, which is worth more than any spinner. And it makes errors *diagnosable by the user*: when the reasoning says "assuming the fiscal year ends in December," a wrong answer becomes a correctable premise rather than an inexplicable failure.

**The case against.** Four real costs. (1) **Reasoning traces are not faithful explanations** — this is the important one and most candidates don't know it. The chain of thought a model emits is a sample from a distribution, not a log of the computation that produced the answer; models can and do produce correct answers with incoherent stated reasoning and vice versa. Showing it therefore implies a guarantee you cannot make. (2) **Leakage.** Raw thinking often restates the system prompt, names internal tools, quotes retrieved documents the user isn't authorized to see, and reveals policy boundaries — which is a direct prompt-extraction and jailbreak-assistance surface. (3) **Confusion and anxiety.** Users read "the user might be trying to trick me" or "I should be careful here" as accusation. Self-doubt in the trace ("I'm not certain this is right") reads as incompetence even when the final answer is correct. (4) **Reading cost.** Most users do not want 900 tokens of deliberation; they want 40 tokens of answer.

**My rule, three parts.** *Show a summarized, product-authored version by default; show the raw trace never; make depth a user choice.* Concretely: generate or extract a small number of short phase labels ("Checking amendment history", "Comparing to the 2021 filing") and stream those. Keep the raw reasoning server-side for your own evals and debugging, where it is genuinely valuable. Offer an explicit "show details" for the power-user cohort that wants it — in developer tools like Cursor that cohort is large and you should serve it; in a consumer product it is a rounding error.

**⚠ Trap:** believing that showing the chain of thought makes the system explainable or auditable, and saying so in a regulated-domain interview. It does not, and at Harvey or a healthcare company that answer will be marked as a compliance misunderstanding. The defensible artifact is **grounding** — the retrieved source and the span it supports — not the model's narration of itself. If you need auditability, cite documents, log the exact prompt and retrieval set, and version the model; do not point at the reasoning.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I'd show a product-authored summary of what the system is doing, not the raw reasoning. The raw trace leaks the system prompt and retrieved content, and — more importantly — it isn't a faithful account of the computation, so surfacing it implies an explainability guarantee I can't back. I'd keep the full trace server-side where it's genuinely useful, in evals and incident review."

### Walk me through the concrete leak vectors in exposing a reasoning trace, and how you'd mitigate them if the product requires showing it.

Assume the requirement is fixed — a developer-facing product where users expect to see the thinking. Then this becomes a straightforward data-flow control problem and you should treat the trace like any other untrusted, sensitive output stream.

**Vector 1: system-prompt restatement.** Models routinely paraphrase their instructions while reasoning ("The instructions say I shouldn't discuss pricing tiers, so…"). That single sentence hands an attacker your policy surface and tells them exactly what to route around. *Mitigation:* a redaction pass over the trace before it leaves the server — the cheapest effective version is exact and near-exact n-gram matching against your own system prompt (say, any 8-gram overlap), which catches verbatim restatement, plus a small classifier for paraphrase. Do this as a stream transform, not as a post-hoc check, or you cannot stream at all.

**Vector 2: retrieved-content exfiltration.** In a multi-tenant RAG system the trace may quote a chunk that the *retrieval* layer returned but the *answer* layer would have suppressed. If your authorization check lives in the answer-generation prompt rather than in the retriever, the trace is now your ACL bypass. *Mitigation:* this is not a UX fix — the invariant is that **retrieval must be pre-filtered by the user's permissions**, so that nothing the model can see is anything the user cannot. If you find yourself writing "the prompt tells the model not to reveal documents from other tenants," stop; that is a prompt-based access control and it will fail.

**Vector 3: tool and schema disclosure.** Traces name tools and arguments. For an internal enterprise tool this is fine; for a public product it maps your architecture and tells an attacker which injection targets exist. *Mitigation:* maintain a display-name mapping and render only names on the allowlist; anything unmapped renders as a generic "checking internal data."

**Vector 4: PII and third-party data.** The trace may reason about a customer record in full detail on a screen that will be screenshotted into a support ticket. *Mitigation:* the same PII scrubber you already run on logs, applied to the display stream.

**Vector 5: the jailbreak feedback loop.** This one is structural rather than a leak. Showing the trace gives an adversary a **gradient signal** — they can see exactly which phrasing moved the model toward compliance and iterate. A refusal with a visible trace is dramatically easier to attack than an opaque one, because the attacker's search becomes guided rather than blind. *Mitigation:* suppress the trace specifically on turns where a safety classifier fired. Users see reasoning on normal turns and a plain refusal on flagged ones, which is exactly the asymmetry you want.

**⚠ Trap:** implementing redaction on the complete trace and then streaming it. If your redactor needs the whole string, you have serialized the reasoning phase and destroyed the latency benefit that motivated showing it. Write the redactor as a streaming filter with a lookback window (buffer the last ~200 characters so an n-gram straddling a chunk boundary is still caught) and accept a small, bounded display lag instead.

### How do you surface confidence to a user when the model's own probabilities are badly calibrated?

Mental model: **do not surface confidence, surface a decision.** A raw number invites the user to do Bayesian arithmetic they cannot do and you cannot support, whereas a routing decision — auto-applied, flagged for check, escalated — communicates the same information in a form the user can act on. The number is your input; the tier is your output.

Why the raw number is untrustworthy: a language model's token probabilities are the model's estimate of *what text comes next*, not of *whether the claim is true*. Instruction-tuned and RLHF'd models are systematically overconfident — the alignment process rewards decisive-sounding answers — and "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks," Guo et al. (2017), established the general result that modern deep networks are miscalibrated and that a single temperature parameter fitted on a holdout recovers most of the calibration. That temperature-scaling recipe is still the right first move: it is one scalar, it costs a validation set, and it changes nothing about your model.

Signals that actually work, in increasing order of cost:

- **Sequence logprob normalized by length**, for short structured extractions. Cheap, decent, and it fails on long free-form text.
- **Self-consistency spread.** Sample k=5 at temperature 0.7 and measure agreement. If five samples agree on the extracted value, you have a strong signal; if they disagree, you have a strong signal in the other direction. Costs 5× on the sampled subset — which is fine, because you only sample the items near your threshold.
- **Semantic entropy**, which clusters those samples by *meaning* rather than by string, so "Paris" and "the capital of France" count as agreement. Farquhar et al. (2024) in *Nature* showed this detects a meaningful class of confabulation that string-level entropy misses. This is the technically-correct version of the previous bullet.
- **Grounding-based confidence.** For RAG, the honest signal is not the model at all — it is whether a retrieved span entails the claim. An NLI or judge check of claim-vs-source is more predictive of factual correctness than any logprob, because it measures the thing you care about instead of a proxy for it.
- **Verbalized confidence** ("rate your certainty 1–5"). Popular because it's free; unreliable because it is itself a generation, subject to the same overconfidence. Usable *only* after you have measured its empirical precision per bucket and found the buckets separate.

The UI rule: **map the calibrated score to at most three tiers, and label the tiers by what the user should do.** Not "73% confident" but "Verify this" with the source open. And re-fit the mapping whenever the model, the prompt, or the retrieval corpus changes — the score-to-precision curve is a property of the whole pipeline, not of the model.

**💰 Math:** the sampling approach is cheaper than people assume because you gate it. Suppose 100k extractions/day, each 2k input / 100 output tokens. Single-pass cost at $3/$15 per Mtok: 100k × (2000×3 + 100×15)/1e6 = 100k × $0.0075 = **$750/day**. Now sample k=5 only on the 15% of items whose first-pass logprob is near the threshold: extra cost = 15k × 4 extra passes × $0.0075 = **$450/day**, a 60% increase. If that moves auto-apply precision from 96% to 99%, you removed 3% of 100k = 3,000 items/day from human review at ~20 s each = **16.7 operator-hours/day ≈ $1,670/day saved**. Net positive by 3.7×. Show that arithmetic and you have justified the design rather than asserted it.

**⚠ Trap:** displaying a percentage that was never validated against outcomes. If your UI says "85% confident" and the observed accuracy of that bucket is 62%, you have not merely failed to help — you have manufactured a false precision that users will rely on, and in a regulated domain that is a liability finding, not a UX nit.

### Design the "I don't know" experience. How do you let the system abstain without users concluding it's useless?

The mental model is from classical decision theory and it is worth naming: this is **selective prediction** — the model gets a reject option, and you are choosing a coverage/risk operating point. Chow's 1970 formulation of the reject option makes the trade explicit: you accept lower coverage in exchange for lower error on what you do answer. The product question is entirely about how you spend the coverage you gave up.

The failure mode to design against is not abstention itself; it is **empty abstention.** "I don't know" as a terminal response is a dead end, and three of those in a week teaches the user the product doesn't work. Every abstention must ship with something the user can do next. In practice that means an abstention response has four parts:

1. **A specific statement of what's missing**, not a generic disclaimer. "I couldn't find anything in your workspace about the Q3 pricing change" beats "I don't have enough information." Specificity proves you looked, which is itself trust-building.
2. **The closest thing you did find**, offered as adjacent rather than as an answer. "I did find the Q2 pricing doc and a Slack thread from July — want me to use those?" This converts a dead end into a one-click branch and it is the single highest-value element of the design.
3. **A repair affordance.** Upload the missing document, connect the missing data source, broaden the search, rephrase. The abstention is a place where the user can *fix your retrieval*, and you should make that the obvious action.
4. **An escalation path** where one exists — a human, a search over a wider corpus, a slower and more expensive mode ("search the web too, ~20 s").

Tone matters more here than anywhere else in the product. Abstain **factually, not apologetically.** "I don't have that in your workspace" is confident. "I'm sorry, I'm just an AI and I might be wrong…" is a product apologizing for existing, and it depresses trust more than the abstention itself. The rule I enforce in review: abstentions state a fact about the *data*, never a fact about the model's inadequacy.

**⚠ Trap:** tuning abstention with a prompt instruction ("say you don't know if you're unsure") and shipping it. Instructed abstention is nearly uncorrelated with actual correctness — the model abstains when the question *sounds* hard, not when it lacks grounding, so you simultaneously lose easy answers and keep hard wrong ones. Abstention must be driven by a measurable gate: retrieval score below threshold, no span entails the claim, sample disagreement above threshold. The prompt is a formatting instruction, not a control mechanism.

**📐 Numbers you must know:** the coverage/risk frame in numbers. If your system answers 100% of queries at 88% accuracy, that is 12 wrong answers per 100. Abstain on the worst-scoring 15% and if those contained half the errors, you now answer 85 queries at 93% accuracy — 6 wrong answers per 100 asked, a **50% reduction in visible errors for a 15% reduction in coverage**. Whether that trade is good is a business question about the relative cost of a wrong answer versus a non-answer, and *asking the interviewer which one is more expensive in their domain* is a strong move.

### A support agent in a Sierra-style deployment hits something it can't handle. Design the handoff.

The mental model: **the handoff is not the agent's failure mode, it is the agent's most important feature**, because the entire enterprise buying decision rests on "what happens to my customers on the 20% you can't resolve." An agent with 80% containment and a great handoff outperforms one with 88% containment and a bad one, because the bad handoff produces the escalations that end up on Twitter.

**Trigger the handoff on four independent signals, not one.** (1) *Explicit request* — the user asks for a human, and this must be honored immediately and unconditionally; delaying or deflecting it is the single most hated pattern in support automation and it is often a regulatory issue in financial services. (2) *Capability boundary* — the task requires a tool or permission the agent doesn't have (issue a refund over $500, change an account holder). This is deterministic and should be a hard gate in the tool layer. (3) *Progress failure* — the agent has taken N turns without advancing state, or the user has restated the same intent twice. Repetition is the highest-precision distress signal you have and it is trivially detectable. (4) *Sentiment / risk* — frustration, threats of churn, legal or safety language. Route these hard and fast; the cost of an unnecessary human is $6, and the cost of a missed one is a churned account.

**Carry the context across.** The human must open the conversation with: a one-paragraph summary of what the customer wants, what the agent already tried, what it verified against the account, and *why it escalated*. The customer must not repeat themselves — being asked to re-explain after ten minutes with a bot is the moment the deployment's reputation is decided. This is a real engineering requirement: your agent needs a `handoff` tool whose arguments *are* the summary, generated with the full transcript in context, and written into the ticketing system's first internal note.

**Set expectations on the wait.** "Connecting you to someone — currently about 4 minutes" is a fundamentally different experience from a silent transfer. If it's outside business hours, say so and offer async with a committed response time.

**Make the seam invisible to the customer and explicit to the agent.** The customer should not have to change channels. The human agent should see a clear visual boundary between what the bot said and what they are saying, and — this is the part that earns its keep — should be able to *see the bot's proposed answer and edit it* rather than starting from a blank box. That copilot mode is where most of the human-side efficiency gain actually comes from.

**🔍 Failure taxonomy — handoff.** (1) *The loop*: human resolves, transfers back, agent doesn't know it happened, re-asks the opening question. Fix: the conversation state machine must have a terminal `human_owned` state that the agent cannot re-enter without an explicit hand-back event. (2) *The context bomb*: you paste a 4,000-token transcript into the agent's console and the human reads none of it. Fix: summary first, transcript collapsed. (3) *The silent drop*: escalation fires outside hours into a queue nobody watches. Fix: escalation must have an SLA and a monitor, exactly like any other queue you'd own. (4) *The confidence inversion*: the agent escalates on the easy 20% (unusual phrasing) and confidently handles the hard 20% (nuanced policy questions). Fix: this only shows up if you sample and review *contained* conversations, not just escalated ones — the escalated ones are already labelled, so they teach you nothing about your false negatives.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I'd instrument two numbers and defend them together: containment rate and escalated-conversation CSAT. Optimizing containment alone produces an agent that traps people, which is how these deployments get killed. The pair tells you whether you're resolving more or just refusing to let go."

### Build me the citations experience for a research product. What does a citation actually have to do to be worth showing?

Mental model: **a citation is a promise that the user can verify the claim in under five seconds.** If verification takes longer than that — if the link opens a 90-page PDF at page 1, or points at a document that contains the topic but not the claim — the citation is decoration, and decoration on a factual claim is worse than nothing because it borrows credibility it hasn't earned.

That gives you the functional requirements directly:

**Claim-level, not response-level.** A footnote at the end of a four-sentence paragraph tells you nothing about which sentence came from where. Attach markers at the sentence or clause level. This has a real engineering cost — you need the model to emit inline markers reliably, which means a strict output format and a post-hoc validator — and it is the difference between a citations feature and a citations *product*.

**Deep-link to the span, not the document.** Hover or click should open the source scrolled to and highlighting the exact passage. For PDFs this means you preserved page and bounding-box coordinates through your ingestion pipeline; for HTML it means you kept character offsets or can re-locate the span with fuzzy matching at render time. Teams that discover this requirement *after* building the ingestion pipeline end up re-ingesting the whole corpus — I would raise it in the design conversation for exactly that reason.

**Hover-to-preview before navigate.** The five-second budget is blown by a page load. Show the surrounding ~40 words in a hover card so the common case never leaves the page. This one affordance moves verification rate more than anything else you can build.

**Show the source's identity and age.** Title, author or domain, and date. Half of "the model is wrong" complaints in a research product are actually "the source is old" or "the source is a forum post," and surfacing provenance moves that judgment to the user where it belongs.

**Make the marker density honest.** If four sentences all cite the same source, the UI should say so once, not sprinkle four identical markers. Inflated citation counts read as rigour and are a form of lying with layout.

**📄 Paper:** Rashkin et al. — *Measuring Attribution in Natural Language Generation Models* — introduced the **AIS** (Attributable to Identified Sources) framework, which defines attribution as: a standalone reading of the statement is supported by the cited source. That definition is the one to use in the room, because it rules out the two failure modes people conflate — a source that is merely *topically related*, and a statement that is only true given surrounding context.

**⚠ Trap:** generating the answer and then retrieving citations for it ("post-hoc citation"). It is very easy to build, it demos beautifully, and it is structurally dishonest: you are searching for evidence that matches text the model already committed to, which is exactly the shape of motivated reasoning, and it will happily attach a plausible source to a fabricated claim. The defensible architectures are cite-while-generating (the model emits `[3]` markers referencing chunks in its context, validated post-hoc against those chunk IDs) or generate-then-*verify* (each claim is checked for entailment against its cited span and unverified claims are marked or removed). Post-hoc *retrieval* is neither.

### Why do you say an unverifiable citation is worse than no citation at all? Push on that.

Because a citation changes the user's verification behaviour, and a bad citation changes it in the wrong direction. This is the crux and it's worth being precise about the mechanism rather than asserting it as a slogan.

Without citations, users treat AI output as a draft. They apply their own skepticism, they cross-check the things that matter, and their error rate on downstream decisions reflects that. Add citation markers, and two things happen. First, **the markers themselves signal rigour** — the presence of `[1][2][3]` is read as "this system is grounded," which lowers scrutiny globally, including on the claims that have no marker. Second, **almost nobody clicks through.** Verification rates on inline citations in real products are low; users treat the marker as the verification. So you have converted "user is skeptical and checks" into "user is unskeptical and doesn't check," and if any of those citations don't support their claims, you have manufactured false confidence at scale.

The failure is compounded in domains where being wrong is expensive. A legal research product that attaches a real case name to a proposition that case does not stand for is not producing an obviously-bad output — it is producing an output that passes every superficial check a busy associate will apply, and gets caught at the worst possible moment. There is now well-publicized case law of exactly this shape reaching court filings. That is the argument to make in a Harvey or a Glean interview.

So my rule: **the citation-quality bar must be higher than the answer-quality bar, not equal to it.** Concretely, I'd rather show a claim with no marker and a visible "not found in your sources" tag than show a marker I haven't verified. And I would build the verification as a gate before render, not as an offline eval — because an offline eval tells you 4% of citations are bad and the product ships them anyway.

**How to actually verify at serving time, within budget:** for each cited claim, run an entailment check of `claim` against `cited_span`. A small cross-encoder NLI model does this in single-digit milliseconds on CPU-scale hardware; an LLM judge does it better for $0.0002 per claim. Claims that fail get their marker stripped and the sentence marked as unsupported (or regenerated). With 6 claims per answer and an LLM verifier at ~300 input tokens each, that is 1,800 tokens ≈ **$0.005/answer at $3/Mtok** — roughly 10–20% on top of a typical answer's cost, and it runs in parallel with nothing else on the critical path if you verify as you stream, per sentence.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Citations change user behaviour before they change user knowledge — the markers lower scrutiny whether or not they're correct. So I hold citation precision to a higher bar than answer accuracy and verify entailment against the cited span before render. An unsupported claim shown plainly is recoverable; an unsupported claim wearing a footnote is not."

### Your RAG answer is factually correct but the citation points to the wrong paragraph. How serious is that, and how do you fix it?

Serious, and specifically more serious than it looks in an eval spreadsheet — because your correctness metric passes and your user's trust metric fails. The first time a user clicks a marker and finds nothing relevant, they learn that your markers are noise, and that lesson generalizes to every marker in the product. One misattributed citation devalues the entire citation feature, which is why I'd treat citation precision as a page-one dashboard metric rather than a sub-metric of answer quality.

Root-cause it in order, because these have different fixes:

**1. Chunk-ID drift.** The model cited chunk 3, and chunk 3 in the rendered payload is not the chunk 3 that was in the context — because you renumbered after dedup, reranking, or a truncation step that dropped a chunk mid-pipeline. This is a plain off-by-one bug and it is by far the most common cause. Fix: never use positional indices as citation IDs. Use opaque stable chunk IDs in the prompt and resolve them at render, and assert that every emitted ID exists in the context set — a mismatch is a hard error, not a silent drop.

**2. Span localization drift.** You cited the right chunk but highlight the wrong offsets, because your chunk text was normalized (whitespace collapsed, ligatures fixed, headers stripped) and your offsets refer to the raw document. Fix: carry a character-offset mapping through normalization, or re-locate the span at render time with fuzzy matching and fall back to highlighting the whole chunk when confidence is low — a slightly-too-wide highlight is fine; a confidently wrong one is not.

**3. Genuine model misattribution.** All the plumbing is correct and the model simply attached the wrong marker — extremely common when several retrieved chunks say similar things. Fix: the entailment gate. Verify the claim against the cited span; if it fails, check the other in-context chunks and re-point the marker to one that does entail it. Re-pointing is cheap and it converts a trust-destroying error into a correct citation.

**4. The claim is synthesized across sources** and no single span supports it. This is not a bug in the marker, it is a claim that should carry two markers or be decomposed. Fix: allow multi-source markers and make your verifier check entailment against the *union* of cited spans.

**🏋 Drill (45 minutes, unaided):** build a citation-precision harness. Take 50 answers from a RAG system, extract every (claim, cited_chunk) pair, and score each pair with an entailment judge on a three-way label — supported / not supported / partially supported. Report precision per source type and per answer length. **Pass criterion:** you produce a number with a confidence interval, you can name which of the four failure classes above dominates, and your harness re-runs in under two minutes so it can go in CI. If your dominant class is (1), you have a bug, not a model problem — and finding that out from a harness rather than from a customer is the entire point.

### Draw me the confidence-versus-blast-radius matrix, and then tell me what each cell looks like as actual UI.

This is the single most useful framework in the whole product round, and I open agent-design answers with it. Two axes: **how confident the system is** that the action is right, and **how expensive it is to be wrong** — where expense means reversibility, visibility to third parties, and monetary or legal consequence. The interaction pattern falls out of the cell; it is not an independent choice.

**High confidence, low blast radius → act silently.** Reformat the code, fix the typo, categorize the transaction, apply the label. No confirmation, no notification, just a visible result and an undo. Confirmation dialogs here are pure friction and they train users to click through dialogs, which destroys your ability to use dialogs where they matter. This is the cell teams over-defend.

**High confidence, high blast radius → act, but show the diff and require one deliberate keystroke.** Sending the email, merging the PR, issuing the refund. The user should not be *composing*, they should be *approving*, and the approval must show exactly what will happen in the same units the consequence lands in ("Refund $412.50 to card •4471"). One click, but a real one.

**Low confidence, low blast radius → propose.** Ghost text, suggested edits, a draft in the compose box. This is where the bulk of AI product surface lives and where being wrong is genuinely free. Do not put a confirmation on it — the proposal *is* the confirmation.

**Low confidence, high blast radius → ask, or don't do it.** Either surface a genuine question ("I found two accounts matching Jane Doe — which one?") or refuse and hand off. The cardinal sin is the split-the-difference design: acting anyway with a warning banner nobody reads. If you're not confident and the action is expensive, the answer is a question, not a disclaimer.

Two refinements that separate a good answer from a great one. First, **blast radius is often controllable, so change it instead of the confidence.** Cannot confidently push to `main`? Push to a branch and open a PR — same work, one-tenth the blast radius, and now you're in the "act, show diff" cell. Designing the action down the axis is a much better move than adding a confirmation dialog, and it is what distinguishes a well-designed coding agent from an annoying one. Second, **the axes are per-user and per-tenant, not global.** An admin's blast radius differs from a viewer's; a sandbox tenant's differs from production. The matrix should be evaluated against the *effective* permission at call time.

**⚠ Trap:** confirmation-dialog inflation. Every dialog you add to a low-stakes action reduces the attention paid to every other dialog, including the one guarding the wire transfer. I treat confirmations as a scarce budget — roughly, no more than one per user session — and I'd push back in review on any spec that adds a second one to a reversible action.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I place every action on confidence versus blast radius. If it's cheap to be wrong, act and offer undo. If it's expensive and I'm confident, act but show a diff and take one deliberate approval. If it's expensive and I'm not confident, I ask a real question rather than shipping a warning banner. And where I can, I redesign the action to shrink its blast radius rather than adding a dialog."

### Undo, diff preview, dry run — when do you use each, and what do they cost you to build?

They are three points on one axis: **how much of the consequence do you let happen before the user gets a say.** Dry run: none. Diff preview: none, but you've computed it. Undo: all of it, reversibly. Cheapest correct choice wins, and the cost is dominated by whether your side effects are reversible.

**Undo** is the best experience when it's available, because it costs the user nothing on the happy path — no dialog, no wait, full speed — and pays only when they're unhappy. Available whenever the action is local and reversible: text edits, file changes, labels, drafts. Implementation is the standard command-pattern inverse. The engineering trap is **partial undo**: the agent made 12 edits across 5 files, and undo must revert the *turn*, not the last edit. That means grouping side effects into a transaction with a single inverse, which is exactly the unit-of-work pattern you already know; the AI-specific part is that the transaction boundary is a *conversational turn*, so your agent loop has to open and close it.

**Diff preview** is right when the action is reversible-but-annoying or the user needs to review semantics rather than outcomes — a code edit, a bulk rename, a schema migration draft. The requirement people underestimate: the diff must be at the granularity the user thinks in. A character-level diff of a rewritten paragraph is unreadable; a word-level or sentence-level diff is legible. For code you want syntax-aware hunks, and you want **per-hunk accept/reject**, because the modal outcome of an AI edit is "three of these four changes are right." A product that forces all-or-nothing acceptance throws away most of the model's value, and this is one of the clearest differentiators between coding tools that people keep using and ones they abandon.

**Dry run** is for irreversible or external side effects: sending, paying, deleting, provisioning, posting. The model produces the plan; you execute it against a simulator or with a `dry_run=true` flag and render the *predicted* effects — "would send 42 emails; 3 recipients have no address on file; total charge $1,847." Building this is genuinely expensive because it requires every tool to have a no-op mode that returns realistic results, and I would be honest about that cost in the interview: it doubles the surface area of your tool layer. The payoff is that it's the only mechanism that lets you offer high-blast-radius automation at all.

**⚠ Trap:** treating undo as a substitute for a gate on externally-visible actions. You cannot undo a sent email, a posted message, a triggered webhook, or a charged card — the "undo send" affordances that appear to do this are actually *delayed send*, which is a completely different mechanism (a 10-second hold before the side effect fires). If you propose undo for an external action in an interview, expect to be asked how, and the correct answer is "I'd implement it as a delay window, and I'd say so in the UI."

**💰 Math:** the case for diff preview in a coding agent, in money. Suppose 200 engineers, 20 agent edits/day each = 4,000 edits/day. Without per-hunk review, a wrong edit costs a full regeneration (~$0.08 in tokens, ~40 s of wall clock) plus 2 minutes of human re-reading; at a 25% wrong-edit rate that is 1,000 edits/day × 2 min = **33 engineer-hours/day**. With per-hunk accept, the 25% becomes "reject one hunk, keep three" at ~15 s = 4.2 engineer-hours/day. **Nearly 29 engineer-hours/day recovered ≈ $2.9M/year at $100/hr fully loaded**, from a UI affordance that costs one engineer a month to build. That is the kind of arithmetic that wins the design argument with a PM.

### The agent is about to do something expensive. Should it ask the user, or act and report? Give me a rule I could put in a spec.

The rule, stated so it can go in a spec: **ask when the information you're missing is something only the user has, and the cost of guessing wrong exceeds the cost of the interruption. Otherwise act, and make the result cheap to reject.**

Unpack the two clauses, because both do work. "Only the user has" excludes everything you could find out yourself — if the answer is in the repo, the docs, the database, or the conversation history, **go look**; asking a question you could have answered with a tool call is the most common and most irritating agent failure, and it reads to users as laziness. It also excludes preferences you can infer from the last five accepted actions. What genuinely qualifies: which of two ambiguous entities they meant, an intent you cannot disambiguate ("clean this up" — reformat or refactor?), an authorization you don't have, and a value judgment with no defensible default ("should this be a breaking change?").

"Cost of the interruption" is the clause candidates forget. An interruption is not free — it costs the user a context switch, it stalls a background agent for however long they take to notice, and it converts a 90-second autonomous task into a 20-minute round trip. In a background pattern the interruption cost is enormous, which is why background agents should be tuned to **act with a documented assumption** rather than to ask: "I assumed the fiscal year ends in December — see step 4." That single sentence converts a blocking question into a reviewable decision and it is the highest-leverage pattern in autonomous agent design.

So the practical policy has three tiers, and I'd write it exactly like this:

- **Look it up** — anything discoverable by a tool. Never ask.
- **Assume and annotate** — anything with a defensible default, in a low or medium blast-radius action. Act, record the assumption in the summary, and make it one click to change.
- **Ask** — genuine ambiguity with no defensible default, or any high-blast-radius action, or missing authorization. Ask *once*, batched with every other question you have, not one at a time.

That last point is worth its own emphasis: **batch your questions.** An agent that asks four sequential clarifying questions across four minutes is unusable. If you need three things, front-load a single structured ask.

**⚠ Trap:** the clarifying-question reflex, where the agent opens every task with "Just to make sure I understand…". It feels safe and it tests well with cautious reviewers, but it destroys the core value proposition — you asked the user to do the thinking you were hired to do. My review heuristic: if the clarifying question could have been resolved by reading one file, it is a bug. I'd rather ship an agent that acts on a stated assumption and is wrong 15% of the time with a one-click fix than one that asks first and is right 100% of the time twenty minutes later.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Ask only for information the user uniquely holds, and only when guessing costs more than interrupting. Everything else, act with a stated assumption and make it one click to correct — because an assumption you can see and fix is strictly better than a question you have to answer."
### What's the north-star metric for an inline suggestion product, and how would you actually instrument it?

Accept-rate — the fraction of *shown* suggestions the user takes — is the right north star for inline products, and the reason is that it is the only metric that is simultaneously high-volume, low-latency, and causally attached to the model. You get millions of samples a day, you know within seconds, and a model change moves it. Nothing else about an AI product has that property; task-completion surveys and CSAT are weeks-latent and confounded by everything.

GitHub's published telemetry work on Copilot reported that acceptance rate correlated with developers' self-reported productivity better than more elaborate derived measures did, which is a useful thing to be able to cite because it forestalls the "but is acceptance really value?" objection long enough to have the real conversation. **📅 Volatile:** treat the specific correlations as dated; the durable claim is that the simple metric held up against fancier ones.

Instrumenting it correctly is where people go wrong, and the failure is always the **denominator**. Accept-rate is accepts ÷ *shown*, and "shown" needs a real definition:

- A suggestion that arrived after the user typed past it was **not shown**. If you count it, you will pay a latency regression as an accuracy regression and chase the wrong fix for a month.
- A suggestion displayed for under ~500 ms was probably not *seen*. I log a `displayed_ms` duration and compute a second metric, accept-rate-over-seen, with a minimum-dwell filter.
- A suggestion your suppression classifier withheld was **not shown**, and must not enter the denominator — otherwise improving suppression looks like improving the model and you lose the ability to attribute either.

The event schema I'd write on day one — five events, all carrying a `suggestion_id` and a `session_id`:

```
suggestion.requested   {trigger, ctx_tokens, model, prefix_cache_hit}
suggestion.suppressed  {reason}                       # never shown; excluded from denominator
suggestion.displayed   {latency_ms, n_tokens, displayed_at}
suggestion.dismissed   {displayed_ms, reason: typed_past | escape | new_trigger}
suggestion.accepted    {displayed_ms, n_tokens_accepted}   # partial accepts count partially
```

Two subtleties worth raising unprompted. **Partial acceptance**: many editors let you accept a word or a line of a multi-line suggestion. Counting that as a full accept inflates your metric and hides a real quality signal — the model is right for one line and wrong for the next three. Log tokens-accepted-of-tokens-shown. **Segment by trigger context**: acceptance after a newline in an empty function body is structurally different from acceptance mid-expression. A model change that helps one and hurts the other shows as flat in aggregate, which is how teams ship regressions.

**⚠ Trap:** optimizing accept-rate directly. It has a trivial degenerate solution — show fewer, safer, shorter suggestions. Suppress everything hard and only complete `for i in range(` and your accept-rate goes to 60% while the product's value collapses. Accept-rate must always be reported alongside **suggestions shown per active hour** and **accepted tokens per active hour**; the last of those is the closest thing to a true value metric, because it measures how much text the product actually contributed.

### Why is edit-distance-after-accept a better quality signal than accept-rate, and how do you compute it?

Because accept-rate measures whether the suggestion looked right in the half-second before Tab; edit-distance-after-accept measures whether it *was* right. Those diverge constantly, and the gap between them is the most honest quality number in the product.

The mechanism: after an accept, watch the accepted region for a retention window and measure how much of it survives. Concretely, capture the exact character range inserted, track it through subsequent edits (you need stable anchors — a marker range or an operational-transform mapping, not raw offsets, because edits above shift everything below), and at the window boundary compute the normalized edit distance between what you inserted and what remains. Retention = 1 − normalized distance.

Window choice matters and is worth stating: 30 seconds captures immediate rejection, 5 minutes captures "I kept typing and then fixed it," and one commit-cycle captures whether it survived to production. I'd instrument all three at different sampling rates — the 30-second one at 100% because it's cheap, the commit-boundary one at a sample because it needs a git hook.

Why this is the better signal: it is **immune to the degenerate strategy** that breaks accept-rate. You cannot game retention by suggesting `for i in range(` — trivial suggestions are retained but contribute almost no tokens, so `retained_tokens_per_hour` (retention × accepted tokens) has no cheap exploit. It also gives you a per-suggestion quality label that requires no human annotation, which means you can build an eval set out of production traffic: high-accept-low-retention suggestions are exactly the confidently-wrong cases you want in your regression suite.

**📐 Numbers you must know:** the shape of a healthy inline funnel, as ratios rather than absolutes since the absolutes vary by language and product. Roughly: **of suggestions requested, ~60–80% are shown** (the rest suppressed or too late); **of shown, ~20–30% accepted**; **of accepted, ~70–80% retained materially unchanged at 5 minutes**. Multiply through and roughly **15–20% of requests turn into retained code**. The reason to hold these as ratios is that the arithmetic tells you where to invest: if your show-rate is 45% you have a latency problem, not a model problem, and no amount of model work will fix it.

**⚠ Trap:** measuring edit distance on the raw string. Reformatting by a linter or a formatter-on-save shows as a massive edit and will make your best suggestions look worst — this bites hardest in Python (Black) and Go (gofmt). Normalize before diffing: run both sides through the formatter, or diff on the token/AST level. Teams have shipped whole quality "improvements" that were actually just formatter noise.

### Walk me through regenerate rate and abandonment. What do they tell you that accept-rate can't?

They are your two **dissatisfaction** metrics, and they matter because accept-rate and its cousins only measure the happy path — they are silent about the users who got something and hated it.

**Regenerate rate** — the fraction of responses where the user hits regenerate, rephrases the same intent, or asks a near-duplicate follow-up. It is the cleanest available signal of "wrong but I still believe you can do it," which is a much more actionable state than churn. It is also unusually cheap to compute and unusually easy to misread. Two decompositions I'd insist on:

- **Regenerate-with-edit vs regenerate-as-is.** Editing the prompt means "I asked badly"; hitting regenerate unchanged means "you answered badly." Only the second is a model signal. Conflating them is the standard mistake and it will send you optimizing the model when you have a prompt-affordance problem.
- **Regenerate-then-accept vs regenerate-then-leave.** The first is a healthy product where retry works; the second is a failure the user paid for twice.

**Abandonment** — the user got a response and did nothing with it. No copy, no accept, no follow-up, session ends. This is the most under-instrumented metric in AI products because it requires you to define "did something," which forces you to decide what value looks like — and that is exactly why it's valuable to raise in a product round. In a chat product, abandonment after a long response usually means the response was long *and* wrong; the length made it expensive to discover the wrongness.

What they tell you that accept-rate cannot: **the distribution of failure across users rather than across requests.** A 25% regenerate rate spread evenly is a mediocre model; a 25% regenerate rate concentrated in 8% of users is a *segment* failure — a language, a domain, a document type, a tenant whose corpus doesn't retrieve well. Those two situations have completely different fixes and aggregate accept-rate cannot distinguish them. The rule I enforce: every quality metric is reported at p50 *per user*, not just in aggregate, because AI quality failures cluster by user far more than backend failures do.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I'd pair one success metric with two dissatisfaction metrics: accept-rate for the happy path, regenerate-unchanged for 'you were wrong,' and abandonment for 'you were wrong and I gave up.' And I'd look at all three per-user rather than per-request, because AI failures concentrate in segments — one language, one document type, one tenant — and aggregates hide that completely."

### For a customer-support agent, what do you measure? Give me the metric tree.

The top of the tree is **resolution rate** — the fraction of conversations where the customer's problem was actually solved without a human — and everything else exists to make that number trustworthy, because the naive version of it is trivially gameable and every vendor in the category games it.

The gameable version is **containment**: conversations that didn't reach a human. An agent maximizes containment by being hard to escape, and the customer's problem going unsolved counts as a success. So containment is an input, never the goal. The tree:

**Level 1 — Resolution rate.** Contained *and* resolved. Determining "resolved" is the hard part and there are three practical estimators, which I'd use together: (a) **no re-contact within 7 days on the same issue** — the strongest signal, but 7 days latent; (b) **an outcome event** in the system of record — the refund posted, the address changed, the subscription cancelled — which is instant and exact but only exists for transactional intents; (c) **an LLM judge over the transcript** with a rubric, calibrated against a human-labelled sample — instant, covers everything, and drifts if you don't re-calibrate.

**Level 2 — the three things that make Level 1 honest.** *Escalation rate* with its inverse relationship to containment. *Escalated CSAT*, because a good handoff must not be a bad experience. *Re-contact rate*, which catches false resolutions directly.

**Level 3 — diagnosis.** Intent-level resolution (which of your 40 intents are failing?), turns-to-resolution (rising means the agent is grinding), tool error rate, and **time-to-first-meaningful-response**, which for support means the first turn that advances the conversation rather than a greeting.

**Level 4 — the business number.** Cost per resolved conversation, all-in. This is the number the buyer signs on and it is the one to name in an interview.

**💰 Math:** worth having ready. A human-handled ticket costs roughly $5–$8 fully loaded (call it $6). An agent conversation at 12 turns, with context growing 2k → 14k tokens, bills roughly the sum of prefixes ≈ 90k input tokens plus 3k output; at $3/$15 per Mtok that is 90,000×3/1e6 + 3,000×15/1e6 = $0.27 + $0.045 = **$0.315**, or about **$0.10** with 90% prefix-cache hits at a 10% read rate. At 60% resolution, cost per *resolved* conversation = $0.10 ÷ 0.6 = **$0.167**, plus the 40% that escalate and still cost $6 each. Blended: 0.6×$0.167 + 0.4×($0.10 + $6) = $0.10 + $2.44 = **$2.54 per conversation vs $6.00** — a 58% reduction. Notice what this arithmetic shows: **the escalated conversations dominate the blended cost**, so moving resolution from 60% to 70% saves far more than making the model 10× cheaper. That is the insight to deliver, not the total.

**⚠ Trap:** reporting resolution rate without a denominator definition that includes the conversations the agent refused to start. If your router sends "hard-looking" conversations straight to humans and you compute resolution over the remainder, you have measured your router, not your agent, and the number will look excellent while the deployment saves nothing.

### Our model scores 8 points higher on the benchmark suite but every product metric is flat. Explain that to me.

Because benchmark score and product metric measure different distributions, different tasks, and different failure costs, and the mapping between them is weak enough that I treat "benchmark up, product flat" as the *expected* outcome rather than an anomaly. Four independent reasons, and naming them in order is a strong answer.

**1. Distribution mismatch.** Benchmarks are curated, well-formed, self-contained problems. Your production traffic is truncated pastes, ambiguous pronouns, half-written functions, mixed languages, and questions whose answer is "that's not in your workspace." The head of your traffic distribution is often *trivially easy* — 40% of real chat traffic is short, formulaic, and already at 95% — so improvements on hard curated problems move a tail you barely serve. Compute the fraction of your traffic that the old model already handled correctly; the ceiling on any improvement is one minus that number.

**2. The bottleneck isn't the model.** In a RAG product, if retrieval misses the right chunk 22% of the time, a better generator cannot fix those; you are measuring the model's contribution to a pipeline where it is not the constraint. I'd ask for the retrieval recall@k number before I looked at any model comparison. Same story for latency-bound inline products, where a model that is 8 points better and 300 ms slower is a *product regression*.

**3. The failure modes that hurt you aren't what benchmarks score.** Benchmarks score correctness. Products die of tone, verbosity, formatting, refusals on legitimate requests, and confidently-wrong-with-a-citation. A model can improve accuracy by 8 points and simultaneously become 40% more verbose — which in an inline product tanks accept-rate, because a 12-line suggestion is harder to evaluate in 300 ms than a 3-line one.

**4. Contamination and overfitting.** Public benchmarks leak into training data, and the vendor optimized against them. This is not cynicism, it is the base rate. Any benchmark that has been public for two years is measuring memorization to an unknown degree.

**What I do instead, and this is the part they're grading:** maintain a **product-traffic eval** — a few hundred examples sampled from real usage, stratified by intent and by segment, with graded rubrics tied to the product metric. And run an **online A/B on the product metric itself**, because that is the only measurement that is definitionally causal. Offline eval is for catching regressions fast; online is for deciding.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I'd expect that. Benchmarks measure curated hard problems; my product metric is dominated by easy traffic where the old model was already right, and by failure modes benchmarks don't score — verbosity, tone, refusals, and retrieval misses that no generator can fix. Before shipping a model I want a stratified eval built from my own traffic and an online A/B on accept-rate, because offline score has never predicted it for me."

### Design the telemetry schema for an AI feature. What do you log on every request, and what do you deliberately not log?

The mental model: **your telemetry is your future training set, your future eval set, and your incident-response tool, and you get exactly one chance to have logged the right fields.** Retrofitting a missing field means waiting weeks for data. So I over-log identifiers and under-log content, and I make the content decision explicit and configurable per tenant.

Every request carries a **trace hierarchy**: `session_id` → `turn_id` → `generation_id` → `tool_call_id`, all opaque and all propagated to the client so a user-reported issue maps to a row. This is the single most valuable thing on the list; without it, "the answer was wrong yesterday" is unfixable.

**Per generation, always:**
```
model_id, model_version, prompt_template_id + version, sampling params (temp, top_p, seed),
retrieval: {query, k, chunk_ids[], scores[], retriever_version, index_snapshot_id},
tokens: {input, cached_input, output, reasoning}, cost_usd,
timings: {queue_ms, ttft_ms, total_ms, itl_p50_ms}, finish_reason,
flags: {safety_fired, abstained, degraded_mode, fallback_model_used}
```
`index_snapshot_id` and `prompt_template_id` are the two people forget, and they are the two you need most: without them "it got worse on Tuesday" cannot be attributed to the reindex or the prompt tweak, and it is *always* one of those two.

**Per turn, the outcome events** — accepted, edited, copied, regenerated, thumbs, escalated, abandoned — joined to the generation. A generation log without outcome joins is a bill, not telemetry.

**What I deliberately don't log, or log conditionally:** raw user content by default in a multi-tenant enterprise product. Instead: content hashes plus lengths plus a per-tenant opt-in flag for full-content retention, with a TTL. This is not just compliance theatre — the ability to say "prompts are retained for 30 days only with your DPA opt-in" is frequently a *sales* requirement at Glean, Harvey and Ramp, and if you build unconditional logging you will be re-plumbing it under deadline. I also don't log raw reasoning traces to the same store as application logs, because they contain everything the redactor was supposed to strip.

**⚠ Trap:** sampling your logs at 1% to control cost, and thereby losing exactly the rare failures you needed. LLM failures are long-tailed and low-frequency; a 1% sample of a 0.4% failure mode gives you four examples a day and no ability to reason about it. The right shape is **outcome-stratified sampling**: 100% of turns with a negative outcome (thumbs-down, regenerate, escalation, abandonment, safety fire), 100% of turns whose confidence was near a threshold, and 1–5% of the boring successes. Storage cost falls almost as much and the dataset becomes useful.

**💰 Math:** at 5M generations/day with ~1.5 KB of structured metadata each, full metadata logging is 7.5 GB/day = 225 GB/month — trivially affordable in any columnar store (single-digit dollars per month in object storage, tens of dollars queryable). Full *content* logging at ~12 KB/generation is 60 GB/day = 1.8 TB/month, which is still only a few hundred dollars but is a much bigger compliance surface. The cost argument for dropping content is weak; the compliance argument is strong. Make the argument you actually believe.

### Talk to me about latency masking — optimistic UI, speculative prefetch, filler content. What's in your toolkit?

Latency masking is spending compute or risk to buy back perceived time, and the toolkit is bigger for AI products than for CRUD because generation is speculative-friendly in ways a database write is not.

**Optimistic UI.** Render the consequence before the server confirms it. In AI products the honest applications are narrow: echo the user's message immediately, show the accepted suggestion in the buffer before the accept is persisted, mark the thumbs-up before it's written. Do *not* optimistically render model output you haven't received — that's fabrication, not optimism.

**Speculative prefetch.** This is where the real wins are. The pattern: start the expensive work before the user commits to it, on a signal that predicts commitment. Concretely — fire retrieval on the user's partial query while they're still typing (they type for 4 seconds; retrieval takes 300 ms; the search is free by the time they hit enter). Warm the prefix cache with the system prompt and the document context the instant the user opens the panel, so the first real request hits a warm cache. On a selection-action palette, prefetch the top-1 verb's result on hover, before the click. In an agent, start the next likely tool call while the model is still emitting the tokens that will request it.

**Speculative generation on branch points.** Where there are two likely continuations and both are cheap, run both and discard one. This is the same trade as branch prediction and the same math: worth it when `p(hit) × latency_saved > cost_of_the_wasted_branch`.

**Filler content that is real.** "Searching your workspace…", "Found 8 documents", the retrieved source cards rendered *before* the answer starts. This is my favourite because it isn't a mask at all — it's genuine information delivered during a wait, so it improves both perceived latency and the user's ability to judge the answer that follows. Perplexity's showing of sources during generation is the canonical example: the sources are useful *and* they eat 800 ms.

**Progressive rendering of partial results.** Show the outline, then fill sections. Show the first three of ten results as they rank. Show the diff header before the hunks.

**💰 Math:** speculative retrieval on partial query. Suppose retrieval costs $0.0004/call (embedding + vector search) and you fire it 3 times per query as the user types, with a 70% chance the last speculative result is reusable. Cost goes from $0.0004 to $0.0012 per query, +$0.0008. At 2M queries/day that is **$1,600/day = $48k/month** — not free. But it removes ~300 ms from TTFT on 70% of queries. Whether $48k/month is worth 300 ms is a real business question, and the right answer is to only speculate when the partial query is stable for ~250 ms (which cuts the speculative fires from 3 to ~1.2 and the cost to **$16k/month**) and to cache aggressively on the embedding, since prefixes repeat. Showing that you'd tune the trigger rather than accept the naive cost is the point.

### Where does latency masking cross the line into dishonesty?

There's a clean test, and I'd give it as a principle rather than a list: **masking is honest when it changes what the user perceives about the wait, and dishonest when it changes what the user believes about the world.** Making a wait feel shorter is legitimate product craft. Making the user believe work happened that didn't, or that a result is more certain than it is, is deception — and in AI products it is also usually a bug factory.

Concrete lines I hold:

**Fake progress bars — over the line.** A percentage you computed from a timer is a claim about how much work remains, and it's false. Users calibrate on it and then distrust every progress indicator you ship.

**Artificial delay to signal effort — over the line, and disturbingly common.** Some products deliberately slow a fast response so it "feels thorough," or run a visible "thinking" animation for a fixed 2 seconds on a cached answer. This is manipulating the user's quality judgment with fabricated evidence. It also has a nasty second-order effect: you have made your product permanently slower and you can never speed it up without looking broken.

**Fabricated status text — over the line.** "Analyzing 47 documents" when you retrieved 8 is lying. "Consulting the knowledge base" when you made a single embedding call and it missed is lying. Status text must correspond to events.

**Streaming a response you've already fully generated, at a simulated typing speed — depends.** If you're smoothing chunky network delivery into an even token cadence, that's rendering polish and it's fine. If you generated the whole answer in 200 ms and are dribbling it out over 6 seconds to look like a chatbot, that's theatre and I'd argue against it — though I'd note honestly that many shipped products do exactly this and defend it as consistency of experience. The line I'd hold: never make the total *longer* than the real generation took.

**Optimistically rendering model output — over the line, always.** Predicting what the model will say and rendering it before it says it produces flicker, and worse, occasionally shows the user text the model never produced. If they screenshot it, it's yours.

**Hiding failures inside the mask — over the line and dangerous.** A retry that silently swaps to a weaker fallback model while the same "Thinking…" indicator runs means the user cannot tell they got a degraded answer. If quality changed, say so — even a subtle "answered in fast mode" chip is enough.

**⚠ Trap:** the "confidence theatre" gradient. Teams rarely decide to be dishonest; they add one small thing (a 400 ms minimum display time so the spinner doesn't flash), then another (a status line that's mostly true), and end up with a system whose visible behaviour is decoupled from its actual behaviour. The guardrail I put in review is procedural: **every user-visible status string must be emitted by the component that does the work**, never by the presentation layer on a timer. That single rule makes the dishonest version structurally hard to build.

### A user asks "why did it give me a different answer this time?" What do you tell them, and what do you build?

First, be honest with yourself about the causes, because there are more than one and they have very different fixes.

**Sampling.** Temperature > 0 means the output is a draw. Obvious, and the only one users guess.

**Nondeterminism at temperature 0.** This surprises people and it's a great thing to know in a room. Greedy decoding is not bit-reproducible on GPUs in practice, because floating-point reductions are not associative and the *order* of accumulation changes with batch composition, kernel selection, and tensor-parallel layout. Two identical requests batched with different neighbours can take different reduction paths, produce logits that differ in the last bits, and — at a near-tie between two tokens — diverge into completely different completions. So "set temperature to 0 for reproducibility" is a misconception worth correcting explicitly: it reduces variance enormously but does not eliminate it.

**Everything around the model.** The retrieval index changed. The system prompt was edited. The vendor silently updated the model behind a floating alias. Your router sent this request to a different model under load. Conversation history differs by one turn. In my experience these cause *more* user-visible inconsistency than sampling does, and they're the ones you can actually fix.

**What I tell the user:** something short, true, and non-defensive. "Answers are generated fresh each time, so wording varies. If you want a specific answer to stay put, you can pin it." Do not explain floating-point associativity to a user. Do explain it to the interviewer.

**What I build**, in priority order:

1. **Pinning / saving.** The user can freeze a specific response — stored verbatim, with its sources, timestamp and model version — and reference it later. This solves 80% of the felt problem, because what users actually want is not determinism, it's *not losing the good answer*.
2. **Version stamping on the response.** A small "generated with model X on date Y from N sources" affordance. It turns "the product is flaky" into "the sources changed," which is a comprehensible and often correct explanation.
3. **A pinned model alias in your own config**, never the vendor's floating one, with model upgrades as deliberate, evaluated deploys. This is the engineering discipline that prevents the whole class.
4. **Caching for exact repeats.** If the same user asks the identical question within a session, serve the same answer. Cheap, and it removes the most jarring instance of the complaint.
5. **Seeded sampling where the API supports it**, understanding it is best-effort, not a guarantee.

**⚠ Trap:** promising determinism in an enterprise contract or a compliance document. "The same input always produces the same output" is a commitment you cannot honour across a model version change, a batch-composition change, or an index update, and legal will hold you to it. The correct commitment is **reproducibility of the record** — you can always show exactly what was generated, from what sources, by what model version — which is what auditors actually want and which you can genuinely deliver.

### What should the regenerate button actually do? Same prompt again, or something else?

Naively re-running the identical request at the same temperature is the worst option, because the most likely outcome is a response that is different in wording and identical in substance — which reads to the user as "the product ignored me." The user pressed regenerate to express dissatisfaction; your job is to *change something*, and the design question is what.

My ladder, in the order I'd apply it:

**First regenerate: change the sampling.** Raise temperature modestly, or use a different seed, and — better — re-run with instructions to take a different approach. Cheap, fast, and it produces a genuinely different answer rather than a paraphrase.

**Second regenerate: change the pipeline, not just the sample.** The user has now told you twice that you're wrong, and the problem is probably upstream of the sampler. Re-run retrieval with a rewritten query, widen k, disable a filter, or escalate to a stronger model. This is exactly the place to spend money — the user's willingness to pay attention is at its peak and their patience is at its floor.

**Third: stop regenerating and ask.** Two failures means your understanding of the request is wrong. Offer a structured clarification or a set of alternative interpretations rather than a third dice roll.

Then the affordances around it. **Give the user a reason picker**, optional and one click: "too long / wrong / missing something / wrong tone." This is the single best labelled-feedback source you will ever build, because the user is already motivated and the categories are actionable — and it lets you condition the regeneration on the reason, which visibly improves the result and closes the loop. Users who see that the reason changed the answer keep using it.

**Keep the old response.** Regenerate must never destroy the previous answer; render alternatives as siblings the user can flip between. Destroying a partly-good answer to produce a worse one is enraging, and it is the reason many products' regenerate button is used once and never again.

**Bill honestly.** Regeneration costs tokens. In a usage-priced product, decide explicitly whether the second attempt is charged, and tell the user. My default: don't charge for the first regenerate of a turn. It costs you a few percent of tokens and it removes the "why am I paying for your mistakes" objection entirely.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Regenerate should escalate, not repeat. First press changes the sample and the approach; second press changes retrieval or the model; third press should stop guessing and ask a clarifying question. And I'd attach an optional one-click reason, because that's the highest-quality label I can collect and it lets me condition the retry on it, so the user sees their feedback do something."

### How do you capture feedback that produces usable labels without annoying people, and what's wrong with thumbs data?

Start with what's wrong with thumbs, because that framing is what the question is really probing.

**The response rate is ~1% and it is not a random 1%.** Thumbs data is a self-selected sample with a strong negativity bias for functional tools (people report failures, not successes) and, confusingly, a positivity bias in consumer chat (people who enjoy the product engage with everything, including the buttons). You cannot compute a quality rate from it. If your dashboard shows "92% positive," that means 92% of the 1% who clicked, and the number is a measure of your rating population, not your quality.

**The label is ambiguous.** A thumbs-down can mean wrong, too long, wrong tone, refused, slow, or "I disagree with a correct answer." Without a reason, it's an unusable training or eval label — you cannot tell which axis to improve, and mixing these into a preference dataset actively degrades it.

**Rater drift and gaming.** Power users click for different reasons than new users; a small number of users generate most of the clicks.

So I treat explicit feedback as a **triage queue, not a metric**, and I build the metric out of **implicit signals**, which have three enormous advantages: they're ~100% coverage, they're unbiased by willingness-to-click, and they measure behaviour rather than opinion. The implicit set: accepted, copied, edited-then-kept, exported, followed a citation, regenerated, rephrased, abandoned, escalated. In a coding product, "did it survive to commit" is worth more than any survey.

Then I add explicit feedback in the three places where it's genuinely cheap for the user:

1. **Attached to an action they were taking anyway** — the reason picker on regenerate, the "why" chip on a rejected diff. Zero incremental interruption.
2. **Micro-surveys sampled at ~1 in 200 turns**, one question, dismissible, and — critically — *stratified*, so you oversample the segments and intents where you have the least signal instead of asking the same happy power users forever.
3. **A structured escape hatch** — "report a problem" — that captures the full trace context automatically. Low volume, extremely high value, and it must actually route to a human who fixes things, or it becomes theatre.

**⚠ Trap:** training or evaluating on thumbs data without correcting for selection. If you build your eval set from thumbs-down examples, you have built a set that over-represents the failure modes users bother to report and under-represents the silent ones — which are usually the dangerous ones, because a confidently-wrong-but-plausible answer generates *no* thumbs-down at all. It got accepted. The failures that most need finding are invisible to your feedback widget by construction, and that argument — that your feedback channel is blind to your worst failure mode — is the one that wins this conversation in a design review.

**🏋 Drill (30 minutes, unaided):** write the feedback taxonomy for a product you know. Enumerate every implicit signal your client could emit, map each to the quality dimension it evidences, and mark which ones are confounded (e.g. "copied" is confounded by users who copy everything). Then state the one explicit question you'd ask, where you'd ask it, and at what sampling rate. **Pass criterion:** at least eight implicit signals, an explicit acknowledgement of which are confounded and how, and a sampling scheme that is stratified rather than uniform.
### The user starts typing while the model is still streaming. What should happen — and how does the answer change for voice?

Mental model: **interruption is the user telling you your output has negative value from here on.** The only wrong response is to keep going. Everything else is a question of how gracefully you stop and what you do with the partial output.

**In text chat**, my default: typing does *not* interrupt — the user may be composing a follow-up while reading — but hitting send does. The send action stops generation immediately, keeps the partial response in the transcript clearly marked as stopped, and includes it in the context of the next turn. That last part is the one people get wrong. If the user interrupts with "no, in Python" and you drop the partial response from context, the model has no idea what "no" referred to and produces something unrelated. The partial output is a real conversational turn and must be treated as one.

A stop button must exist, must be the same button as send (so the target doesn't move), and must be instantaneous on the client — do not wait for the server to acknowledge before stopping the text.

**In voice**, everything hardens, because in speech overlapping talk is a hard social error and latency tolerance collapses. Requirements:

- **Barge-in must be default-on and near-instant.** When the user's speech is detected, TTS playback stops within roughly 100–200 ms. Any longer and you get the "talking over each other" experience that makes voice agents feel broken.
- **You must handle the false trigger.** Backchannels — "mhm", "right", "yeah" — are not interruptions, they're the listener signalling engagement, and stopping on them makes the agent seem skittish. So the barge-in detector needs to distinguish backchannel from interruption, usually by duration plus a light semantic check. This is the detail that separates people who have shipped voice from people who have read about it.
- **Track what was actually heard, not what was generated.** If the agent had produced 40 words of TTS and was interrupted after 12, the conversation state must record those 12 — the user cannot respond to words they never heard. Feeding the full generated turn into context makes the agent reference things it never said, which is deeply confusing.
- **Endpointing is a product decision, not a VAD parameter.** Too-eager end-of-turn detection cuts people off mid-thought; too-lazy produces dead air. The tuning target is different per domain: someone reciting an account number pauses a lot, someone chatting does not. Adaptive silence thresholds by conversational state is the real answer.

**📐 Numbers you must know:** the voice-agent latency budget, end to end, because it gets asked directly. Human turn-taking gaps in natural conversation average around **200 ms**, and beyond roughly **500 ms** a pause reads as hesitation or a bad connection. Budget: VAD endpointing 100–300 ms + ASR finalization 50–150 ms + LLM TTFT 200–400 ms + TTS first audio 100–200 ms ≈ **450–1,050 ms**. That arithmetic is why voice pipelines stream at every stage and why they use small fast models — you have less than a second total and four components want to spend it.

**⚠ Trap:** treating cancellation as a client-only concern. If you stop rendering but the server keeps generating, you pay for every token, you keep a batch slot occupied (degrading latency for everyone else), and any tool the agent was about to call still fires. Cancellation must propagate: client abort → server request-disconnect handler → generation loop checks a cancel flag → scheduler frees the KV blocks → in-flight tool calls are cancelled or compensated.

### Walk me through cancellation end to end. What happens to the tokens, the bill, and the side effects?

This is the question where backend instincts pay off directly, because it is a distributed-cancellation problem with an unusual twist: **the work is billable per unit as it happens, and some of it is irreversible.**

**The tokens.** Generation is a loop; cancellation is a flag the loop checks each step. With an HTTP/SSE transport, client disconnect surfaces server-side as a closed connection — in an ASGI app, that's a `receive()` yielding `http.disconnect`, or in practice `asyncio.CancelledError` propagating into your generator when the client goes away. You must actually handle it: wrap the generation loop so that on cancellation you break, release the sequence from the scheduler, and free its KV blocks. If you don't, a cancelled request keeps consuming a batch slot and GPU memory for its full `max_tokens` — which is exactly how a product with heavy cancellation (inline completion, chat with an itchy stop button) develops mysterious latency degradation under load with no traffic increase.

**The bill.** You are charged for tokens generated, not tokens delivered. With a vendor API, cancelling an in-flight stream typically still bills what was produced up to the abort — and if you cancel by dropping the connection without the SDK signalling abort, you may be billed for the whole completion. So: use the SDK's cancellation path, and record `tokens_billed` from your own streaming counter rather than assuming a cancelled request is free. This matters for the accept-rate arithmetic in an inline product, where the *majority* of requests are cancelled.

**The side effects.** Hardest part, and the one that separates a good answer. A turn may have already fired tool calls. Cancellation must classify them:

- **Pure reads** (search, fetch, query): cancel freely, discard results.
- **Idempotent writes** (upsert a label, set a status): safe to let complete, or safe to retry later; let them finish rather than adding compensation complexity.
- **Non-idempotent externally-visible actions** (send email, charge card, post message): these must never be in a cancellable window in the first place. The correct design is that such actions sit behind an approval gate or a short delay-and-commit window, so cancellation is a matter of not committing rather than compensating. If you find yourself designing a saga to un-send an email, back up — the architecture is wrong.

**The user-visible contract.** Say what happened. "Stopped. 2 files were already modified." Silent cancellation that leaves partial state is the worst outcome; the user believes nothing happened and it did.

```python
# The shape that matters: a cancel scope around generation whose cleanup is guaranteed.
async def stream_turn(req, sink):
    seq = scheduler.admit(req)
    billed = 0
    try:
        async for tok in engine.generate(seq):
            billed += 1
            await sink.send(tok)          # raises CancelledError if client is gone
    except asyncio.CancelledError:
        await sink.send_event("stopped", {"tokens": billed})
        raise
    finally:
        scheduler.release(seq)            # free KV blocks unconditionally
        meter.record(req.tenant, billed)  # bill what was produced, not what was delivered
```

**⚠ Trap:** implementing stop as "hide the output." I have reviewed this more than once — the client sets a flag and stops rendering while the stream runs to completion in the background. The demo is indistinguishable from a correct implementation. The bill, the GPU occupancy, and the tool side effects are not.

### Your product works well in English. How do you find out how badly it works in Japanese and Hindi before your customers tell you?

The mental model: **quality is not a scalar property of your system, it is a property of the system-language pair, and the variance across languages is larger than the variance across model versions.** Teams routinely ship a model upgrade that is +3% overall and −15% in Turkish, because the eval set was 92% English.

Where the degradation actually comes from — and enumerating these is the substance of the answer, because each has a different fix:

**1. The model itself.** Pretraining data is heavily English-skewed, so instruction-following, factuality and formatting adherence are all weaker in lower-resource languages. Nothing you can do at the application layer fixes this; you can only measure it and choose a model that's better for your language mix.

**2. Tokenization economics.** This is the one that surprises people and it's concrete. Byte-level BPE vocabularies trained mostly on Latin script fragment other scripts badly. The same semantic content can cost **1.5–3× more tokens** in Japanese, Hindi, Thai or Arabic than in English. That has three consequences at once: the request costs more, it's *slower* (more decode steps for the same content), and it eats more of your context window — so your RAG system silently retrieves fewer documents for Hindi users than for English users at the same token budget. **📅 Volatile:** exact ratios are tokenizer-specific; measure yours rather than quoting a number.

**3. Retrieval.** Multilingual embedding models are weaker cross-lingually, your chunker may split on English sentence rules, and your corpus may simply have less content in that language. In my experience retrieval, not generation, is the dominant cause of non-English quality collapse in enterprise RAG — and it's the one you can actually fix.

**4. The prompt.** An English system prompt tends to pull the output toward English. Explicit "respond in the user's language" instructions help but leak — you'll get English headings on a Japanese answer, or English tool-call reasoning bleeding into the response.

**5. The product layer.** Your guardrail classifiers, PII detectors, and safety filters were probably trained on English. A safety classifier that under-fires in Hindi is a real risk surface; one that over-fires produces mysterious refusals for a whole market.

**How I find out:** a **stratified eval by language**, sized so that each language you actually serve has a statistically meaningful bucket — not a proportional sample, which gives you 11 Japanese examples. Translate a core eval set with human review (machine-translated evals mostly measure your translator), and add native-authored examples for the idiomatic cases. Then report **every product metric by language, always** — accept-rate by language, regenerate rate by language, abstention rate by language. The dashboard cut is the whole discipline; without it, nobody looks.

**💰 Math:** the tokenization tax made concrete. Suppose a support agent with 12-turn conversations, ~90k billed input tokens in English at $3/Mtok = $0.27. At a 2.2× token ratio for Japanese, the same conversation is ~198k tokens = **$0.594** — 120% more expensive for identical value delivered. If Japan is 15% of your volume at 1M conversations/month, that is 150k × ($0.594 − $0.27) = **$48,600/month of pure tokenizer tax**. It also means Japanese conversations hit your context limit ~2.2× sooner, so your truncation policy fires earlier and quality degrades first in exactly the market you were least measuring.

### What breaks when you stream generated text into a right-to-left or mixed-direction layout?

Specific, unglamorous, and it will absolutely appear in a product bug queue at any company shipping globally — which is why it's a good question and why a confident answer stands out.

**Bidirectional resolution is a whole-paragraph operation, and streaming gives you partial paragraphs.** The Unicode bidi algorithm decides the visual order of a run of text based on its surrounding context. When you append tokens one at a time, previously-rendered text can *legitimately change its visual position* as new text arrives — an Arabic sentence with an embedded English product name or a number will reflow as the paragraph completes. The user sees characters jumping around, which looks like a rendering bug. The mitigation: set an explicit paragraph direction rather than relying on auto-detection mid-stream (`dir="rtl"` on the container, or `dir="auto"` computed once from the first strong directional character and then *frozen* for that paragraph), and buffer to a sentence boundary before painting if the jitter is severe.

**Punctuation and brackets flip.** Neutral characters take direction from their context, so a trailing period or a closing parenthesis rendered before its Arabic context arrives will sit on the wrong side and then move. Same class of problem, same fix.

**Mixed-direction code blocks.** Code is LTR even inside an RTL document. If you let the container direction cascade into a `<pre>`, you get syntactically-correct code displayed in an order that is unreadable. Force `dir="ltr"` and `text-align: left` on code, tables of identifiers, URLs, and file paths, unconditionally.

**Layout mirroring versus content direction.** The chat UI itself should mirror — avatars, timestamps, the send button, message alignment — using logical CSS properties (`margin-inline-start`, `padding-inline-end`, `inset-inline-start`) rather than left/right. Icons with direction semantics (back arrows, reply, indent) must mirror; icons without (a clock, a checkmark, a play button) must not. Getting this wrong is the visible tell that RTL was an afterthought.

**Markdown parsing.** List markers, blockquote bars and heading anchors need to sit on the correct side, and many streaming markdown renderers hard-code left.

**The streaming cursor.** A blinking caret hard-positioned to the right of the last character is wrong in RTL.

**⚠ Trap:** testing RTL by machine-translating your UI strings and eyeballing it. That catches mirroring and misses every bidi issue, because your translated strings are pure RTL with no embedded Latin. The tests that find real bugs use **mixed content** — Arabic or Hebrew prose containing English brand names, numbers, URLs, and inline code — because directional mixing is where the algorithm actually does work and where streaming breaks it.

### How do you make streamed content accessible? A screen reader user is on your chat product right now.

The core problem in one sentence: **a screen reader announces changes to live regions, and token-by-token streaming produces hundreds of changes per response.** Naively marking the response container `aria-live="polite"` produces either a stuttering announcement of fragments or, with `assertive`, an unusable machine-gun of interruptions. So the honest answer is that **you must decouple what you paint from what you announce.**

The design I'd ship:

**Announce at semantic boundaries, not token boundaries.** Maintain a separate visually-hidden live region that receives *complete sentences* (or paragraphs) as they finalize, while the visible region streams freely. The screen reader user hears coherent units; the sighted user sees fluid streaming. This is the key architectural move and it costs about thirty lines.

**Use `aria-live="polite"` and never `assertive` for model output.** Assertive interrupts whatever the user is currently reading, including their own message. Polite queues.

**Announce state transitions as short, discrete messages**: "Generating response", "Searching your workspace", "Response complete, 240 words", "Stopped". The completion announcement is the one people forget and the one users need most — without it there is no way to know the response ended versus stalled.

**Make the whole flow keyboard-operable, including stop.** The stop button must be reachable and focusable while streaming, and focus must not be stolen by the arriving content. Auto-scroll must not move focus.

**Give static access to the finished thing.** Once complete, the response must be a normal, navigable document region with proper headings, lists and code semantics — so the user can navigate by heading, re-read, and copy. A response that only ever existed as a stream of live-region announcements is unreviewable.

**Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`** by disabling the typing cursor animation and any shimmer skeletons; for some users these are genuinely disabling. Consider a preference to disable streaming entirely and receive the complete response at once — for screen-reader and cognitive-accessibility users this is often strictly better, and it's a five-line change server-side.

**Don't hide meaning in colour alone.** Confidence tiers, diff add/remove, and citation-verified state all need a non-colour encoding (icon, text, pattern) — this is standard WCAG, and in AI products it bites specifically on the confidence and diff affordances that are core to the design.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I'd split the visual stream from the announced stream — paint tokens as they arrive, but push complete sentences into a polite live region, plus discrete announcements for phase changes and completion. Token-level `aria-live` is the classic mistake and it makes the product unusable rather than merely imperfect. I'd also offer a 'deliver the full response at once' preference, because for a lot of assistive-tech users streaming is a downgrade, not a feature."

### How would you price an AI feature — per seat, usage-based, or outcome-based? What does each do to your engineering constraints?

The mental model that makes this tractable for an engineer: **your pricing model determines who absorbs the variance in your cost of goods, and cost variance in AI products is enormous — a factor of 50 between your median and p99 user is normal.** Pick the model that puts the variance where it can be managed.

**Per seat.** You absorb all the variance; the customer gets predictable cost, which is why enterprise buyers strongly prefer it and why it is still the default at Notion, Glean and Harvey. It is a bet that usage per seat is bounded and that your average cost stays below your price. Engineering consequences are direct and severe: **you now own margin, so you own caching, routing, model selection and abuse control.** You need per-seat cost accounting, a fair-use policy with teeth, a small-model router, and prefix caching, because a 10× cost increase is a 10× margin hit rather than a 10× revenue event. It is also the model under which "let's use a bigger model" becomes a finance conversation.

**Usage-based** (per token, per request, per document, per credit). The customer absorbs variance; your margin is structurally safe. The costs move elsewhere: adoption friction (buyers hate unbounded bills, and procurement will demand caps), you now need metering that is accurate enough to bill on and to dispute, and — the subtle one — **the pricing model now shapes user behaviour in ways that damage the product**. Users avoid regenerating, avoid the agent, and avoid exploration, which is exactly the behaviour you don't want in a product whose value depends on trying things. Credits with a generous inclusion are the usual compromise. Engineering consequence: metering becomes a correctness-critical subsystem, and you need idempotent usage recording, because double-billed tokens are a support incident.

**Outcome-based** (per resolved ticket, per completed task, per document processed to acceptance). Sierra's per-resolution model is the well-known example in support. This aligns with value better than either alternative and is the strongest sales story in enterprise. But it hands you the hardest engineering problem in the three: **you must define and measure "resolved" precisely enough to survive a customer disputing the invoice.** That means the resolution definition is now a contractual artifact, your judge or heuristic is auditable, and you need an appeals path. You also take on adverse selection — a customer with unusually hard tickets pays the same per resolution — so you need per-customer cost modelling before you sign.

**💰 Math — the margin question, worked.** Suppose you charge $30/seat/month for an AI writing feature. Median user: 40 requests/month at 6k input / 800 output tokens = 240k in, 32k out. At $3/$15 per Mtok: 0.24×3 + 0.032×15 = $0.72 + $0.48 = **$1.20/month → 96% gross margin.** Comfortable. Now the p99 user: 1,200 requests/month = 7.2M in, 0.96M out = 7.2×3 + 0.96×15 = $21.60 + $14.40 = **$36.00/month → negative margin on that seat.** If 2% of seats look like that, blended cost is 0.98×$1.20 + 0.02×$36.00 = $1.18 + $0.72 = **$1.90, still 94% margin** — fine. But if your product is agentic and the p99 user runs 200 agent turns/month at $0.25 each = $50/month, and 10% of seats do that, blended cost = 0.9×$1.20 + 0.1×$50 = $1.08 + $5.00 = **$6.08 → 80% margin**, and one enterprise customer whose whole team are power users takes you underwater. **This is why agentic features are rarely priced per seat without a usage ceiling**, and being able to produce this arithmetic live is the difference between "I've thought about pricing" and "I've shipped a priced feature."

**⚠ Trap:** assuming token-price deflation will rescue a bad unit economic. Prices per token have fallen dramatically, but per-*task* cost has not fallen proportionally, because the tasks got bigger — reasoning tokens, longer contexts, more agent turns. Betting your margin on next year's price cut is how teams end up repricing under duress. Design to be profitable at today's prices.

### One customer's usage is 40× the average and they're on a flat per-seat plan. Walk me through what you do — product and engineering.

Do the diagnosis before the policy, because the four causes have completely different responses and picking the wrong one costs you either the account or the margin.

**Is it abuse or is it value?** Pull their trace distribution. If it's a script hammering your API through the UI, that's abuse and it's a terms conversation. If it's 200 humans using the product as intended and loving it, that customer is your best reference and throttling them is a strategic error. **If it's genuine heavy use, the answer is almost never to limit them — it's to make them cheaper to serve.**

**Is the cost concentrated in a pattern you can fix?** In my experience it usually is. Check, in this order: (1) **prefix-cache hit rate for that tenant** — a customer with a 40k-token custom instruction set and a poor cache hit rate can single-handedly explain the bill, and fixing the cache key ordering is a one-day change with a 10× effect; (2) **agent turn counts** — a handful of runaway loops can dominate a month; (3) **retrieval k and context size** — a tenant whose documents are large may be sending 8× the context per request; (4) **model routing** — are they hitting the frontier model on trivial requests a small model would handle?

**Then the levers, in the order I'd try them:**

1. **Optimize the serving path for their shape.** Cache, route, trim context. Zero customer friction, best outcome.
2. **Add a fair-use ceiling with a soft landing.** Not a hard cutoff — degrade gracefully: a smaller model, a lower `k`, a queue. And *tell the user*: "You're in high-volume mode; responses may be briefer." Silent degradation is the worse-in-every-way option and teams choose it constantly because it's easier to ship.
3. **Rate-limit per seat, not per tenant.** Tenant-level limits punish the whole company for one power user and produce a support ticket from the wrong person. Seat-level limits are also the right shape for the fairness story you'll have to tell.
4. **Reprice at renewal**, with the usage data as the conversation opener. A usage tier or a usage-based add-on for agentic features is the durable fix.

**The engineering prerequisite for all of it:** you need **per-tenant, per-seat cost attribution as a first-class metric**, computed from your own token counters and joined to your billing dimensions, available in near-real-time. If you learn about the 40× customer from the monthly vendor invoice, you are two months behind. I would put this in place before launching any flat-priced AI feature, and I'd say so in a design review — it is the AI equivalent of not shipping a service without request-level metrics.

**⚠ Trap:** rate-limiting on requests per minute when your cost is driven by tokens. A user making 10 requests/minute with 200k-token contexts costs 100× a user making 100 requests/minute with 2k contexts, and an RPM limiter lets the expensive one through while throttling the cheap one. Limit on **tokens per minute**, with a token bucket keyed on the tenant and seat — which is exactly the Redis token-bucket pattern you already know, with the refill denominated in tokens rather than requests, and with the wrinkle that you must *reserve* an estimate up front and reconcile after, because you don't know the output length until you're done.

### The PM wants AI in the search bar. How do you decide whether this should be an AI feature at all?

By asking what the model is buying you that deterministic code cannot, and being willing to conclude "nothing." This is the most valuable instinct in the product round and the rarest, because everyone in the loop is incentivized to say yes. The interviewer is very often specifically checking whether you will.

The model earns its place when at least one of these is true: the input is **unstructured natural language** and the mapping to intent is genuinely open-ended; the output is **generative** (text that didn't exist); the task requires **world knowledge or judgment** that isn't in your data; or the alternative is a rules engine whose rule count grows without bound. If none of those hold, you are paying tokens, latency, and a nonzero error rate for something a query parser does exactly.

For the search bar specifically, decompose it. "Find the doc called Q3 planning" — that is lexical search, and BM25 with a fuzzy match answers it in 8 ms with a 0% hallucination rate. "Find docs about our pricing strategy" — semantic retrieval, which is embeddings, not generation; you need a vector index, not an LLM. "What did we decide about pricing in Q3?" — *now* you need generation, because the answer is a synthesis that exists in no single document. Three different questions, three different systems, and the mature design routes between them rather than pushing everything through a model. I'd propose exactly that: a fast lexical path for the head of the distribution, hybrid retrieval for the middle, and generation only for the questions that are actually questions.

The costs you're accepting when you say yes, which I'd enumerate out loud: latency goes from ~10 ms to ~1,000 ms; per-query cost goes from ~$0.000001 to ~$0.005 (a factor of several thousand); correctness goes from deterministic to probabilistic, so you now need an eval harness, a monitoring story, and a design for being wrong; and you've added a vendor dependency with its own outages and version churn. Those are real and I would rather name them and proceed than have them discovered in the post-launch review.

The counterargument I'd also make honestly: **the model is often the right call even when a deterministic system would work, because the deterministic system requires the user to know the query language.** A faceted filter UI with twelve dropdowns is strictly more precise and used by 3% of people. If the deterministic path's real completion rate is 30% because nobody can drive it, "worse and used" beats "better and ignored."

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I'd split the traffic before I answer. Lexical lookups should never touch a model — that's 10 ms and zero error rate versus a second and a hallucination budget. Semantic recall needs embeddings, not generation. Generation earns its cost only for questions whose answer exists in no single document. I'd ship the router first and let the traffic mix tell us how big the generative slice actually is."

**⚠ Trap:** the AI-shaped feature that exists to be demoable. If the honest metric is "does this help the user finish faster," and the deterministic version wins on it, shipping the model version anyway is a judgment failure — and it is a failure interviewers at product companies are actively screening for, because they have all watched it happen.

### They open with "design the UX for an AI feature that summarizes long email threads." How do you run the next 45 minutes?

I run it in six moves, and I say the structure out loud at the start so the interviewer knows where we're going — that alone reads as senior.

**Move 1 (3 min) — establish the user, the trigger, and the failure cost.** Who opens this, at what moment, and what do they do next? Is a wrong summary embarrassing, expensive, or merely annoying? For email threads: the user is triaging, they open a 40-message thread they've been CC'd on, and they need to decide in ten seconds whether to read it. Wrong summary cost: they miss a commitment. That's medium blast radius — recoverable but real.

**Move 2 (3 min) — pick the interaction pattern and defend it.** Not chat. This is an **inline, pre-computed summary** that appears at the top of the thread, because the trigger is "opened the thread" and the intent needs no expression. And because it's pre-computed on thread arrival rather than on open, the latency budget is **zero** — the summary is already there. Naming that move (shift the work off the interactive path entirely) is usually the single strongest idea available in this problem, and most candidates never get there because they anchor on a request/response frame.

**Move 3 (10 min) — design the artifact.** What does the summary contain? For threads: the decision or open question, the commitments with owners, the participants, and what changed since I last read. Not a prose paragraph — a structured object rendered as a small number of labelled lines, because the user is scanning. **Each element links to the message it came from** — this is the grounding affordance and it's non-negotiable here, since the user's next action is "go read that message."

**Move 4 (10 min) — design for being wrong.** Missed a commitment: mitigate by biasing toward recall on commitments and marking uncertain ones. Misattributed a statement: the deep-link makes this self-correcting in one click. Summarized a thread that didn't need it (3 messages): don't show at all — a suppression rule, and I'd call it out as such. Sensitive content: a thread containing HR or legal material may need to not be summarized at all, which is a policy question I'd raise. And an explicit **"this summary is stale"** state when new messages arrive after generation.

**Move 5 (8 min) — metrics.** Primary: **did the summary replace reading?** Measured as the rate of thread-opens where the user acted (archive, reply, forward) without scrolling into the messages. Secondary: click-through on the deep links (verification behaviour, which should *fall* over a cohort's life as trust builds), and thread re-opens within an hour (a proxy for "the summary was insufficient"). I'd explicitly reject "thumbs-up rate" as the primary and explain why.

**Move 6 (8 min) — cost and rollout.** Pre-computing every thread is the expensive choice; compute the number. A 40-message thread is ~6k tokens in, 200 out: 6,000×3/1e6 + 200×15/1e6 = $0.018 + $0.003 = **$0.021/thread**. If 30M threads/day cross the fleet, that is **$630k/day**, which is obviously untenable — so you don't pre-compute everything. You pre-compute on a **predicted-open signal** (threads with the user in To:, threads over N messages, threads active in the last hour), which might be 5% of volume = $31.5k/day, and lazily compute the rest on open with a 1.5 s spinner. **The cost arithmetic changed the design**, and demonstrating that loop — design, cost, redesign — is the highest-scoring thing you can do in this round.

**⚠ Trap:** spending the first 20 minutes on prompt design. The interviewer is not grading your prompt. They are grading whether you thought about the trigger, the failure, the metric and the money. Prompt content should occupy under five minutes and should be described at the level of "structured output with per-field source message IDs," not written out.

### "What does the user see when we're wrong?" — give me your complete answer to that question.

This is the question the whole round exists to ask, so I'd answer it as a system rather than as a screen: **being wrong is not an exception path, it is 5–20% of the main path, and it needs a design with the same care as the happy path.** Four layers, and I'd name them in this order.

**Layer 1 — Prevention: don't produce the wrong answer.** Abstention gates on low retrieval scores or sample disagreement, entailment verification of claims against sources before render, structured output with schema validation so malformed results never reach the screen, and suppression rules that decline to show a suggestion at all. Every wrong answer prevented here costs nothing downstream. This is also the layer where "act on a smaller blast radius" lives — writing to a branch rather than to `main`.

**Layer 2 — Detection by the user: make wrongness visible in seconds.** Grounding markers with hover-to-source so a claim is checkable without leaving the page. Diff previews so an edit is reviewed rather than absorbed. Confidence tiers rendered as instructions ("verify this"), not numbers. Stated assumptions surfaced inline ("assumed FY ends December"). The design goal is that **the time to notice an error is shorter than the time to be harmed by it** — that's the actual invariant, and I'd state it exactly that way.

**Layer 3 — Recovery: make correction cheap and specific.** Undo scoped to the whole turn. Per-hunk accept/reject so partial value survives. Regenerate that escalates rather than repeats, with a reason picker. An edit affordance that lets the user fix the answer in place rather than re-prompting for it. Escalation to a human where one exists. The measurable target here is **post-error retry rate** — if users retry rather than leave, this layer works.

**Layer 4 — Learning: convert the error into a fix.** Every correction is a label. The rejected hunk, the edited output, the escalated conversation, the followed-then-abandoned citation. These flow into a failure taxonomy, a regression eval set, and — where appropriate — retrieval or prompt changes. The loop must close visibly for the user too: if a customer reports a bad answer and the same question is right next week, you have bought more trust than ten correct answers would have.

And the tone layer running through all of it: **be wrong the way a good colleague is wrong.** State what you did and what you assumed, don't over-apologize, don't fabricate to fill a gap, and make it obvious what to do next. A product that says "I couldn't find this in your workspace — want me to search the web?" is stronger than one that guesses, even though the guessing one scores better on answer rate.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Four layers: prevent it with abstention and verification, make it visible in under five seconds with grounding and diffs, make it cheap to correct with scoped undo and escalating regenerate, and convert it into a label that fixes the class. The invariant I design to is that the time to notice an error is shorter than the time to be harmed by it — everything else follows from that."

### A new user opens your AI feature for the first time. How do you make sure their first three interactions succeed?

Because the first three interactions decide whether there is a fourth, and the base rate of failure is high — a new user's first prompt is usually a bad prompt, aimed at a capability you may not have, against data you may not have indexed yet.

**Interaction zero: don't show an empty box.** An empty chat input is the highest-variance input surface ever designed. Replace it with three to five **concrete, contextual starting points** derived from the user's actual data — "Summarize the Q3 planning doc you edited yesterday," not "Try asking me anything." Contextual suggestions do two jobs at once: they teach capability boundaries by example, and they guarantee the first request is one you're good at. If you cannot generate contextual suggestions, that itself is a signal your integration isn't ready to be shown.

**Bias the first interactions toward high-confidence, low-blast-radius work.** Reading and summarizing before writing; suggesting before acting; one document before the whole workspace. Progressive disclosure of *capability*, not just of UI — the "send email on my behalf" affordance does not need to exist in week one, and gating it behind a few successful interactions is both a trust ramp and a safety ramp.

**Set the boundary explicitly and early.** One sentence about what it can see ("I can read documents you have access to in this workspace") does more for trust and for prompt quality than any amount of onboarding copy. Users write better prompts when they know the corpus.

**Make the first failure survivable.** New users are the most likely to hit an abstention (their data isn't indexed, their question is out of scope) and the least equipped to interpret it. First-session abstentions need the strongest version of the repair affordance — "I don't see any Q3 documents yet; your Drive finished syncing 2 minutes ago, try again or connect Notion."

**Instrument the first session separately.** Time-to-first-successful-interaction, first-session abandonment, and week-1 return rate. Aggregate metrics are dominated by power users and will hide a catastrophic new-user experience indefinitely. I'd put a new-user cohort cut on the main dashboard from day one for exactly this reason.

**⚠ Trap:** onboarding tours as a substitute for legible capability. A five-step modal tour is skipped by most users and remembered by none; it also signals that the interface didn't explain itself. The durable version of onboarding for an AI product is **suggested actions that are always present**, including on the empty state after every completed task — because the discoverability problem never actually goes away, it just moves to the next capability.

**🏋 Drill (60 minutes, unaided, whiteboard or paper):** design an AI feature end to end under the six-move structure above — pick "Ramp: flag anomalous expenses for review" or "Figma: generate component variants from a selection." Produce: the interaction pattern with its latency budget, the artifact the user sees, the confidence-vs-blast-radius placement for every action, the three failure modes with their UI response, the primary metric with its denominator defined, and a cost estimate at 10k requests/day with arithmetic shown. **Pass criterion:** you finish in 60 minutes, your cost estimate changes at least one design decision, and you can name the metric you'd *refuse* to optimize and say why. If your design has a chat box in it, justify that choice explicitly or replace it.
