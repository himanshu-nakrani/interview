## 87. Leveling, Comp Mechanics, Offer Choreography and Closing

*Mastering this proves you can convert a strong loop into the right level and the right number, which is where a surprising amount of lifetime compensation is decided.*

### Before we talk numbers — who actually decides my level, and at what moment is it decided?

The mental model that changes how you behave in a loop: your level is not assigned at the end by a negotiation, it is *inferred* during the loop and then ratified in the debrief. By the time the recruiter calls you with a number, the level is already a written artifact — a hiring-committee note, a debrief doc, a set of interviewer scorecards each with a "level recommendation" field. Negotiating level after that point means asking a committee to reverse a written decision, which is possible but expensive. Negotiating level *during* the loop means supplying evidence to people who are actively looking for it. The asymmetry is enormous and almost nobody exploits it.

Mechanically, three inputs feed the level decision. First, the **recruiter's initial slotting**, which comes from your resume and your years-of-experience and is frequently one level too low for a career-changer — a backend engineer moving into AI reads as "junior in the domain" unless you actively reframe it. Second, the **interviewer scorecards**, which at most large companies have an explicit dropdown ("meets bar for L4 / L5 / L6") that each interviewer fills in independently. Third, the **debrief or hiring committee**, which reconciles them and where the loudest voice is usually the interviewer who has the most specific scope evidence to cite — positive or negative. At Meta, Google and Amazon this is a formal committee separate from the hiring manager; at Cursor, Perplexity, Sierra or Harvey it is a founder or an eng lead making a judgment call over lunch.

The practical consequence: every interviewer in your loop is filling in a level box, including the ones who think they are only assessing coding. If your system-design round contained no evidence that you have owned a system end to end and set direction for other people, that interviewer will default to the level implied by your years of experience, and defaults are conservative.

**⚠ Trap:** believing that "level is set by HR from a band, so I'll fight it at offer stage." Recruiters can move a number inside a level far more easily than they can move a level, because the level requires re-opening a committee decision. The order of difficulty is: number within band (easy) < sign-on (easy) < equity above band (medium) < level (hard, and only with new evidence).

**🗣 Say this in the room** — when an interviewer asks the throwaway "anything else you want to add?" at the end: "One thing I don't think came out in the technical questions — the retrieval system I described, I owned end to end from the eval harness through the on-call rotation, and I set the re-embedding migration pattern that two other teams adopted. Happy to go into either if it's useful for your write-up." That sentence is written directly into a scorecard.

### Walk me through the difference between IC5, IC6 and staff in AI engineering. What actually separates them?

Strip the titles and there is one axis: **the size of the ambiguity you can be handed and still produce a correct outcome.** Every ladder is a restatement of that. Companies use different names — Meta E5/E6, Google L5/L6, Amazon SDE III / Principal, and flat "Member of Technical Staff" at labs and startups that refuse to publish a ladder — but the underlying question a debrief asks is identical: what is the smallest input this person needs, and how large is the blast radius they can be trusted with?

**IC5 / senior** in AI engineering means: given a defined feature — "ship RAG over the customer's Confluence" — you deliver it well, including the parts nobody told you about. You built the eval before the feature. You have latency and cost numbers. You handled the reindex migration without a stale-answer incident. You did not need someone to tell you that chunking strategy interacts with your retrieval metric. The scope is a *system*, and the ambiguity you resolve is technical.

**IC6 / staff** means: given a *problem area* — "our support deflection rate is 22% and the CEO wants 50%" — you decompose it, decide that half of it is not an AI problem at all, pick which third of it to attack first, define the metric the org will be judged on, and get two other teams to align on it. The scope is a *domain*, and the ambiguity you resolve is partly organizational. The tell is that a staff engineer's largest contributions are often *deletions* — the model they argued the company should not fine-tune, the agent framework they killed, the six-month project they redirected in week two.

**IC7 / senior staff and above** shifts again: you are now responsible for technical direction that outlives your involvement, and for the calibration of other senior people. In AI engineering specifically this often looks like owning the evaluation culture of an org — the golden set, who is allowed to change it, how model upgrades are gated — because that is the artifact everyone else's work is measured against.

The AI-specific wrinkle worth saying out loud: because the field is four years old at product scale, **domain scope compresses faster than in backend**. Someone with eighteen months of serious agent-harness work can hold staff-level scope in that area at a company where nobody else has done it, and companies know this. That is the single strongest argument you have for leveling up on the transition rather than down.

**⚠ Trap:** equating staff with "harder technical problems." Plenty of IC5s solve harder problems than their staff colleagues. The staff differentiator is that the problem *was not handed to them in a solved form* — they chose it, framed it, and convinced others it was the right one. If your best story is "the algorithm was really tricky," you are arguing for a strong IC5, not an IC6.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "The way I'd describe my scope: I don't need the problem defined for me, I need the business constraint. Give me 'support costs are too high and quality is slipping' and I'll come back with the decomposition, the eval that proves whether we're winning, and the part I recommend we *don't* build."

### What are the specific pieces of evidence that move a level, and how do I phrase each one?

There are five, they are boringly consistent across companies, and every one of them has a GenAI-native version that lands harder than its backend version because the field is young. Prepare one story per slot, ninety seconds each, with a number in it.

**1. Owned a system end to end.** Not "worked on" — owned: designed it, shipped it, was paged for it, deprecated part of it. The GenAI version is strongest when it spans the full stack of the discipline: "I owned the retrieval layer for our internal knowledge search — ingestion, chunking, the embedding-refresh migration, the reranker, the eval harness, the cost model, and the on-call runbook. 40k docs, p95 1.4s end to end, $0.011 per query at 90k queries a month." The specificity of the last clause is what converts.

**2. Set a technical direction that others followed.** The unit of proof is *adoption by people who did not report to you*. "I wrote the pattern for versioned vector-index aliases so re-embedding is an atomic swap instead of a rolling rebuild; two other teams adopted it and it became the template in our platform docs." Weak version: "I advocated for X." Strong version: "I wrote X down, defended it in review, and here is who uses it now."

**3. Made a call under ambiguity that was later validated.** This is the highest-value slot and the one most candidates fumble because they tell it as a success rather than as a *decision under uncertainty*. The structure must be: what was genuinely unknown, what you decided with incomplete information, what would have made you wrong, what actually happened. "In Q2 the team wanted to fine-tune for our structured-extraction task. I pushed back and argued for constrained decoding plus a 200-example eval first, because we had no labelled set and a fine-tune without one is unfalsifiable. I said explicitly that if constrained decoding didn't clear 90% field-level accuracy in two weeks, I'd be wrong and we'd fine-tune. It hit 94%, and we saved the labelling budget — but the reason I was comfortable was the falsifier, not the confidence."

**4. Mentored.** Named people, named outcomes. "I onboarded two backend engineers onto the LLM stack; I wrote the eval-first checklist we now use in review, and both of them shipped agent features without me in the loop within a quarter." Vague mentorship claims are worth zero.

**5. Ran an incident.** Not "was involved in" — ran: you were IC, you made the call, you wrote the postmortem, you owned the follow-ups. GenAI incidents are unusually good material because they are unfamiliar and memorable: a silent quality regression after a provider model upgrade, a prompt-injection exfiltration attempt, a retrieval index that went stale after a reindex and served confidently wrong answers for eleven hours. "I ran the incident where our answers went stale after a reindex — root cause was the alias swap completing before the reranker's cache invalidated, so we served the old top-k against new doc IDs. Detected via a 12-point drop on the golden set, not by an alert, which is why the first follow-up was a canary eval on every index swap."

**⚠ Trap:** the passive-voice team story. "We decided," "the team shipped," "it was determined that" — every one of those sentences deletes you from your own evidence, and debriefs are full of interviewers writing "unclear what *he* did." Say "I" when it is true. If a story has no "I" in it, it is not level evidence, it is context.

**🏋 Drill:** write all five stories at 150 words each. Pass criterion: every one contains (a) a first-person decision, (b) a number, and (c) something that could have gone wrong and how you'd have known. Then have someone read them and tell you what *you* specifically did. If they can't, rewrite.

### I'm mid-loop and I get the sense they're calibrating me one level below where I want to land. How do I correct that before the debrief instead of after?

First, recognize the signals, because they are legible. You are being calibrated low if: the questions stay at implementation altitude and never move to "how would you decide whether to build this at all"; the interviewer explains context you should be expected to supply; nobody asks about cross-team influence, mentorship or incident ownership; the system-design prompt is scoped tight ("build a rate limiter for our LLM gateway") rather than open ("we're spending too much on inference, go"); or the recruiter's language shifts from "senior" to "the role" mid-process. Also: if a scheduled "hiring manager / scope" round quietly turns into another coding round, that is a level signal.

The correction has to happen *inside* an interview, because the scorecard is written within an hour of it. Three moves, in ascending order of directness.

**Move one — raise the altitude of your own answers unprompted.** When handed a tight prompt, solve it *and* frame it. "I'll build the token-bucket gateway, and I want to flag the thing I'd actually push on first: at our volume the gateway isn't the cost problem, prefix-cache hit rate is. I'd instrument that before I built any of this. But let's do the gateway." That single sentence is the difference between "implements well" and "sets direction." Do it once per round, not every answer.

**Move two — donate the evidence they didn't ask for.** At the end of a round, use the "any questions for me" slot: "Before questions — one thing that hasn't come up and might be relevant for your write-up: I've owned the eval and on-call side of these systems, not just the build. Would that be useful to cover, or is it out of scope for this round?" Interviewers almost always say yes, and now it is in the notes.

**Move three — go to the recruiter, in writing, before the debrief.** This is the underused one. "Quick note before the panel wraps: I want to make sure I'm being calibrated against the right level. My last two years have been [scope], and I'd expect that to map to [E6/L6/staff] at your ladder. If the loop is currently scoped to [E5], is there a round you'd want to add or a specific type of evidence the committee would need? Happy to do it." Recruiters do not find this presumptuous — leveling ambiguity creates rework for them, and a candidate who names the target early lets them add a "scope" round or brief the panel. The version that *does* read badly is doing this after the offer.

**⚠ Trap:** confusing a hard interview with a high-level interview. Being asked a brutal transformer-internals question is not a signal that you are being considered for staff; it is a signal that they hired a researcher to interview you. Level signal lives in the *ambiguity* of the prompt, not its difficulty.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Can I check how you're scoping this question? I can answer it as 'build the thing well,' or I can answer it as 'decide whether this is the right thing to build and what I'd cut.' The second is closer to how I actually work, but I'll go where you want."

### At the same company, how do AI Engineer, Research Engineer, AI Infra and Forward-Deployed roles get leveled and paid differently?

They are on different ladders, or on the same ladder with different calibration, and this materially changes both your number and your ceiling. The durable structure — the specific bands are volatile, the shape is not:

**Research Engineer / Research Scientist** ladders at frontier labs typically sit at the top of the compensation distribution and are calibrated against research output. At the same nominal seniority, an RE offer often carries more equity than an applied offer, because the labs are competing for a smaller pool. The catch is the *entry bar*: without publications or a demonstrated training-stack contribution, you will be slotted at the bottom of that ladder, which can be worse than the top of the applied ladder. Do not chase the RE title if your evidence is applied.

**AI Engineer / Applied AI** is the product-adjacent ladder and is usually calibrated on the *standard software ladder* at big-tech (Meta E5/E6, Google L5/L6) with a domain flavor. At AI-product companies it is often the only ladder. This is where your backend depth converts most cleanly, because the scope evidence the ladder wants — owned a system, set direction, ran an incident — is evidence you already have.

**AI Infrastructure** is leveled on the software ladder but calibrated against systems depth (serving engines, GPU scheduling, distributed training, kernels). Compensation is broadly comparable to applied at the same level and sometimes above it at inference providers, because the supply of people who can debug a CUDA OOM under continuous batching is genuinely thin. The ceiling is high; the lateral mobility into product is worse.

**Forward-Deployed** is the one to read carefully. At Palantir-lineage orgs and at OpenAI/Anthropic's deployment teams, FDE is a real engineering ladder with real scope. Elsewhere — and this is the risk — "Forward-Deployed" is a solutions-architect role wearing an engineering title, leveled against a *sales* or *services* ladder, with lower equity, a variable component tied to bookings, and a promotion path that runs through customer count rather than technical scope. The diagnostic question is blunt and you should ask it: "Is this role on the engineering ladder or a separate one? Who writes the performance review, and are FDEs promoted on the same calibration cycle as product engineers?"

**💰 Math on why the ladder matters more than the title:** suppose two offers at nominally the same seniority, one at $220k base + $180k/yr equity, one at $200k base + $120k/yr equity + a $60k target variable. Year one they look like $400k vs $380k. But the variable is at-risk and does not compound, while equity refreshes stack: if refreshes run at ~60% of initial annual grant, by year three the first role is at 180 + 108 + 108 ≈ $396k of vesting equity plus base ≈ $616k, while the second is at 120 + 72 + 72 = $264k plus base plus an unchanged variable ≈ $524k. A $20k year-one gap becomes a ~$92k year-three gap. **📅 Volatile:** those refresh ratios vary enormously by company and year — treat the structure as the lesson, not the constants.

**🗣 Say this in the room** (to a recruiter, early): "Before we go deep — is this position on the software engineering ladder, and which level? I want to make sure the loop is calibrated to the right scope, and it affects which examples I bring."

### My resume said "Senior Backend Engineer" and the recruiter slotted me at the level below where I think I belong. How do I get re-slotted before the loop starts?

The window is the recruiter screen, and the currency is *evidence density per sentence*, not seniority claims. Recruiters slot from three things: years of experience, current title, and the scope words your resume uses. A backend-to-AI transition gets down-slotted by default because the recruiter's mental model is "years in AI" rather than "years of engineering judgment," and their screening rubric may literally say so.

The reframe that works is to refuse the "years in AI" frame and substitute an artifact frame. "I've been doing production LLM work for [N] months, and before that eight years of distributed backend. The reason I'd expect [level] calibration is what I've owned in that window, not the duration — [one sentence of the end-to-end system, one number, one cross-team adoption fact]. Also, [public artifact]." A merged PR to vLLM or TRL, a public eval harness, a from-scratch reimplementation with benchmarks — these do disproportionate leveling work precisely because they are verifiable in thirty seconds and most candidates have none.

Then ask the direct question, once, without apology: "What level is this req open at, and is there a higher band open on the same team?" Many reqs are open at two adjacent levels, and the recruiter will just tell you. If they say the req is single-level and below your target, you have a real decision: run the loop and try to over-perform into a re-slot (works maybe a third of the time and depends on the company's willingness to hire above req), or ask them to route you to a higher req.

**⚠ Trap:** leading with the level ask before you have given them anything to justify it. "I'm looking for staff" in minute two, unsupported, reads as entitlement and gets you screened out. Evidence first, ask second, in the same breath.

**📐 Numbers you must know:** the re-slot success rate is far higher *before* the loop than after — anecdotally at large companies, hiring above the req at debrief happens but requires an interviewer to affirmatively argue for it, whereas opening a higher req before scheduling is an administrative act. Optimize for the administrative act.

### How do the big-tech ladders map onto each other, and why does the AI org sometimes not follow them?

The rough correspondence — and I want to be precise that this is a *conventional* mapping used for cross-company comparison, not an official equivalence any of these companies publishes:

Senior engineer sits at Meta E5, Google L5, Amazon SDE III, Microsoft 63–64. Staff sits at Meta E6, Google L6, Amazon Principal (a notably larger jump than the others — Amazon's Principal is closer to a Google L6/L7 hybrid in scope), Microsoft 65–66. Above that the ladders diverge enough that comparison stops being useful. **📅 Volatile:** Microsoft's level numbering, Amazon's band structure and every company's internal titles change; verify against levels.fyi and, better, ask the recruiter directly which external level they benchmark against.

Two reasons the AI org may not follow the standard mapping. First, **dedicated AI bands**: several large companies have introduced compensation adjustments or separate bands for AI-adjacent roles in response to lab poaching, so the same level in the AI org can pay meaningfully more than in the general org. This is real and you should ask about it: "Is this role compensated on the standard band, or is there an AI-specific band?" Second, **acquired-team and lab-inside-a-big-company structures** (a research org inside a hyperscaler, an acquired startup running its own calibration) frequently keep their own leveling for a while.

The practical move is to stop trying to reason about it from the outside. Ask two questions of the recruiter and you get everything: "What level is this, and what's the total comp range for that level for this role?" and "Is that the standard band or an AI-specific one?" Recruiters at large US companies are increasingly required by pay-transparency law to disclose a range anyway; asking just gets you there faster.

**⚠ Trap:** comparing offers by level name across companies. An E6 offer and an L6 offer are not the same job and are frequently not the same money; comparing the *packages* is the only sound method, and even then you must normalize for vesting shape and refresh (which the next chunk does properly).

### What does "staff" mean at a 200-person AI product company versus at Google? Should I care about the title?

They mean genuinely different things and you should care, but not in the direction people assume.

At Google or Meta, staff is a *calibrated* level. There is a written expectation document, a promotion committee, a peer group, and a market rate other companies will honor. If you leave with an L6 stamp, your next recruiter starts you at senior-staff conversations. The title is portable capital.

At a 200-person AI product company — Cursor, Sierra, Harvey-scale — "staff" is frequently either (a) a title given to make an offer competitive without giving equity, or (b) a genuine description of scope because the company has twelve engineers touching AI and you would own a third of the surface. The scope version is often the better career outcome and the worse portable-title outcome: two years owning the entire retrieval-and-eval stack at a fast company teaches more than two years of L6 at a hyperscaler, but a future recruiter cannot verify the title against a public ladder.

My rule in review, and the one I would apply to my own offer: **at large companies, optimize for level; at small companies, optimize for surface area and ignore the title entirely.** The reason is what the title buys you. A big-company level buys you calibration, mobility and a floor on your next offer. A small-company title buys you nothing external — what buys you the next job is "I owned the agent harness that serves 4M requests a day," which is a scope fact, not a title.

**⚠ Trap:** taking a title down-level at a small company because "titles don't matter there," and then discovering that the small company's internal comp bands *do* key off it and that your equity refresh is banded by title. Titles don't matter for prestige; they often still matter for money. Ask: "Does the title map to a compensation band internally, and what does the refresh look like at that band?"

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I care about the level insofar as it sets the scope I'm trusted with and the band I'm paid from. If the title is cosmetic here, tell me and I'll stop asking — but then I want to talk about what I own."

### Give me your best "I set a technical direction that others followed" story. What separates a passing answer from a failing one at IC6?

A failing answer describes advocacy. A passing answer describes an *artifact plus adoption plus a cost of being wrong*. The shape is: here was the fork in the road, here is what the org was about to do, here is what I argued instead and why, here is the thing I wrote or built that made it adoptable, here is who adopted it without reporting to me, and here is what it changed in a number.

Worked example, in the register I would actually use: "Late last year three teams were independently building LLM features and every one of them was hand-rolling a prompt-plus-parse loop with retries. The failure mode was uniform — retries on malformed JSON, which amplified cost roughly 2.4× on the tail without improving quality, because a model that fails schema once usually fails it again for the same reason. I argued we should standardize on constrained decoding at the gateway with a schema registry, not on better retry logic. The direction-setting artifact wasn't the argument, it was a 300-line gateway middleware plus a bake-off doc: 500 real requests, three approaches, field-level accuracy and cost per successful extraction on each. Constrained decoding was 96.2% vs 88.5% for retry-and-repair, at 0.41× the cost because there was no second call. I put it in the platform repo with a migration guide. Two of the three teams migrated within a month; the third had a streaming requirement the middleware didn't handle, so I wrote down that limitation rather than pretending it generalized. Aggregate effect was about $9k a month at our volume, but the durable win was that nobody has written a bespoke retry loop since."

Note what that contains: a fork, a mechanism-level reason (not a preference), a *measured* bake-off, an artifact someone else can adopt, voluntary adoption, an honest scope limit, and a number. The honest scope limit is doing real work — an IC6 who claims their pattern is universal is telling a debrief they do not understand its boundaries.

**⚠ Trap:** the "I introduced [framework]" story. Introducing LangChain or LangGraph or vLLM is a tool decision, not a direction. Direction is a *pattern with a rationale that survives the tool*. If your story would be invalidated by the framework being deprecated, it was not direction.

**🏋 Drill:** write your direction story, then delete every sentence that does not contain either a mechanism, a measurement, or a named adopter. Pass criterion: what remains is still 100+ words and still tells the whole arc.

### GenAI-specific: what does a good "call under ambiguity that was later validated" story look like in this field, and what makes one ring false?

The reason this slot is worth more in AI engineering than in backend is that the field's defaults are *wrong often enough* that resisting them is a real skill and a demonstrable one. The four canonical calls, each of which is a defensible story:

**"We should not fine-tune yet."** The single highest-value ambiguity call in applied AI, and the one whose reflexive opposite gets candidates rejected. The credible version names the precondition you would have needed — a labelled set, a plateau on prompt-and-retrieval, a task where the failure is capability rather than context — and states what you did instead.

**"This use case will not work and here's what I'd offer instead."** Killing a project on capability grounds, with an eval as the evidence rather than an opinion. "We ran 80 real cases through it; on the subclass the customer cared most about we were at 61%, and their manual baseline was 94%. I told them we'd fail, and proposed the narrower triage version that we could hold at 97% with a human in the loop on the 12% we flagged."

**"We should ship a worse model."** Choosing the cheaper or faster model against the team's instinct, backed by an eval showing the quality delta is inside the noise band for *your* task distribution.

**"We should build the eval before the feature."** Unglamorous, and the one that most reliably reads as senior, because it is the thing juniors skip.

What makes a story ring false: no falsifier, no cost, no counterfactual. If you cannot say "here is what would have made me wrong and here is roughly when I would have known," it was not a call under ambiguity, it was a preference that happened to work. Interviewers at labs and at serious product companies probe exactly there — the follow-up is always some version of "what would have changed your mind?" and having a crisp answer ready is most of the score.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "The reason I was comfortable making that call without more data was that I could state the falsifier cheaply. I said: if constrained decoding is under 90% field accuracy on 200 labelled cases in two weeks, I'm wrong and we fine-tune. That's a two-week, one-engineer bet against a two-month one. I'd make that trade every time even if the outcome had gone the other way."

### Suppose they come back with an offer one level below what I asked for. When should I take it, and what do I extract in exchange?

Take it in exactly three situations, and run the math before you decide any of them.

**One: the company is a step-change in what you'd learn or in your next-job optionality.** Going from a non-AI-native company to an AI-product company or a lab is worth a level, because the level you regain in eighteen months is levered on top of a much better platform. **Two: the down-level comes with an explicit, written promotion path** — not "we see you growing into it," but "we'll calibrate you at the next cycle, here is the cycle date, here is the expectation doc, and here is the manager's commitment." Get the cycle date in the offer email; it costs the recruiter nothing and it converts a vibe into an obligation. **Three: the package at the lower level is at or above your target anyway**, which happens constantly across company types — a lab's senior band can exceed a big-tech staff band.

Refuse it when the down-level is a *scope* down-level: you would be handed defined features rather than problem areas. That is not a title problem, it is a job problem, and you will spend two years accumulating evidence that does not level you.

**💰 Math on what a down-level actually costs.** Say the target level pays $400k/yr total comp and one level down pays $330k. If you take the down-level and get promoted at the 18-month mark, the cost is 1.5 × $70k = $105k of direct comp, plus the equity you did *not* get granted at the higher band — if initial grants scale with level, a $600k/4yr grant instead of $480k/4yr is another $120k spread over four years, of which you lose the portion vesting before the promotion (~$45k over 18 months). Total ≈ $150k, before any appreciation. That is the number to weigh against the platform value. If the down-level comes with no promotion commitment and you assume 30 months instead of 18, the same arithmetic gives 2.5 × 70 = $175k plus ~$75k of grant delta ≈ $250k. **📅 Volatile:** the band figures are illustrative — plug in your real numbers, but do the calculation before you answer, not after.

What to extract in exchange, in order of how easily companies say yes: a larger sign-on that explicitly bridges the gap ("$70k sign-on to cover the first-year delta"); an off-cycle equity refresh at the six-month mark; a written promotion-review date; the specific team and scope in writing; and a start date that lets you close another process. The sign-on is the easiest yes because it is one-time cash that does not disturb internal band equity — which is the real reason companies resist level moves in the first place.

**⚠ Trap:** accepting a verbal "we'll fast-track you." Verbal promotion promises survive neither reorgs nor manager changes, and manager tenure in this industry is short. If it is not in the offer letter or a written email from the hiring manager, price it at zero.
### Break an offer down for me. Which components are actually money, and which just look like money?

The mental model: an offer is a portfolio of instruments with wildly different risk, liquidity and duration, and recruiters quote it as a single scalar ("$X total comp") precisely because that scalar hides the differences. Your job is to de-aggregate it before you compare anything.

**Base salary.** Cash, certain, compounds into every future offer because it is the number the next recruiter anchors on. It is also the most band-constrained component — the thing recruiters have the least room on, because base sets internal equity comparisons.

**Bonus.** Usually a target percentage of base (commonly 10–20% at big-tech for senior ICs, sometimes 0% at labs and startups that fold it into base) paid annually, multiplied by a company factor and an individual factor. Treat target bonus as ~85–100% expected value at a stable large company, and treat "discretionary" bonus with no stated target as ~0 when comparing offers.

**Equity.** The largest and least certain component; the whole rest of this chunk is about valuing it properly. The critical distinction is public RSU (a real, sellable security with a market price) versus private equity (RSU, PPU or options in a company with no public market), which are not the same asset class and should never be added together in a comparison table without a discount.

**Sign-on.** One-time cash, typically paid in one or two tranches, almost always with a clawback if you leave inside 12 (sometimes 24) months. It is the recruiter's most flexible lever because it does not touch band equity, and it is the correct thing to push on when base and level are stuck.

**Everything else that is not money but is worth money:** relocation, remote allowance, equipment budget, conference/learning budget, and — the one AI engineers should actually negotiate — compute or API budget for personal projects.

**💰 The de-aggregation you should do on every offer, worked.** Offer: $210k base, 15% target bonus, $640k RSU over 4 years vesting 25/25/25/25 with a 1-year cliff, $80k sign-on. Year one: 210 + 31.5 + 160 + 80 = $481.5k. Year two: 210 + 31.5 + 160 = $401.5k. Year four, same. Year five, with no refresh: 210 + 31.5 = $241.5k — a 40% pay cut the moment the grant runs out. That year-five number is the one to hold in your head, because it is what makes refresh policy the most important question in the entire offer conversation and the one nobody asks.

**⚠ Trap:** comparing offers on year-one total comp. Year one is the number the recruiter optimizes because it contains the sign-on, which is the cheapest thing they can give you. Compare on **years 2–4 average, plus a refresh assumption you asked about explicitly.** I have seen a $40k year-one edge invert into a $150k four-year deficit purely on vesting shape.

### Explain RSU versus PPU versus options. What is each actually worth?

Three instruments, three completely different risk profiles.

**RSUs (Restricted Stock Units)** are a promise to deliver shares on a vesting date. You pay nothing. At a public company they convert to shares you can sell immediately, taxed as ordinary income at vest on the full market value. Their value is knowable to the dollar on any given day. This is the cleanest instrument in existence and it is why public-company offers are easy to compare.

**PPUs (Profit Participation Units)** are the instrument OpenAI is publicly known to use, and variants exist at other capped-profit or LLC-structured entities. The mental model: a PPU is not ownership of the company, it is a claim on *future profit distributions*, subject to the entity's structure and cap. In practice, for the holder, the thing that turns a PPU into money is a liquidity event the company runs — typically a tender offer where the company or an investor buys units at a set price. So a PPU's value depends on two things you do not control: the unit price set at the next tender, and whether a tender happens at all while you hold them. **📅 Volatile:** the specifics of any company's PPU structure, cap, and tender cadence change; get the actual plan documents and read them, and do not take a recruiter's verbal summary of the tax treatment as authoritative.

**Options (ISOs / NSOs)** give you the *right to buy* shares at a fixed strike. Your value is the spread — (current fair value − strike) × shares — and it can be zero or negative. Options carry three costs RSUs do not: you must pay the strike to exercise, exercising a private company's ISOs can trigger AMT on the paper spread even though you have received no cash, and if you leave you usually have a 90-day post-termination exercise window (some companies extend to 7–10 years; this is a real and negotiable term). A $600k "option grant" at a private company means "600k of paper spread at today's 409A price," which is not $600k.

**💰 Math that makes the difference concrete.** 10,000 shares. As RSUs at a $50/share public price: $500k, and you can sell at vest. As options with a $12 strike and a $50 current fair value: paper spread $380k, but exercising all of them costs you $120k in cash out of pocket, plus potential AMT on the $380k spread in the exercise year, and you cannot sell until a liquidity event. Same headline "equity," radically different asset. The rule I apply: **an option grant is worth its spread times a probability-of-liquidity discount, minus the cash you must front.** For an early-stage private company I personally discount hard; for a late-stage lab with a demonstrated annual tender, much less.

**⚠ Trap:** letting a recruiter quote private equity at the last round's *preferred* share price. Preferred shares carry liquidation preferences and other rights your common shares/units do not. The common-share 409A valuation is typically materially lower than the preferred price, and the number you are handed in an offer is often the preferred-derived one. Ask: "Is that valued at the last preferred round price or the current 409A?"

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Can you send me the plan documents rather than the summary? I'm not questioning the number — I just want to model vesting, the liquidity mechanism and the post-termination window myself before I compare it to a public RSU offer."

### Walk me through vesting schedules and cliffs. Which variants actually change what the offer is worth?

Vesting shape is the most-ignored, easiest-to-model lever in an offer, and the differences are worth six figures.

**Standard: 4 years, 25% per year, 1-year cliff, then quarterly or monthly.** The cliff means you get nothing if you leave inside 12 months. Everything after the cliff is linear.

**Front-loaded / accelerated:** some companies vest 33/33/22/12 or similar, or vest monthly from month one with no cliff. This is strictly better for you and is common at startups competing on cash-equivalent value.

**Back-loaded:** Amazon is the canonical example, historically vesting roughly 5% / 15% / 40% / 40% across years one through four, with large cash sign-on bonuses in years one and two explicitly designed to smooth the resulting dip. **📅 Volatile:** Amazon's structure has been adjusted over time — verify the current schedule in your actual offer letter, not from memory.

**Why it matters, with the arithmetic.** Two offers, both "$800k equity over 4 years." Offer A vests 25/25/25/25. Offer B vests 5/15/40/40. Present-value them at a 10% annual discount rate, vesting at year-end for simplicity:

- A: 200/1.1 + 200/1.21 + 200/1.331 + 200/1.4641 = 181.8 + 165.3 + 150.3 + 136.6 = **$634.0k**
- B: 40/1.1 + 120/1.21 + 320/1.331 + 320/1.4641 = 36.4 + 99.2 + 240.4 + 218.6 = **$594.6k**

That is a $39k gap on identical headline numbers, before you account for the much larger effect: **median tenure.** If you leave at 24 months, offer A has paid you $400k and offer B has paid you $160k. At a company type where two-year tenure is normal, back-loading is not a $39k difference, it is a $240k one. That is the real argument, and it is the one to make to the recruiter: "Given typical tenure, the back-loaded shape means I'm realistically capturing 20% of this grant. I'd like the sign-on to bridge years one and two."

**⚠ Trap:** assuming the cliff is only about year one. Some private-company plans have a *second* cliff or a performance condition on later tranches; some have a "vesting commencement date" set to your start date, others to the next board-approval date, which can silently push your cliff two months out. Read the grant agreement for the vesting commencement definition.

**🏋 Drill:** take any two offers with different vesting shapes and compute the PV of each at a 10% discount rate and at a 2-year exit assumption, unaided, in five minutes. Pass criterion: you produce four numbers and can say which offer wins under which assumption.

### Tell me how a sign-on bonus clawback actually works and what I should be negotiating in that clause.

The mental model: a sign-on is a loan the company forgives over time, and the clause defines the forgiveness schedule. The default in most US offers is a full clawback if you voluntarily leave (or are terminated for cause) within 12 months, sometimes 24 for large sign-ons or for a second tranche. Some companies prorate — you repay 1/12 per remaining month — and some do not, which means leaving at month 11 costs you the entire bonus.

Four things to actually read and negotiate:

**1. Prorated versus cliff repayment.** Ask for prorated. It is a small ask and companies frequently agree because the alternative creates an obviously perverse incentive.

**2. Gross versus net.** The vicious version says you repay the *gross* amount, but you received the *net* after withholding. On an $80k sign-on at an effective 40% marginal rate you received $48k and could be asked to repay $80k. Push for net repayment, or at minimum for language allowing you to recover the withholding.

**3. What triggers it.** Voluntary resignation, yes. But some clauses trigger on any termination other than a formal reduction in force — meaning a performance termination at month 10 leaves you with a bill. Ask for the clawback to be waived on involuntary termination without cause.

**4. Second-tranche timing.** If the sign-on is split across two years, the second tranche is a retention device and usually carries its own 12-month clawback, meaning you are effectively bound for 24 months. Know that before you accept.

**💰 Worked:** $80k sign-on, gross clawback, non-prorated, 12 months, 40% withholding. You leave at month 11 for an offer worth $60k/yr more. Cost of leaving = $80k repaid − $48k received = a $32k net loss *plus* returning cash you may have spent, against a gain of $60k/yr. It still pencils, but only just in year one — and if the clause is 24 months you are trapped for a year. The clause is worth more than the number.

**⚠ Trap:** treating the sign-on as compensation when comparing offers. It is one-time. In a 4-year comparison an $80k sign-on is $20k/yr, and in a *steady-state* comparison it is $0. Recruiters lean on sign-on precisely because it inflates the year-one headline without touching the recurring cost structure. Take it — it is free money — but do not let it decide the comparison.

### Why do you say refresh policy is the most under-negotiated part of an offer?

Because the initial grant is a four-year asset and your career is longer than four years, and the refresh policy determines whether year five is a raise or a 40% pay cut. Candidates spend three weeks negotiating a $20k base bump and zero minutes on a mechanism worth $200k+ over the same horizon.

The mechanics vary in ways you must ask about specifically:

**Annual refresh at big-tech** typically grants additional RSUs each year based on performance, vesting over a new 4-year (sometimes 2-year) schedule. Because grants overlap, steady-state vesting after ~4 years is the sum of the tails of four grants. A company granting refreshes at roughly 50–60% of the initial annual rate produces a steady state near your initial run rate; one granting 25% produces a cliff.

**Front-loaded-grant companies** (a large initial grant, small or no refresh) are common at startups. The arithmetic there is brutal at year five.

**Performance-banded refresh** means the number is a function of your rating. Ask what the refresh looks like at "meets expectations," not at "exceeds" — the recruiter will quote you the top band.

**💰 The steady-state calculation to do out loud.** Initial grant $640k/4yr = $160k/yr vesting. Refresh at 60% of that = $96k granted per year, itself vesting over 4 years = $24k/yr from each refresh. By year five you are vesting: nothing left from the initial grant, plus $24k × 4 (the refreshes from years 1–4) = $96k. Your equity income fell from $160k to $96k — a 40% cut in the equity component. To hold flat you need refreshes at 100% of the initial annual rate, which almost nobody does. Now do it with a 25% refresh: $40k granted per year, $10k/yr each, $40k total in year five, a 75% cut. This is the "equity cliff" people hit at year four and it is why so much senior tenure in tech is 3.5 years.

**The question to ask, verbatim:** "What did refresh grants look like last cycle for someone at this level meeting expectations, as a percentage of the initial grant? And is there an off-cycle refresh mechanism?"

**⚠ Trap:** accepting "we do competitive annual refreshes" as an answer. That sentence is content-free. Push once: "I'm not asking for a commitment, just the actual range last cycle." If they will not answer, model the offer at a 25% refresh and see if it still wins.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "The initial grant matters less to me than the steady state. If the refresh at this level runs around 50–60% I'm comfortable; if it's front-loaded with a thin refresh, I'd want more of the value in the first two years, because that's what I'd actually capture."

### How do I value equity at a private frontier lab against RSUs at a public company?

This is the hardest comparison in the current market and the one where I see the most sloppy thinking, in both directions — people who treat lab paper equity as cash, and people who discount it to zero.

The honest framework has four inputs.

**1. Liquidity mechanism and cadence.** A public RSU is liquid at vest. Private equity is liquid only via a tender offer, a secondary sale, or an exit. The question is not "is the company valuable" but "how do I convert this into money and how often has that actually happened?" Ask directly: "Has the company run employee tenders? How often? What percentage of holdings were employees permitted to sell, and were there eligibility restrictions by tenure or unit type?" A company with two completed annual tenders at increasing prices is a fundamentally different asset from one that has never run one.

**2. Valuation basis.** As noted, the number in your offer may be derived from the last preferred round. Ask which. A $2M grant at preferred pricing might be $1.2M at the common/409A basis.

**3. Transfer restrictions and rights of first refusal.** Most private plans forbid you from selling on secondary markets without company consent, and the company has a right of first refusal. Assume you cannot sell outside a company-run event.

**4. Dilution and structural specifics.** Future rounds dilute you; capped-profit or LLC structures may have their own priority waterfall.

**💰 A discount you can defend out loud.** Suppose the private grant is nominally $1.6M over 4 years ($400k/yr) and the public offer is $700k over 4 years ($175k/yr) plus a $30k higher base. Apply a liquidity-and-outcome discount to the private paper. If you believe there is an annual tender and the company is likely to be worth at least its current mark, a 30–40% discount is defensible: $400k × 0.65 = $260k/yr risk-adjusted, still well above $175k. If there has never been a tender and no exit path is visible, a 70% discount is equally defensible: $400k × 0.3 = $120k/yr, and now the public offer wins on a risk-adjusted basis *and* on liquidity *and* on base. **The point of the exercise is not the exact discount — it is that you state your discount and the assumption behind it, rather than comparing $400k to $175k and calling it obvious.**

**📅 Volatile:** private-lab valuations, tender terms and structures have moved fast and will keep moving. Verify current mechanics with the company, and note that reported secondary-market prices are not the price you will get.

**⚠ Trap:** the upside-only narrative — "if it 10×s." Do the same arithmetic on a flat or down round, because the down case is what determines whether you can pay rent in year three. Also note the correlation risk: if you hold a large concentrated position in your employer's private equity, your salary and your net worth are the same bet.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I'm not discounting the equity because I don't believe in the company — I'm discounting it because I can't sell it. If there's a regular tender, tell me about it and my discount goes down a lot."

### What is double-trigger vesting and why should I care at a private company?

Short and specific, because this is a term you either know or get surprised by.

Private-company RSUs frequently carry **double-trigger vesting**: the units vest only when *both* conditions are met — (1) you satisfy the time-based service schedule, and (2) a liquidity event occurs (IPO or acquisition), usually within a defined window after grant. The reason companies do this is tax: single-trigger RSUs at a private company would create a taxable event at vest with no shares you can sell to pay the tax bill.

The consequence for you: your "vested" units may not actually be yours in any usable sense. If you leave a private company after three years with three years of time-vested double-trigger RSUs and no liquidity event ever happens, you get nothing. Some plans also let unvested-on-the-liquidity-condition units expire after a fixed number of years from grant (7 is common), meaning early grants can *lapse* before an IPO.

Questions to ask: "Are these single- or double-trigger? What is the expiration window from grant? If I leave after the service condition is met but before a liquidity event, what happens?"

**⚠ Trap:** modeling private double-trigger RSUs on the same schedule as public RSUs in your comparison spreadsheet. They vest on a *company* timeline, not yours. This is a separate and additional discount from the liquidity discount above, and it is the reason I model private RSU offers with an explicit probability-of-event term rather than a flat haircut.

### What is the honest 2026 compensation spread for GenAI roles, and how do I anchor when I have no competing offer?

Two parts to this, and I want to be straight about the first.

**The spread is genuinely wide and I will not quote you a table.** For senior AI/applied-AI engineering roles the observable range across the archetypes on your list spans from packages that look like standard senior-engineer comp at a big non-AI-native company to multiples of that at frontier labs and at AI-product companies flush with recent funding — and the variance *within* a single company across teams and levels is large enough that any single figure I gave you would be wrong for your specific req. **📅 Volatile:** this is the most volatile fact in this entire guide. Before every loop, re-verify against levels.fyi for public companies, recent aggregated survey data, and — most usefully — the pay ranges companies are now legally required to post in several US states. Quoting a stale 2024 band in a 2026 negotiation is a credibility event.

**Anchoring without a competing offer** is a solvable problem, and the solution is to anchor on *market data and role scope* rather than on leverage you do not have.

The method: (1) Assemble three data points before the conversation — the posted range for this req or a comparable one at this company, the levels.fyi band for the level at this company, and one comparable company's posted range. (2) Anchor at the upper-middle of the *documented* range, not at a number you made up. (3) Justify with scope, not need: "Based on the posted range for this level and the scope we discussed — owning the retrieval and eval stack end to end — I'd be looking at the upper part of that band, around $X." (4) Do not name a number until you have made them name the range.

What substitutes for a competing offer: an active process elsewhere ("I'm in final stages with two other companies" — only if true), a strong public artifact, a genuine reluctance to move (a real BATNA is your current job), and — most underrated — *scarcity of your specific combination*. "Backend depth plus shipped production LLM systems plus eval discipline" is a thin market and you may say so.

**⚠ Trap:** inventing a competing offer. Recruiters at this tier talk to each other, some ask for written verification, and getting caught ends the process instantly and sometimes permanently. There is no version of this that is worth the downside. Vague-but-true is fine; false is disqualifying.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I don't have a signed competing offer and I'm not going to pretend I do. What I do have is a clear read on the market for this specific skill set and a strong current situation, and the number I'd need to move is $X. Here's how I got there."

### The recruiter asks for my current compensation or my expected number in the first call. What exactly do I say?

Have the script memorized, because this is asked in the first eight minutes and improvising costs money.

On **current compensation**: in several US states and jurisdictions asking is illegal, and in all of them it is irrelevant to your value here — especially for you, where a current package benchmarked to one market will drag your offer down if disclosed. The line: *"I'd rather not anchor on my current number — it's benchmarked to a different market and a different role. I'm happy to talk about what I'm targeting for this role, which is what actually matters for whether we're aligned."*

On **expected compensation**, deflect once, then give a range if pressed: *"What's the band for this level? I'd rather calibrate to your range than throw a number at it — if we're in the same universe, the details are easy."* If they insist: *"For this level and scope I'd be targeting $X–$Y total, weighted toward equity if the refresh policy is strong."* Note two things about that: the range's *bottom* is the only number they will hear, so set the bottom at your target, not below it; and the "weighted toward equity" clause signals sophistication and gives them a lever that costs them less than base.

If they refuse to give a range and demand yours first, that is itself information about how the rest of the negotiation will go. Give the range anyway rather than stonewalling into a dead process — but set it high and honest.

**⚠ Trap:** giving a range you would actually accept the bottom of. Every range you state becomes a ceiling-anchored-at-its-floor. If you would accept $380k, say $420–470k, not $380–450k.

**🗣 Say this in the room** (the deflection, verbatim): "Honestly, my number depends on the level and the scope, and we haven't nailed those down yet. What range is the req approved for? If it's in the neighborhood, I'd rather spend our time on whether the work is right."

### Compare these two for me: a big-tech offer of $220k base + $600k RSU over 4 years + 15% bonus, versus a private AI lab at $250k base + $1.8M in units over 4 years, no bonus. Which do you take and show me the work.

Good — this is the question, and the answer is not a preference, it is a model with stated assumptions. I would work it out loud in exactly this order.

**Step 1 — normalize to annual run rate.**
Big-tech: base 220 + bonus 220×0.15 = 33 + equity 600/4 = 150. **Total $403k/yr nominal.**
Lab: base 250 + equity 1800/4 = 450. **Total $700k/yr nominal.**
Headline gap: $297k/yr, or 74%.

**Step 2 — apply the liquidity/outcome discount to the private paper, and state the assumption.** Case A, the lab has run employee tenders in each of the last two years at flat-or-up prices and I have confirmed I would be eligible: discount 35%. Equity becomes 450 × 0.65 = 292.5, total **$542.5k/yr**. Case B, no tender history, double-trigger units, no visible liquidity path: discount 70%. Equity becomes 450 × 0.30 = 135, total **$385k/yr** — now *below* the big-tech offer.

**Step 3 — vesting shape and the tenure assumption.** Both assumed 25% annual here; if the lab's is back-loaded or double-trigger, re-run at a 2-year exit. Under a 2-year exit with double-trigger and no liquidity event, the lab equity realized is **$0** and the comparison is $250k vs (220+33+300) = $553k. That case matters more than people admit, because it is not rare.

**Step 4 — refresh.** Big-tech: assume a 50% refresh, so steady-state equity stays near $150k/yr indefinitely. Lab: ask. Many fast-growing private companies grant infrequently and the year-five picture is thin. If the lab refreshes at 25%, year five is base 250 + roughly 112 = $362k, while big-tech year five is 220 + 33 + ~140 = $393k. **The 74% headline gap can invert entirely by year five.**

**Step 5 — the non-financial terms that are actually worth money.** Concentration risk (my salary and my net worth in the same private bet), tax treatment of the specific instrument (get a CPA — PPU and private-RSU treatment differs and I am not going to pretend to give tax advice), the portability of what I learn, and the level. If the lab role is scoped smaller, I am paying for the logo.

**My decision rule:** take the lab if Case A holds — demonstrated recurring liquidity, a discount I can defend, and scope at least as large. Take the big-tech offer if Case B holds, or if the lab's units are double-trigger with no path, because at that point I am comparing $403k of liquid money against $385k of risk-adjusted paper with worse diversification. And in either case I would go back to the lab with: "I'm modeling the units at a 35% liquidity discount, which is how I'd model any private paper. If you can tell me about the tender cadence, or move base up $30k, that closes the gap." That sentence is respectful, informed, and gives them something concrete to fix.

**⚠ Trap:** the "life-changing money" reasoning error. A large private grant is life-changing only conditional on liquidity, and the correct response to a big conditional number is to price the condition, not to be impressed by the number. I have watched strong engineers take a 70%-discountable package over a liquid one because the headline was bigger, and then sit through three years without a tender.

### What is negotiable besides base? Rank them by how likely I am to get them.

Ordered by expected value, which is (probability they say yes) × (dollar value), not by how big the number sounds.

**1. Sign-on bonus.** Highest hit rate by a wide margin. It is one-time cash that does not disturb internal band equity, so approvers say yes easily. This is where you push when base is stuck. Ask in a specific amount tied to a reason: "a $75k sign-on to offset the unvested equity I'm walking away from" is a request an approver can justify in writing; "more sign-on" is not.

**2. Equity above the midpoint of band.** Moderate hit rate. Companies have more room here than on base, and equity is not cash today. Frame in annualized terms, not grant terms.

**3. Level.** Low hit rate post-offer, enormous value. Only worth pursuing with new evidence (see the earlier answers) — and pursue it *before* the debrief where possible.

**4. Start date.** Very high hit rate, and it is worth real money: a later start lets a competing process finish, and an earlier start can be traded for something else. Also, if your equity vesting commencement date is your start date, a start date just before a grant-approval cycle can matter.

**5. Team and scope, in writing.** High hit rate at companies that do team match, and this is the single largest determinant of whether you get promoted in two years. Under-negotiated by everyone.

**6. Refresh commitment or an off-cycle review.** Low-to-moderate hit rate for a hard commitment, high for a written "we will do an off-cycle equity review at 6 months." Worth asking every time.

**7. Remote allowance, equipment, conference/learning budget, and — the AI-specific one — a personal compute/API budget.** Nearly always yes, individually small, but they cost the company almost nothing and asking signals you know what you need to do the job. A $5k/yr API and GPU-credit budget is a genuine productivity asset in this field.

**8. Relocation and immigration support.** For your situation specifically, immigration terms (who files, how fast, whether they'll do premium processing, whether they support an O-1 or only H-1B) are worth more than most cash line items and are absolutely negotiable. Get commitments in writing, with named timelines.

**💰 Why the ordering is right:** a 20% shot at a $40k base bump has an expected value of $8k. An 80% shot at a $50k sign-on has an expected value of $40k. Spend your negotiating capital where the probability lives. And note that you get roughly *two* substantive asks before the tone changes — so bundle: "If we can do $X sign-on and move the equity to $Y, I'm ready to sign today."

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I want to make this easy to close. Two things and I'm done: the sign-on at $75k to cover what I'm leaving on the table, and a written note that we'll do an off-cycle equity review at six months. Base as-is is fine."
### Run a multi-offer process for me. How do you actually sequence four companies with different loop lengths so the offers land together?

The mental model is scheduling under variable latency with a hard join: you want all the offers to arrive inside the same two-week window, and the loops have wildly different service times. Treating this as "apply to things and see what happens" is how people end up with one exploding offer and no leverage. Treating it as a pipeline you schedule backwards from a join date is how you end up with three offers on the table.

**Step 1 — measure the service times.** Published medians vary and are worth verifying before each cycle, but the durable shape: AI-product companies and startups are fast (roughly 2–3 weeks from first screen to offer — Perplexity has been reported around an 11-day median, Anthropic around 19 days), big-tech is slow (4–8 weeks, sometimes longer with team match and committee cycles), and companies with paid work trials or 8-hour onsite projects (Cursor, OpenAI's work-trial variants) add a scheduling-dependent tail because *you* need a free day. **📅 Volatile:** verify current cycle times — these move with hiring climate.

**Step 2 — start the slowest first.** If big-tech takes 6 weeks and the AI-product company takes 2.5, you start big-tech in week 0 and the startup in week 3.5. Most candidates do the exact opposite because startups reply faster, and then spend three weeks stalling an offer they cannot compare to anything.

**Step 3 — designate a pace-setter and use it honestly.** Pick the company you most want, tell *them* your timeline once you have any real deadline, and tell the others the truth. Recruiters are extremely used to this and will compress a loop by a week or two if you give them a concrete reason.

**Step 4 — batch the practice loops first.** Run one or two loops at companies you would take but do not love, two to three weeks ahead of the target loops. Your fifth system-design round of the month is meaningfully better than your first, and the interview-tax on your evenings is real, especially at 11pm IST.

**Step 5 — build in a decision buffer.** Ask every recruiter, at the *start*, "if this results in an offer, what's the typical decision window?" That single question surfaces the exploding-offer companies before you have sunk four rounds into them.

**⚠ Trap:** running loops serially "so I can focus." Serial loops mean you never have two offers at once, which means you never have leverage, which is worth more than the marginal preparation quality. The exception is if you genuinely cannot sustain parallel loops on top of a job — then run two, not four, but never one.

**🗣 Say this in the room** (to a recruiter at the start of a fast process, when a slower one is running): "I want to be upfront on timing: I have another process that concludes around [date], and I'd like to make a decision with complete information. Is your timeline compatible, or should I be trying to move something?"

### A company gives me a 72-hour exploding deadline while another loop is still running. What do you do?

First, diagnose which kind of deadline it is, because there are three and they behave differently.

**The real budget deadline** — "this req closes at end of quarter" or "we have one headcount and two finalists." Rare but real, and usually the recruiter will say something concrete about *why*. **The process deadline** — "our offers are standardly open for a week," which is a policy, not a physics constraint, and is routinely extended when asked. **The pressure tactic** — a short window with no stated reason, deployed specifically to prevent comparison. The third is the most common at companies with weaker hiring brands, and it is a genuine signal about how they will treat you as an employee.

The response is the same opening move in all three cases: ask, once, plainly, and give a reason that is about *decision quality*, not about shopping the offer. "I'm genuinely excited about this and I intend to say yes or no with conviction rather than under time pressure. I have one process concluding on the 14th. Can we set the decision date to the 16th? If the constraint is real on your side, tell me and I'll work within it." In my experience the extension is granted the large majority of the time, because the recruiter's actual failure mode is a declined offer, not a slow one.

If they refuse and the deadline is genuinely short, you make a decision under uncertainty, which means you need the *other* process to give you information fast. Call the other recruiter and say exactly this: "I have an offer in hand with a decision date of Friday. I'd rather work with you, but I need to know whether you're in a position to make an offer by then. If you can't, I understand completely and I'll make my decision — but I'd rather ask than assume." This is honest, it is specific, and it very frequently compresses a loop. Note what it does *not* do: it does not claim the other offer is better, and it does not threaten.

**⚠ Trap:** accepting an exploding offer and continuing to interview. Reneging on an accepted offer is a real and permanent reputational cost in a small industry where recruiters move between the exact companies on your list. If you accept, stop. If you cannot stop, do not accept.

**🔍 Failure taxonomy of deadline handling.** (1) You never asked about the decision window at kickoff → you discover it at offer stage with no room. Fix: ask in the first call, every time. (2) You asked for an extension without a reason → reads as shopping; often refused. Fix: always attach a concrete date and a decision-quality framing. (3) You asked for a second extension → this is where goodwill actually breaks; one ask is free, two is expensive. (4) You let the deadline pass while "thinking" → offers do occasionally get pulled, and the recruiter's internal credibility takes the hit. Communicate before the date even if the answer is "still deciding."

### Walk me through the actual negotiation conversation. What do I say when they tell me it's their best offer?

Set the frame first: this is not adversarial and it is not a haggle. The recruiter's incentive is to close you, and the thing that unblocks an approver is a *reason they can write down*. Your job is to supply reasons, not pressure.

The sequence I use:

**1. Say yes to the job before you say anything about the number.** "I want to work with this team — the retrieval and eval scope is exactly what I want to own. So I'm coming at this from a place of trying to make it work." This is not a nicety; it changes the recruiter's read of the entire call from "shopping" to "closing."

**2. Make one specific, justified ask, bundled.** "Two things and I'm ready to sign: the equity at $X annualized rather than $Y, and a $70k sign-on. The reason on the equity is [market data / the scope discussed / the unvested equity I'm leaving]. The sign-on is to bridge the year-one gap."

**3. Then stop talking.** Genuinely. The silence after a specific ask is the most productive ten seconds in the process, and filling it is the single most common self-inflicted wound.

**4. When they say "this is our best offer":** treat it as information about *which lever* is exhausted, not about all of them. "I hear you on base — is that a band constraint or an approval constraint? If base is fixed, is there room on the sign-on or the equity?" Nine times out of ten, "best offer" means "best offer on the component we discussed." Also acceptable: "Understood. Then let me ask about the non-cash side — start date, off-cycle review at six months, and the team assignment."

**5. If it is genuinely final, close cleanly.** "That's fine — I appreciate you pushing on it. Let me confirm by [date]." Do not sulk, do not re-litigate, and do not go silent. You may work with this recruiter again.

**⚠ Trap:** negotiating against yourself. You state $450k, they say nothing for two seconds, and you say "but I'm flexible" — you have just conceded before they countered. Say the number, state the reason, and stop.

**⚠ Trap two:** making your ask about your needs. Rent, cost of living, and "I was hoping for" are not reasons an approver can write in a justification form. Market data, scope, competing processes and unvested-equity replacement are.

**🗣 Say this in the room** (the whole ask, verbatim, memorize it): "I want to be direct because I'd like to close this. I'm in. Two adjustments and I'll sign today: equity at $X annualized, and the sign-on at $70k. The equity number comes from the posted range for this level and the scope we discussed; the sign-on covers the grant I'm walking away from. If those work, we're done."

### How do I use a competing offer without lying and without burning the relationship?

The rule is simple and I hold it absolutely: **never state a number you do not have, and never imply a preference you do not hold.** Everything else is fair.

What you may truthfully say: that you have an offer; the company (optional — I usually name it, because naming a credible competitor is itself the signal); the deadline; and, if you choose, the number. What you should generally *not* do is show the offer letter — some companies ask, and you are within your rights to decline, though at a few companies verification is a genuine precondition for a match.

The framing that works is **preference plus gap**, never **auction**. Auction framing ("I have $X, can you beat it?") invites the recruiter to decline, because a company that wins on price alone knows you will leave on price alone. Preference framing gives them a reason to spend approval capital: "I'd rather be here. The other offer is $X and yours is $Y. If you can get within $Z I'll sign today and stop the other process." Note the commitment attached — that is the part that unlocks approvals, because the recruiter can promise a close.

Three specific mechanics. **One:** tell the company you prefer *before* the other offer's deadline, not on the last day, so they have time to run an approval. **Two:** if you say "I'll sign today," you must actually sign today. Doing this twice ends your credibility. **Three:** tell the losing company promptly and warmly, with the real reason. "I took the other one — the scope was closer to what I want to own, and honestly the comp gap was significant. I'd genuinely like to stay in touch." Recruiters remember declines that were handled well, and half the time the company you decline is the one you interview at in two years.

**⚠ Trap:** using a lowball offer from a company you would never join as leverage against your top choice. Sophisticated recruiters ask "who's it with?" and a mismatched comparison ("I have an offer from a 15-person seed startup") destroys the leverage and looks naive. Use comparators the company considers peers.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I'll be transparent because I think it serves both of us: I have an offer from [company] at [$X] with a decision date of the 14th. Your team is my first choice on the work. If you can get to [$Z], I'll accept and withdraw from everything else."

### Let's flip it. What questions do you ask us, and why does the answer to "do you have a golden set and who owns it?" tell you so much?

Reverse interviewing is the highest-leverage seniority signal in the entire loop, because the questions you ask reveal what you have actually operated. A candidate who asks "what's the tech stack?" and "what does a typical day look like?" has told the interviewer they have never owned an AI system in production. A candidate who asks about the golden set has told them the opposite in eleven words.

**"Do you have a golden eval set, and who owns it?"** is my single favorite question, and it is diagnostic on three axes at once.

*Do they have one at all?* If the answer is "we mostly vibe-check in staging" or "the PM tries things," you have learned that quality is unmeasured, which means every model upgrade is a coin flip and every regression is discovered by a customer. That is not automatically disqualifying — it might be the reason they are hiring you — but it changes the job from "improve quality" to "build the ability to know whether quality changed," and it changes your 90-day plan.

*Who owns it?* The best answer is a named person or team with the authority to block a release. The worst answer is "everyone" — a golden set owned by everyone gets edited by everyone, which means yesterday's numbers are not comparable to today's, which means the metric is decorative. Ask the follow-up: "Can a single engineer add cases to it, and is there a review?"

*How does it gate anything?* "Does a regression on the golden set block a deploy, or is it advisory?" An advisory eval is a dashboard nobody reads. A blocking eval is an engineering culture.

**⚠ Trap:** asking the question and not listening to the *texture* of the answer. The tell is specificity. "About 400 cases, roughly half from real production failures, PM-and-eng co-owned, and we block on a 2-point drop in the top-line metric" is a company that ships AI. "Yeah, we have evals" is not an answer, and the polite follow-up ("how many cases, and where did they come from?") is where you find out.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "How do you know when a change made things better? Specifically — is there a golden set, who owns it, how many cases, and does a regression block a release?"

### What do you ask about on-call and about model upgrades, and what do the answers actually tell you?

These two questions map the operational maturity of the org better than anything on the careers page.

**On-call for agents.** "Who gets paged when an agent misbehaves, and what does the page actually say?" The interesting part is the second clause. Traditional on-call pages on availability and error rate; agent systems fail *silently and semantically* — the agent completes successfully and does the wrong thing. So the real question is: **what is your detector for a quality incident?** Good answers: a continuously-running canary eval against production traffic, an automated LLM-judge on a sample of live trajectories with a human review queue, a tool-call error-rate and loop-detection alert, a spend-per-resolved-task alarm. Bad answer: "customers tell us." Follow-ups worth asking: "What's your rollback story for a prompt change? Are prompts versioned and deployable independently of code, and can you roll one back without a deploy?"

**Model upgrades.** "Walk me through what happens when your provider ships a new model version — or deprecates one." This is a fantastic question because it exposes the whole release culture in one answer. The mature answer contains: pinned model versions in config (not floating aliases), a shadow-traffic or replay phase against the golden set, a per-cohort rollout with a quality metric as the gate, an explicit owner for the go/no-go, and a documented deprecation-migration runbook because providers *do* sunset models on their own schedule. The immature answer is "we're on the latest, it's usually fine" — which means somewhere in their system a model change has already silently regressed something and nobody knows.

**💰 Why you care personally, in money:** if the org has no eval gate on model upgrades, you will spend your first two quarters doing forensics on regressions instead of shipping. Consider an assistant handling 50k requests/day where a silent quality drop takes the resolution rate from 68% to 61%. That is 3,500 extra escalations per day; at a $6 fully-loaded cost per human-handled ticket that is $21k/day, or roughly $630k a month, running until someone notices. The question "how would you notice?" is not an academic one.

**🔍 Failure taxonomy you are probing for:** (1) floating model aliases in production config → an upgrade you did not choose; (2) prompts in source code with no independent rollback → a one-line fix requires a deploy; (3) evals that are advisory rather than blocking → measured but not governed; (4) no cost alarm per resolved task → a retry-loop regression shows up in the invoice, 30 days late; (5) no trajectory logging → an incident you cannot reconstruct.

### "Who owns the prompts?" Why is that a seniority-signal question, and what answers are red flags?

Because prompt ownership is the cleanest proxy for whether an organization has figured out that LLM behavior is *product surface with engineering consequences*, and the answer sorts companies into three types instantly.

**Type one: nobody owns them.** Prompts live inline in a dozen Python files, edited by whoever is touching the feature, with no versioning, no eval attached, and no review. Every change is unmeasured. You will find the same instruction repeated in four places with slight divergence, and a fix applied to one of them. This is extremely common and it is the source of the "we changed one word and quality dropped" incident.

**Type two: the PM owns them, with no engineering gate.** Better than nothing, worse than it looks. Prompts get edited in a UI, shipped without eval, and there is no rollback story. The tell is if you ask "how do you know the edit helped?" and the answer is a story rather than a number.

**Type three: prompts are versioned artifacts with owners, evals and a rollout mechanism.** They live in a registry or in source with semantic versioning, changes require an eval run, there is a canary, and rollback does not require a deploy. Product and engineering co-own the content, engineering owns the mechanism.

The follow-up questions that separate a rehearsed answer from a real one: "If I change a system prompt today, what has to happen before it hits production, and how would I roll it back at 2am?" and "Is the same prompt used by more than one code path, and how do you know?"

**⚠ Trap:** hearing "we use LangSmith / a prompt-management tool" and concluding they are type three. Tooling is not governance. The governing question is whether a prompt change is *gated on a number*. A team with a great prompt-management UI and no blocking eval is type two with better ergonomics.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Who owns prompt content, and is a prompt change gated on an eval run? I ask because in the systems I've run, the prompt is the highest-blast-radius, lowest-friction change in the codebase, and how a team governs that tells me a lot about what my first quarter looks like."

### Ask me how we handled a bad model release. Why is that your question, and how do you read the answer?

I ask it because incident stories are the least-rehearsed part of any interviewer's repertoire and therefore the most honest. Careers pages describe aspiration; incidents describe reality. And in this field there has definitely been at least one — a provider upgrade that changed formatting behavior, a fine-tune that regressed on a subclass, an agent that started looping, a retrieval index that went stale. If the interviewer says "we haven't had one," either they are new, they are not measuring, or they are managing me.

What I listen for, in order:

**Detection.** How long between the regression starting and someone knowing? Was it a metric, an alert, or a customer? "Our canary eval caught it in 40 minutes" is a completely different organization from "a support ticket escalated on day four."

**Attribution.** Could they tell *why*? This requires trajectory logging, versioned prompts and pinned model versions. Teams without those spend days bisecting.

**The fix and the follow-ups.** Did they roll back, and how fast? And crucially, what *systemic* change came out of it? "We added the failing cases to the golden set and made it blocking" is a learning organization. "We told everyone to be more careful" is not.

**Blame texture.** Listen to whether the story is told with names attached in a negative way. That tells you what happens when *you* cause the incident.

**Honesty.** An interviewer who tells you a story where their team looked bad is giving you a real signal about the culture — and, incidentally, it is what makes me want to work there.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Tell me about the last time a model change made something worse in production. I'm interested in how long it took to notice and what changed afterwards — not the mistake itself, everyone has those."

### How do you ask "what happened to the person who had this role before me?" without sounding paranoid?

Ask it as a scope question rather than an attrition question, and it lands as diligence instead of suspicion.

The phrasing I use: **"Is this a backfill or a new headcount? If it's a backfill, what did that person move on to — and what would you want done differently this time?"** That formulation does three things. It is a legitimate question with an operational purpose. It gives the manager an easy, non-defensive path to answer. And the "differently this time" clause is where the real information lives — a manager who has a crisp answer has thought about what went wrong, and a manager who has none may be re-hiring the same failure.

Answers that should worry you, ranked. **The vague deflection** ("they moved on to another opportunity," full stop, no elaboration) after a direct question is the loudest signal in the whole loop; ask one gentle follow-up ("were they on the team long?") and note the answer. **Short tenure with an unclear reason** — under a year, with no internal transfer, is worth understanding. **Multiple predecessors** in two years is a role problem, not a person problem, and the role problem is usually unclear ownership or an impossible mandate. **"They got frustrated with how slowly we ship"** is at least honest and tells you exactly what you are signing up for.

Complementary questions in the same family, all of which read as senior rather than suspicious: "How long have the current team members been here?", "Who was the last person promoted on this team, and what did they do?" (this one is exceptionally good — it tells you the actual promotion criteria, not the written ones), and "What does the manager's own tenure look like?" Manager churn is the underrated risk: your level, your scope and any verbal commitment you were given all evaporate in a reorg.

**⚠ Trap:** asking this of the recruiter instead of the hiring manager or a peer engineer. Recruiters give the sanctioned answer. Peers on the team, especially in a casual "meet the team" slot, give the real one — and asking a future teammate "what's the thing you'd want to know if you were joining today?" is the highest-yield question in the entire process.

### What do you ask to find out how a company decides what to build? Why does that matter more in AI than elsewhere?

It matters more because AI features have an unusually wide gap between *demoable* and *shippable*, and organizations that decide what to build by demo enthusiasm will burn you. A backend feature that demos works; an agent that demos might be at 60% on the real distribution. So the question underneath is: **does this company convert enthusiasm into a measurable claim before committing engineering quarters?**

The questions, and what each reveals:

**"Walk me through how the last AI feature you shipped got prioritized."** Listen for whether there was a claimed outcome metric before the work started. "The CEO saw a demo" is a real answer people give.

**"How do you decide something isn't going to work, and who's allowed to say it?"** The most senior-sounding question you can ask, and the answer is usually revealing. Companies with a kill mechanism ship better than companies without one. If the answer is "we haven't killed anything," they either have extraordinary judgment or they are carrying zombie projects.

**"When you scoped it, did you set a quality bar up front, and did you hit it?"** Asking whether the bar was set *in advance* separates real eval culture from post-hoc rationalization, because a bar set after the numbers arrive is not a bar.

**"How much of your engineering time goes to eval and data work versus feature work?"** If the answer is under 15%, either they have solved something hard or they are not measuring. Both are worth knowing.

**"What's the process for a feature that's working at 80% and needs 95%?"** This is the actual job in applied AI, and the answer tells you whether they understand that the last 15 points is where the quarters go.

**⚠ Trap:** treating this as a culture-fit chat. These are diligence questions and you should take notes visibly. The notes signal you are evaluating them, which — at senior level — is exactly the right posture and consistently improves how the offer conversation goes afterwards.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "Two questions on how work gets chosen: how did the last AI feature you shipped get prioritized, and what's the most recent thing you decided *not* to build after starting it? I've found the second question tells me more about an engineering culture than almost anything else."

### Give me the failure taxonomy for a senior-level negotiation. What actually goes wrong?

Written as a decision procedure, because these are all recoverable if you spot them early and mostly fatal if you do not.

**1. Anchored low by your own history.** You disclosed your current comp, or gave a range whose floor you would accept, and the offer arrived at your floor. *Detect:* the offer matches your stated minimum suspiciously exactly. *Recover:* you generally cannot re-anchor upward without new information — so bring new information, i.e. another process. *Prevent:* deflect current comp; state ranges whose floor is your target.

**2. Leveled below your scope and you noticed at offer stage.** *Detect:* the level maps to a band whose ceiling is under your target, so the recruiter keeps saying "that's the top of the range." *Recover:* the ask is now a level review, which needs an advocate — go to the hiring manager, not the recruiter, with scope evidence. *Prevent:* the mid-loop correction from earlier in this section.

**3. Negotiated one component and accepted the rest by silence.** *Detect:* you got a base bump and never discussed refresh, vesting shape or team. *Recover:* raise them before signing; they are still open. *Prevent:* make your asks as a bundle, once.

**4. No BATNA, and it showed.** *Detect:* you find yourself explaining why you need the job. *Recover:* limited. *Prevent:* always run at least two processes.

**5. Over-negotiated into resentment.** *Detect:* three separate rounds of asks, response times lengthening, warmth dropping. *Recover:* close immediately and graciously; the money is not worth arriving with a reputation. *Prevent:* one bundled ask, one follow-up, then decide.

**6. Won the number, lost the job.** You negotiated hard on comp and never negotiated team, scope or manager — and joined a team that does prompt maintenance on a product in maintenance mode. *This is the most expensive failure on the list* and the least discussed, because the cost shows up two years later as a career gap rather than a smaller number. *Prevent:* negotiate scope with the same rigor as cash, and get it in writing.

**7. Reneged after accepting.** Permanent, industry-wide, and the AI world is small. *Prevent:* do not accept until you are done.

**💰 Rough magnitudes, so you spend your energy proportionally.** A hard negotiation on cash typically moves a package 5–15% — on a $400k offer, $20k–$60k a year. Getting the level right moves it 20–35% *and* compounds into your next offer — call it $80k–$140k a year plus the option value. Getting the team right determines whether you are promotable in 24 months, which is another level, i.e. the same again. The ordering of effort should be **team > level > cash**, and almost everyone does the reverse.
### I've got an offer and now there's a "team match" process. How does that work and how do I approach it?

The mental model: at companies that hire to the company rather than to a req — OpenAI is the best-known example, and Google's post-offer team allocation has a similar flavor — the offer establishes that you are hireable and at what level, and a second, mostly-unscored process determines what you actually do for the next two years. Candidates treat the offer as the finish line and team match as paperwork. That is exactly backwards: the offer decides your comp for four years, the team decides your comp for the decade, because the team decides what evidence you accumulate.

Mechanically it usually looks like: the recruiter shares a set of teams with openings; you take 30-minute conversations with several managers; both sides express preference; an allocation happens. Sometimes you have real choice, sometimes there are two teams and one of them is the one that needs a body. **📅 Volatile:** the specifics of any company's team-match mechanics change with headcount pressure — ask your recruiter how it works at the time, including whether you can decline a match and how long you have.

How to approach it: treat each conversation as *you* interviewing *them*, because that is literally what it is — you already have the offer, and the manager is now selling. Ask the reverse-interview questions from earlier in this section and take the answers seriously. Ask specifically: "What would I own in the first six months, and who else could do it if I said no?" — the second clause is the load-bearing one. If three other people could do it, you are fungible; if nobody could, you have leverage and scope.

Also ask what the team's relationship is to the model. In an AI org there is a meaningful gradient from *teams that consume the model* (product surfaces, integrations) through *teams that shape it* (post-training, evals, tooling, harness) to *teams that build it*. None is inherently better, but they build different careers, and you should know which one you are choosing rather than discovering it in month four.

**⚠ Trap:** letting urgency drive the match. Recruiters may pressure you to close the match quickly because a manager has headcount expiring. A rushed match is the single most common source of "I joined a great company and hated my job." Ask for the time; two extra weeks on a team decision is cheap.

**🗣 Say this in the room** (to a matching manager): "Since I've got the offer, let me use this differently — I want to understand what I'd own, how you'd know in six months whether hiring me worked, and what the hardest unsolved thing on your roadmap is. If those line up, I'd rather be here than anywhere."

### How do I tell a team that will level me up from one that just needs a body?

There are five diagnostics and they are all askable in a thirty-minute call.

**1. Is the work on the critical path of something the company cares about?** Ask: "If this team's roadmap slipped a quarter, who notices?" A team whose slippage nobody notices will not generate promotion evidence no matter how well you execute. This is the most important of the five.

**2. Is there unowned scope?** Ask: "What's something important that nobody currently owns?" A team with unowned scope is a level-up team, because scope is the currency of leveling and you can simply take it. A team where everything is owned by someone tenured means you will be assigned tasks.

**3. Who was the last person promoted here, and for what?** If the answer is fluent and specific, promotion is a live mechanism. If the manager has to think hard, or the answer is "we've had some reorgs," you are looking at a team where advancement happens by leaving.

**4. Does the manager have credibility upward?** Harder to ask directly, so ask indirectly: "How do decisions about this team's roadmap get made, and what's the last thing you successfully pushed back on?" A manager who cannot describe a successful pushback cannot protect your scope either.

**5. Is the team building or maintaining?** Both are honorable, but maintenance teams generate maintenance evidence. Ask: "What percentage of the team's time is new work versus keeping existing things running?" In AI orgs specifically, ask the sharper version: "Is this team shipping new capability, or absorbing the cost of capability someone else shipped?"

The composite red flag: a team that is off the critical path, fully owned, with no recent promotion, a manager who cannot describe a pushback, and 70% maintenance. That team needs a body. The composite green flag: critical path, obvious unowned scope, a recent promotion the manager describes in detail, and a hard unsolved problem they will name.

**⚠ Trap:** optimizing for the most technically interesting problem regardless of organizational position. The most interesting problem on a team nobody depends on produces a promotion packet with no impact section. I would take the second-most-interesting problem on a critical-path team every time, and I have watched the opposite choice cost people two years.

**📐 Numbers you must know:** the promotion clock. At most large companies a level takes 18–30 months of *demonstrated* scope at the next level, and packets are typically assembled on a semi-annual or annual cycle. That means the work you do in months 1–9 is the work that shows up in your first real packet. Choosing a team where months 1–9 produce no scope evidence costs you a full cycle, which at a $70k inter-level delta is $70k–$105k of direct comp before equity effects.

### Databricks reportedly requires two to three references from recent managers before extending an offer. How do I prepare for a reference process like that?

Treat references as a stage of the loop, not a formality, because at companies that run them pre-offer they genuinely change outcomes — a lukewarm reference can downgrade a level, and a bad one can end a process that was otherwise finished.

**Preparation starts months before you interview, not when they ask.** Maintain three or four people who have managed you or worked adjacently to you, who you have talked to in the last year for reasons unrelated to needing something. This is the unglamorous part and it is the whole game.

**When the request comes, brief your references properly.** Do not just send the recruiter their emails. Call each one and give them: the company and role, the level you are being considered for, the two or three themes you want reinforced (map them to the scope-evidence slots — end-to-end ownership, direction-setting, an incident, mentorship), and one specific story per theme that they were present for. This is not coaching them to lie; it is helping a busy person recall the relevant thirty minutes out of two years. A reference who says "he was great, very solid" is a *weak* reference in a calibrated process — specificity is what converts.

**Handle the "recent manager" constraint carefully.** If you are currently employed and your current manager does not know you are looking, say so plainly and early: "I can provide two prior managers and a current skip-level peer; my current manager isn't aware I'm interviewing and I'd want to keep it that way until there's an offer. Is that workable?" It nearly always is, and companies that require a current manager pre-offer will say so — at which point you decide whether the process is worth the disclosure risk.

**Anticipate the questions they will be asked.** Typical reference calls cover: scope and level ("what would you say his level is here?"), a weakness, how they handled conflict, and "would you hire them again." The weakness question is the one that hurts an unbriefed reference, because a well-meaning person improvises. Give them one: a real, bounded, growth-shaped weakness that you have visibly worked on.

**⚠ Trap:** giving a reference who liked you but never saw your scope. A friendly peer who cannot speak to ownership will produce a warm, level-neutral reference — which in a leveling debrief reads as an absence of evidence for the higher level. Pick references by *what they witnessed*, not by how much they like you.

**🗣 Say this to a reference:** "They'll ask about level. I'm being considered as a staff-equivalent, and the specific things that would help are the retrieval migration I ran end to end and the way the index-alias pattern got picked up by the platform team. If you're comfortable saying that, it's exactly what they're calibrating on."

### Someone at the company back-channels a former colleague of mine without telling me. How do I manage that surface?

Assume it is happening. In a field this small — where the applied-AI population at your target companies is a few thousand people and everyone has worked with everyone — an informal "hey, do you know X?" message is routine, unregulated, and often more influential than the formal reference process. Your former colleagues, not your listed references, are your real reference surface.

Three things follow.

**One: manage the exit, always.** How you left your last two jobs is the single most-queried data point in a back-channel. Notice periods honored, handovers documented, no scorched earth on the way out — this is not sentimentality, it is asset management.

**Two: map your surface before you interview.** For each target company, spend fifteen minutes on LinkedIn identifying who works there that you have worked with. If someone is there who you would rather not be asked about, you want to know that in week zero, not week four. If someone great is there, reach out proactively — a friendly ping ("I'm interviewing at your company, would love to catch up") converts a random back-channel into a warm one, and frequently into an internal referral note that carries real weight.

**Three: pre-empt anything genuinely bad.** If there is a known rough episode in your history — a conflict, a project that failed publicly, a short stint — do not let a back-channel be the first time the hiring manager hears about it. Introduce it yourself in the loop, briefly and without defensiveness, framed as what you learned. A story told first by you is context; the same story told first by a third party is a discovery.

**⚠ Trap:** assuming the back-channel goes to someone senior. It very often goes to a mid-level engineer who overlapped with you for six months on one project, whose entire impression is formed from that project. Which means your reputation is far more distributed than your reference list implies, and the only durable strategy is behaving consistently rather than curating.

### Present me your 90-day plan. What goes in it, and why do you call it a closing argument?

It is a closing argument because a 90-day plan is the only artifact in the process that shows the hiring manager what having you is like, rather than what hiring you would be *worth*. It converts an abstract "strong candidate" into a concrete "this is what happens in January." I have seen a good one move a level and I have seen one turn a maybe into a same-day offer. It costs two hours.

Structure — one page, three phases, and it must contain something specific to *their* system that you could only know by having listened.

**Days 1–30, understand and instrument.** Read the code paths for the top three AI features; sit with support or the on-call rotation and read fifty real failure transcripts; inventory what is measured today. Deliverable: a written map of where quality is currently unknown. This phase says "I will not redesign your system in week two."

**Days 31–60, establish the measurement.** Build or extend the golden set from real production failures — a specific number, e.g. 200 cases sampled from real traffic and labelled with the team — and wire it into CI as a reported (not yet blocking) gate. Deliverable: a dashboard with a baseline number that did not exist before. This is the highest-value first-quarter contribution an AI engineer can make at most companies, and it is one nobody assigns you.

**Days 61–90, ship one thing that moves that number, and make the gate blocking.** A scoped improvement — a reranker, constrained decoding on the extraction path, prompt-caching on the fixed system preamble — with before-and-after numbers on the golden set and on cost. Deliverable: a shipped change with a measured delta, plus a proposal for the eval gate policy.

Add a fourth section titled **"What I would not do in the first 90 days"** — do not migrate the framework, do not fine-tune, do not rewrite retrieval. This section is the one that gets remarked on in the debrief, because restraint is the rarest signal in AI engineering and it directly contradicts the reflex the interviewer has seen in the last ten candidates.

**💰 Put one number in it.** "If prefix caching lands on the fixed 8k-token system preamble at 200k calls/day and the provider's cached-input rate is roughly a tenth of the uncached rate, the input cost on that segment falls from 8,000 × 200,000 = 1.6B tokens/day — at $3/Mtok that is $4,800/day — to roughly $480/day, about $130k/month, minus whatever fraction of calls miss the cache." Even if your assumptions are off, the *habit* of quantifying is what scores. **📅 Volatile:** cached-input discounts and per-token prices move constantly — state your source and the date.

**⚠ Trap:** a 90-day plan full of things you would build. It reads as arrogance and as someone who has not understood the system. The plan should be roughly 40% learning, 40% measurement, 20% shipping. The ratio itself is the argument.

**🗣 Say this in the room:** "I put together a one-page 90-day plan based on what I learned in the loop — mostly it's about establishing what 'better' means before I change anything, because from what you described, the golden set is the missing piece. Want me to send it, or walk you through it now?"

### Does the 90-day plan change depending on whether it's an AI product company, a big-tech applied org, or a forward-deployed role?

Substantially, and sending the wrong one is worse than sending none, because it advertises that you did not understand the job.

**AI product company (Cursor, Perplexity, Notion, Figma, Sierra, Harvey, Glean).** Velocity is the currency and the eval is usually the gap. Compress phase one to two weeks. Make the headline commitment a *shipped user-visible improvement inside 90 days with a measured quality and latency delta*, with the golden set built along the way rather than as a separate project. Include a line about the fastest path to touching real user traffic. What lands here: "in month one I want to ship something small to production so I understand the whole path, then build the measurement, then ship something that matters."

**Big-tech applied AI (Meta, Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Databricks, Snowflake, Stripe).** Scope here is cross-org, the systems are large, and nothing ships in 90 days without alignment. Weight the plan toward *stakeholder mapping and a written technical direction document*: who owns adjacent systems, where the duplicated effort is, what the org's shared eval infrastructure looks like. Deliverable at day 90 is often a design doc with sign-off from two adjacent teams plus a prototype, not a launch. Adding a launch promise here reads as naive about the environment.

**Forward-deployed (OpenAI FDE, Anthropic Applied, Palantir, Scale, Sierra, Harvey deployments).** The plan is customer-shaped, not system-shaped. Days 1–30: onsite (or in the customer's Slack), map the actual current-state process, get 50 labelled real examples. Days 31–60: build a bespoke eval from the customer's own cases and ship a demonstrable slice against the largest unknown. Days 61–90: pilot success criteria signed, a runbook, and a named metric the buyer accepts. The Palantir-derived ethos — something useful in the customer's hands inside 48 hours — belongs in this version explicitly.

**AI infrastructure.** Weight toward benchmarking the existing serving stack before changing it: current tokens/sec at SLO, batch-size distribution, prefix-cache hit rate, GPU utilization and where the memory actually goes. A day-90 deliverable of "a reproducible benchmark harness plus one throughput win with the number" is the right shape.

**⚠ Trap:** the generic plan. If your 90-day plan does not name their product, their model surface, or a failure mode you learned about *in the loop*, it is a template and it will be read as one. One concrete detail from something an interviewer told you is worth more than three paragraphs of methodology.

### What's your ongoing currency routine? What do you re-verify before every loop, and how do you keep up without drowning?

Two separate routines with different cadences, and conflating them is why people either go stale or spend all weekend reading arXiv.

**The pre-loop verification pass (2–3 hours, done in the week before any loop).** This is the volatile layer — everything that would make you sound like a 2024 engineer if it were stale — and it is exactly the contents of §5, which exists as a single refreshable sink for precisely this reason. Re-read §5 and re-verify every date-stamped item in it against primary sources. Concretely: current frontier model lineups and their positioning; per-token prices for input, cached input, output and reasoning tokens on the providers you will be asked about; context limits, and the gap between advertised and usefully-usable context; caching semantics and discounts; batch/flex tier pricing; structured-output and tool-calling API surfaces for the providers this company uses; open-weight family status and license terms; serving-engine feature matrices if it is an infra loop; MCP spec revision; and any regulatory dates that come up in enterprise conversations. Read the target company's own engineering blog and any model or product launch from the last quarter — being asked "have you used our product?" and having a real answer is a disproportionate scoring event at AI-product companies. **📅 Volatile:** every single item in that list; that is the point of the pass.

**The continuous triage routine (about 3 hours a week, and no more).** The failure mode is treating arXiv as a feed to be consumed. Treat it as a queue with a strict service policy. Weekly: skim titles from a small set of sources — the lab engineering blogs (they carry more shipped-systems signal than most papers), one or two curated newsletters or aggregators, and the release notes of the two or three OSS projects you actually run (vLLM, SGLang, TRL, LangGraph, Outlines — whichever are in your stack). For papers: read the abstract and the figures, and make a three-way call — *ignore*, *file*, or *read properly*. Read properly at most one per week and write five lines in a paper journal: problem, key idea, what it replaced, the evidence, the limitation. Fifty entries a year is a genuine competitive advantage and it takes twenty minutes a week.

**⚠ Trap:** breadth reading as procrastination. Skimming forty abstracts feels productive and produces nothing you can defend under a follow-up. One paper you can criticize beats forty you can name — and interviewers probe exactly there. Depth on three, honest "I've only skimmed that" on everything else.

**🗣 Say this in the room** (when asked something version-dependent you are unsure of): "I'd want to check the current pricing page rather than quote you a number that's a month old — the input-caching discounts in particular have moved twice this year. The mechanism I'd rely on is [X], and the arithmetic works like this regardless of the exact rate." That answer scores *higher* than a confidently wrong figure, and it is true.

### Give me a 12-week plan that produces one public artifact alongside interview prep.

The premise: prep alone is a depreciating asset and an artifact is an appreciating one. One public artifact does three jobs — it gets you scheduled, it becomes the thing you talk about in three separate rounds, and it is leveling evidence a recruiter can verify in thirty seconds. The constraint is that it must not consume the prep. So: four hours a week on the artifact, everything else on the curriculum.

**Weeks 1–2 — choose and scope.** Pick one of four shapes, in descending order of how well they convert: (a) a **public eval harness** for a task class nobody has measured well — the strongest option, because eval discipline is exactly the differentiator in applied AI hiring and almost nobody has a public one; (b) a **from-scratch reimplementation with benchmarks** — attention, a KV-cache-aware batching loop, speculative decoding — where the numbers, not the code, are the artifact; (c) a **merged OSS PR** to vLLM, SGLang, TRL, LangGraph or Outlines, starting from a good-first-issue and a bug you actually hit; (d) a **written post-mortem** of a real system you ran, with numbers. Scope it so it is finishable in 40 hours. The most common failure is picking a project that is 200 hours.

**Weeks 3–6 — build the core.** No README, no polish. Get the thing working and get a number out of it. Set a hard checkpoint at week 6: if there is no number by then, cut scope by half rather than extending.

**Weeks 7–8 — measure properly.** This is the part that separates an artifact from a demo: a real baseline, a stated methodology, error bars or at least a variance note, and a table. "I compared X to Y on N cases, here is the table, here is what surprised me, here is what I would run next" is the entire value proposition.

**Weeks 9–10 — write it up.** README as design doc: problem, approach, results table, reproduction instructions that actually work on a clean machine, and an explicit limitations section. The limitations section is what makes senior readers trust the rest.

**Weeks 11–12 — publish and use it.** Post it, pin the repo, put one line on the resume with the headline number, and — importantly — rehearse the 90-second version out loud, because you will be asked about it in every loop and a rambling answer wastes the whole asset.

**🏋 Drill:** at week 12, give the artifact talk to a timer — 90 seconds covering problem, what you built, the number, the limitation, and what you would do next. Pass criterion: a listener who has never seen it can state your headline result and one limitation afterwards. If they cannot, the artifact is not yet an asset.

**⚠ Trap:** building a chatbot demo. The market is saturated with them and they demonstrate nothing that distinguishes you from a weekend hobbyist. The differentiators are *measurement*, *reproduction* and *contribution* — an eval harness, a benchmark table, a merged PR. A repo with a results table and honest limitations beats a slick demo in every loop I have seen.

### I've signed. My current employer counteroffers with a 40% raise. Walk me through how you think about it.

Start by naming what the counteroffer actually is: evidence that you were underpaid, produced only by the threat of your departure. That framing is not cynical, it is diagnostic — if the money was available and justified, it was available and justified last quarter. The reason it appears now is that replacing you costs more than retaining you *this month*, and that calculus changes the moment the vacancy risk clears.

Work the numbers anyway, because sometimes they matter. **💰 Suppose** your current package is $200k, the counteroffer is $280k, and the new role is $400k with better equity. The counteroffer closes 40% of a $200k gap — and it does nothing about the two things you were actually leaving for. If you were leaving purely on money, and the counteroffer had closed 90% of the gap, it would be worth a hard think. At 40%, the arithmetic does not even reach the threshold where the non-financial questions matter.

Then ask the non-financial questions, which are the real ones. Why were you leaving? If the answer was "the work is not moving me toward the career I want" — which for a backend engineer moving into AI is almost always the case — then the counteroffer does not address a single thing on the list. Money was the tiebreaker, not the reason. And the structural point: the new role puts you in a market where your next five years of comp are set by AI-engineering scope evidence, which your current role is not generating. A raise that keeps you in a lane that is not compounding is expensive in exactly the way that is hard to see.

There are two honest cases for accepting a counteroffer: your reasons for leaving were genuinely and only compensation, and the counter fully closes it; or the counter comes bundled with a *scope* change you would have taken anyway, in writing, with a named project and a named date. Both are rare. In every other case I would decline, warmly, and go.

**⚠ Trap:** reneging on the signed offer to take the counter. This is the version that ends relationships permanently. If you are even slightly likely to accept a counteroffer, resign *before* you sign anything, take the counter or don't, and then proceed. Once you have accepted, you are done — and in an industry where the same forty recruiters staff your entire target list, that reputation is a real asset with a real price.

**🗣 Say this to your current manager:** "I appreciate this genuinely, and it's a real number. But I'm not leaving over comp — I'm leaving because the work I want to be doing in three years isn't on this team's roadmap, and that's not something a raise changes. I'd like to leave well: here's my handover plan, and I'd like to stay in touch."

Then honor the notice period, document the handover properly, and leave the door open — because the reference surface described earlier in this section is the asset you will be drawing on in three years, and it is built entirely out of exits.
