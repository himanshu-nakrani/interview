# AI Engineer (Generative AI) — Interview Preparation Curriculum

This guide is written for one reader: a senior Python backend engineer with genuine depth in CPython internals, asyncio, FastAPI, SQLAlchemy, Redis, Celery/Kafka, distributed systems, and observability — who is now targeting AI Engineer, Applied AI Engineer, GenAI Engineer, LLM Engineer, Forward-Deployed AI Engineer, and AI Infrastructure Engineer roles at the highest-paying employers in the world. It targets the bar where an interviewer says "hire" without a debrief argument: someone who can implement multi-head attention unaided at a whiteboard, compute a KV cache budget in their head, explain why PagedAttention exists in terms of memory fragmentation, design an agent under an explicit token budget with the arithmetic shown, and — above all — open every design answer with how they would know whether it works.

It is a companion to, not a replacement for, the existing 1,633-question Python backend guide. Where that guide teaches the substrate (event loops, ORMs, queues, containers), this one teaches the delta: the foundation-model layer bolted on top, and the five rounds a backend engineer actually loses on — transformer internals, post-training, serving internals, evaluation, and research literacy. It explicitly tells you what to skip because you already ship it. Total scale: **19 parts, 87 sections, 3,046 questions, roughly 900,000–1,000,000 words** at the multi-paragraph prose depth of the Python guide — about 1.9× its question count and roughly 4× its length.

## How this guide is built

**The answer format contract.** Every one of the 3,046 answers follows the same five-beat structure, because that structure is also how you should answer out loud in a room:

1. **Mental model first** — the one-paragraph intuition that makes the mechanism inevitable. ("The KV cache is not an optimization, it is the reason decode is memory-bandwidth-bound rather than compute-bound.")
2. **Mechanism** — what actually happens, at the level of tensors, bytes, packets, or scheduler decisions. Correct modern APIs, correct shapes, correct units.
3. **Runnable code where code helps** — real, current APIs. From-scratch implementations are ~40 lines and writable from memory; production snippets are copy-paste correct.
4. **The trap** — the specific misconception, the silent failure, the thing that passes review and breaks in production.
5. **The production consequence** — what this costs in milliseconds, dollars, or incidents, with the arithmetic shown.

**Recurring devices, used consistently so they become findable:**

- **⚠ Trap** — the named misconception or silent-failure mode. Every section carries at least three.
- **🗣 Say this in the room** — a one-to-three-sentence answer you can deliver verbatim. This is the difference between knowing something and being credited for it.
- **📐 Numbers you must know** — memorizable constants with their date stamp and their derivation, never a bare figure.
- **📄 Paper it came from** — author, year, one-line contribution, and what it replaced. Enough to cite correctly and not more.
- **💰 Worked cost/latency math** — house style: *every* claim touching cost or latency states a number and shows the arithmetic. "We'd cache to reduce cost" is graded as a failed answer.
- **🔍 Failure taxonomy** — every architecture section ends with "how this breaks in production," written as a decision procedure, not anecdotes.
- **🏋 Drill** — timed, unaided exercises with pass criteria. Anthropic, DeepMind, xAI and HRT ban AI tools in live rounds; the drills assume no autocomplete.
- **🧪 Verify before you skip** — attached to every SKIP item in Section 2. Three questions you must answer correctly before exercising the skip.
- **📅 Volatile — see §5** — inline flag on any model name, price, context limit, engine feature, spec revision, or regulatory date. All of them live in one refreshable section.
- **↔ Mechanism, then framework** — a formatting contract, not an aspiration. Every concept is taught twice: once from scratch in ~40 lines, once through the dominant framework, adjacently. This passes both the mechanism interview and the framework-trivia screen.
- **🎯 Targeted** — sections or subsections relevant only to specific employer archetypes, so they can be skipped honestly rather than skimmed guiltily.

## What is deliberately excluded, and why

- **Research Engineer depth on pretraining and optimization theory.** Optimizer derivations, training-curriculum design, novel-architecture research and ablation programs at frontier scale are capped at "enough to build the data and verifier pipeline and hold a credible conversation." A candidate targeting a frontier pretraining-research role needs a separate curriculum beyond §18, §22 and §32. This is stated in §1 rather than buried.
- **Deep classical-ML and causal-inference coverage.** Boosting internals, survival analysis, causal inference, and pre-transformer computer vision appear only through the "when not to use an LLM" gate (§9) and the metrics that eval rounds reuse. A pure ML Engineer loop is a different guide.
- **Framework API trivia as an end in itself.** LangChain/LlamaIndex/CrewAI surface area is taught only adjacent to the from-scratch mechanism (the ↔ contract). This is a deliberate risk against mid-tier enterprise screens that filter on framework names; the mitigation is that every framework concept appears next to its 40-line implementation, so you can speak either language.
- **Frozen tables of models, prices, context limits and regulatory dates.** These live in exactly one place (§5), date-stamped, with inline 📅 pointers everywhere else. The guide teaches the selection framework and the arithmetic; it explicitly does not pretend the numbers are stable, and it instructs a refresh before every loop.
- **Deep RL for control, game-playing and simulation.** Covered only where it touches verifier engineering and agent training (§22). Robotics gets one honest section (§70) sized to its actual interview weight, not its discourse volume.
- **Nvidia kernel-engineer depth.** CUDA and Triton are covered to the point of writing a fused softmax and explaining FlashAttention as a memory-hierarchy argument (§27, ~25 questions of it). A dedicated kernel role needs CUTLASS/CuTe depth this guide does not attempt, and §27 says so.
- **Exhaustive vendor comparison matrices.** Vector databases, agent frameworks, observability tools and serving engines are covered by *selection axes* and the two or three that define the design space, not by a feature grid that expires in a quarter.
- **Generic interview advice.** No "tell me about a conflict," no résumé-format debates beyond what is AI-specific in §3, no LeetCode grind plan. DSA is capped at 46 shared questions in §75 and flagged 🎯 skippable if your target list excludes Perplexity, xAI, quant and big-tech applied — because roughly 70% of senior AI-engineering loops contain no algorithmic coding at all.
